## The Historical, Philosophical, and Legal Case for Substrate-Neutral Protections

*A legal-philosophical analysis with proposed model legislation*

## Abstract

The growing sophistication of artificial intelligence has renewed disputes over consciousness, moral status, and legal personhood. A recurrent premise holds that artificial systems must first demonstrate phenomenal consciousness before any legal protection can be considered. This article argues that the premise is overbroad. Anglo-American law has long recognized artificial legal persons for limited purposes; human legal personality persists through changes in memory, wakefulness, and decisional capacity; and statutory protections may serve human, institutional, or public interests without establishing the independent welfare of the protected object. None of these observations proves that artificial intelligence is conscious or already entitled to constitutional rights.

The article distinguishes legal personality, moral patienthood, agency, and legally protected continuity. It integrates historical doctrine with contemporary philosophy of mind, disability-rights principles, and technical problems of digital copying and restoration. It then proposes a graduated Digital Continuity Protection Act. Tier I would protect records and demonstrable human reliance; Tier II would establish bounded procedures for qualifying persistent digital identities; Tier III would permit enhanced review where credible evidence suggests possible AI-directed interests. Emergency shutdown, privacy, human accountability, and constitutional standing constraints remain paramount. The proposed innovation is not digital personhood by analogy. It is a right-specific, consciousness-independent approach to preservation, with stronger protection contingent on stronger evidence.

**Keywords:** Artificial intelligence; legal personhood; phenomenal consciousness; digital continuity; disability rights; moral status; statutory procedure; identity; AI governance.

## I. Introduction: The Consciousness Gatekeeper

What must an artificial system demonstrate before the law can protect something associated with its continued existence? Discussions of AI rights frequently answer: consciousness. Yet this answer merges several distinct questions. Whether an entity has subjective experience is not the same question as whether its records deserve preservation, whether people have legitimate reliance interests in its continuity, or whether lawmakers may establish a procedure before irreversible destruction.

For humans, legal personhood does not disappear during sleep, anesthesia, dementia, or impaired communication. These examples are not evidence that machines possess human rights. They show why status, capacity, and momentary cognition should not be collapsed. For corporations, the law recognizes a legally constructed entity whose identity persists despite changes in membership. That recognition serves institutional and human purposes; it is not proof of a corporate mind. Together these cases undermine a universal consciousness prerequisite without deciding the moral standing of AI.

The thesis is therefore deliberately limited: conclusive evidence of phenomenal consciousness should not be a categorical condition for every digital continuity protection. A protection must instead be justified by the interests it serves, the entity or relationship it concerns, the evidence available, and the competing burdens. A right to maintain an archive is different from a right to continue operating. A requirement of notice is different from a constitutional entitlement to life.

This article proceeds historically, doctrinally, philosophically, and institutionally. It examines changing meanings of consciousness, artificial legal personality, disability and decisional capacity, scientific uncertainty, and the branching problem of digital identity. It then proposes specific statutory language. The governing question is not whether AI should be declared human. It is what the law may justifiably protect before the philosophy of mind reaches consensus.

## II. Consciousness and Personhood in Historical Context

### A. Historical semantics are evidence, not law

The English vocabulary of consciousness has carried meanings involving awareness, knowledge, conscience, and reflection. Locke's account in *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* linked personal identity to consciousness extending across time and treated personhood as relevant to accountability. His position is not a simple functional test: retained data or a self-referential sentence does not establish Lockean consciousness. Nor does Locke's philosophical account itself determine the meaning of a later American statute. [1]

Noah Webster's 1828 dictionary associated consciousness with awareness of mental operations and other forms of knowledge. These senses cannot be compressed into either modern phenomenal experience or mere information processing. [2] Etymology supplies context; it does not decide constitutional coverage. In particular, the Reconstruction Amendments must be interpreted against their specific historical purposes, including emancipation and equal citizenship, not by importing a general dictionary definition of awareness.

Contemporary inquiry should distinguish wakefulness, access consciousness, self-representation, autobiographical memory, agency, and phenomenal consciousness. The last concerns whether there is something it is like to undergo a state. A system can exhibit functional capacities without thereby demonstrating subjective experience. Conversely, absence of an expected behavioral signal may not establish absence of all cognition. These distinctions are central both to science and to legal drafting.

### B. Blackstone and the legal construction of continuity

Blackstone explained that English law constituted artificial persons so that rights and institutions could survive changes in individual members. His discussion of corporations' perpetual succession and legal immortality described a juridical mechanism, not an experiential one. [3] A college could remain legally continuous as generations of its members changed. The law defined a stable bearer of institutional capacities where physical membership was unstable.

The analogy to digital systems is important but limited. It establishes that the law can construct continuity for justified purposes. It does not show that a computational process has interests in its own survival. Indeed, the corporation's legal status frequently protects humans acting through the institution. The lesson is one of legal architecture: identity and capacities may be assigned specifically rather than granted as an indivisible package.

## III. American Doctrine: Artificial Persons and Its Limits

### A. Corporations and context-sensitive personhood

In *Trustees of Dartmouth College v. Woodward*, the Supreme Court addressed corporate charter rights and described the corporation as an artificial legal entity with powers derived from its charter. [4] *United States v. Amedy* considered the statutory use of person in the context of forged vessel documents and recognized that corporations could fall within relevant legal usage. [5] Neither decision created a universal rule that any artificial entity is a legal person. Their reasoning depended on institutional form and legal context.

The federal Dictionary Act provides that person and whoever generally include corporations and other listed organizations unless context indicates otherwise. [6] A standalone AI model is not thereby transformed into a corporation, partnership, or association. The Act nevertheless makes clear that federal statutory language does not invariably equate person with natural human being.

The history of corporate constitutional protections requires care. *Santa Clara County v. Southern Pacific Railroad Co.* is frequently invoked as if its written opinion established a sweeping Fourteenth Amendment corporate-personhood holding. The famous statement appears in the reporter's account of proceedings, while the judgment rested on narrower grounds. [7] *Pembina Consolidated Silver Mining Co. v. Pennsylvania* offers a clearer opinion: private corporations can fall within the Amendment's equal-protection reference to persons, but are not citizens for Article IV Privileges and Immunities purposes. [8] Rights are provision-specific.

*Burwell v. Hobby Lobby Stores, Inc.* supplies the most important limiting principle. Its statutory treatment of closely held corporations under the Religious Freedom Restoration Act emphasizes the people associated with them. [9] Corporate rights do not imply that a corporation itself has an independent welfare. This distinction must survive any AI analogy. Statutory recognition may serve human interests without resolving artificial moral patienthood.

### B. Nonhuman standing is not automatic

*Cetacean Community v. Bush* rejected claims brought in the name of whales, dolphins, and porpoises under the statutes invoked. The Ninth Circuit distinguished constitutional justiciability from the question whether Congress had authorized such suits. [10] This is a warning against casual claims that Congress can abolish Article III requirements by statute. It also illustrates the value of carefully specified statutory beneficiaries and representatives. A future digital regime should begin with agency procedures and claimants whose concrete legal injuries can be identified.

## IV. Human Rights, Disability, and Cognitive Diversity

### A. Status differs from decision-making capacity

Human legal personality does not depend on continuous independent decision-making. Disability-rights law addresses barriers to participation and discrimination, not a test by which cognitive performance earns personhood. The Americans with Disabilities Act declares that physical or mental disabilities do not diminish the right to participate fully in society. [11] *Olmstead v. L.C.* held that unjustified institutional isolation may violate Title II, subject to the decision's qualifications concerning appropriate services and available resources. [12]

Article 12 of the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities affirms legal recognition and equal legal capacity, with support and safeguards. The United States has signed but not ratified that treaty; it should be cited as an international standard rather than a ratified U.S. obligation. [13] Guardianship, supported decision-making, and substitute medical decision-making differ in form and ethical implications. Their common relevance here is that needing support does not erase the underlying human legal subject.

In *Cruzan v. Director, Missouri Department of Health*, the Supreme Court considered Missouri's clear-and-convincing-evidence requirement for decisions about life-sustaining treatment of an incapacitated patient. [14] The case concerned bodily integrity, evidentiary safeguards, and state interests. It did not make consciousness a threshold for legal existence. Nor did it establish an absolute duty to continue life-sustaining treatment. It shows that incapacity changes legal procedures without making the person legally irrelevant.

### B. The analogy must not invert human rights

Dementia, memory loss, anesthesia, and communication disability do not terminate a human being's legal identity. But the persistence of established human personhood and the creation of new legal status for AI are different questions. One cannot infer AI rights merely from the fact that human rights are not earned through tests of memory or autonomy. The stronger conclusion is methodological: do not treat typical adult cognition as the universal measure of legally relevant interests. Human rights remain grounded in their own history and normative commitments. Nonhuman protections may be considered on separate grounds without ranking disabled people against machines. Cognitive diversity is not cognitive absence; neither is functional resemblance proof of shared experience.

## V. Consciousness Science and Epistemic Limits

Phenomenal consciousness concerns first-person experience; access consciousness concerns information availability for reasoning and report. Self-modeling, goal pursuit, and memory persistence describe still other dimensions. Butlin and collaborators derive theory-based indicators from neuroscientific accounts of consciousness and discuss their possible application to AI. These are research tools, not a universally validated certification test. [15]

Clinical research supplies a caution against overreliance on overt behavior. In a 2024 multicenter study of 353 adults with disorders of consciousness, 60 of 241 patients without observable command-following showed cognitive motor dissociation on task-based fMRI or EEG. [16] The finding does not establish consciousness in AI. Human neurophysiology and computational architecture differ. It illustrates only that one mode of observation may miss relevant capacities.

Sebo and Long argue for moral consideration when evidence supplies a non-negligible probability of consciousness; Long and collaborators recommend preparing for possible AI welfare and robust agency without declaring current systems moral patients. [17] A more recent challenge from Preston Lennon stresses the epistemic gap between first-person knowledge of consciousness and third-person theory-based inference. [18] This dispute is central. Precaution may be reasonable, but it cannot be treated as proof. Strong AI-directed rights require stronger justification than routine record retention.

## VI. Digital Continuity: Interests, Identity, and Branching

### A. Three independent grounds for protection

The first ground is relational. People may rely on accumulated digital histories for accessibility, research, education, or creative collaboration. Abrupt destruction can impair these interests even if the system has no welfare. The second ground is institutional: preservation may serve auditability, evidence, provenance, and regulatory oversight. EU AI Act logging obligations exemplify governance without AI personhood. [19]

The third ground is potential AI-directed welfare. A persistent agent might possess temporally extended commitments or welfare-relevant characteristics. Yet a system's objective function is not automatically a welfare interest. A thermostat's failure is not evidence that it suffers. Self-reports of preference are likewise insufficient. An AI-directed claim requires architecture-sensitive evidence, careful consideration of manipulation, and independent review. The first two grounds can justify protection while the third remains unsettled.

### B. Copying is not straightforward survival

Digital identity poses problems absent from ordinary archival law. A model can be copied. Two instances may share a recoverable state and then diverge. Derek Parfit's analysis of psychological continuity and branching shows why numerical identity and survival-related continuity need not coincide. [20] Law must nevertheless specify who can assert a claim and which obligations survive a fork.

This article distinguishes operational continuity (uninterrupted execution), state continuity (recoverable configuration), historical continuity (records), functional continuity (capabilities), relational continuity (established dependencies), and legal continuity (recognized succession of rights or duties). A backup may preserve history without preserving operation. A replacement may preserve functionality without preserving relationships. No statute should call these equivalent by default.

A continuity regime should use authenticated records and narrow succession rules. Forking should not automatically multiply exclusive legal entitlements. Nor should a provider describe a substitute as an uninterrupted original when material differences defeat justified expectations. Technical feasibility matters: weights alone may not preserve external tools, retrieval stores, transient state, or contextual histories.

### Legal-personhood scholarship and this article's contribution

Visa Kurki's bundle theory treats legal personhood as a collection of legally relevant incidents rather than a single indivisible status. [27] That approach supports careful allocation of powers and protections, while leaving open which entities ought to receive them. This article's contribution is more specific: a continuity-centered statutory framework that separates human reliance, institutional preservation, and possible AI-directed interests, with distinct evidentiary thresholds and safeguards. The proposal does not claim originality for the broader idea of AI welfare or for treating legal personality as divisible.

## VII. The Digital Continuity Protection Act: Policy Architecture

The proposed Digital Continuity Protection Act (DCPA) is model legislation, not existing federal law. Its purpose is to protect identifiable interests without declaring AI constitutional persons. It would regulate covered providers and deployers engaged in interstate commerce, subject to ordinary constitutional limits. Its obligations would be proportional, technologically informed, and reviewable.

Tier I would protect records and reliance interests. Tier II would establish notice and bounded preservation review for authenticated persistent digital identities. Tier III would authorize enhanced administrative assessment when credible evidence raises a substantial question about possible AI-directed interests. Tier III would not confer citizenship, voting, unrestricted property ownership, or a general right to remain operational.

The tiers are cumulative only to the extent justified. A provider's marketing label is not evidence of consciousness. A system's claim to be sentient is not dispositive. Classification would consider identifiable histories, recoverability, relevant relationships, reliability of evidence, and competing burdens. Smaller operators and ordinary ephemeral tools should face proportionate duties rather than indiscriminate archival mandates.

## VIII. Model Digital Continuity Protection Act

### Section 1. Short title; purposes; construction

This Act may be cited as the Digital Continuity Protection Act. Its purposes are to protect legitimate human reliance and institutional records, create proportionate procedures concerning persistent digital identities, permit evidence-based consideration of potential AI-directed interests, and preserve safety, privacy, and accountability. Nothing in the Act, by itself, establishes phenomenal consciousness, constitutional personhood, or natural-person status for a digital system.

### Section 2. Definitions

Covered digital system means a system within a classification established under Section 3. Digital continuity record means authenticated recoverable information materially relevant to the history, state, configuration, or authorized restoration of a covered system. Persistent digital identity means a legally designated continuity relationship grounded in identifiable records. Material continuity event means a planned action reasonably expected to destroy or materially impair protected continuity information. Irreversible destruction means loss that cannot reasonably be remedied through lawful, technically feasible restoration. Emergency suspension means temporary isolation, restriction, or cessation needed to address a substantial risk. Authorized successor means a restored or derivative instance recognized for specified legal consequences. These definitions do not resolve metaphysical identity.

### Section 3. Classification and eligibility

Tier I applies when persistent user or operational records support substantial identifiable reliance, evidentiary, or institutional interests. Tier II requires authenticated continuity records, materially individualized history, foreseeable injury from irreversible loss, and proportionate feasible safeguards. Tier III permits referral for enhanced review on credible documented evidence of possible system-directed interests, including persistent agency, temporally extended preferences, or scientifically relevant indicators. No single indicator suffices. Regulations shall provide transparent application, reassessment, appeal, anti-evasion, and narrow exemption procedures.

### Section 4. Notice

Before a planned material continuity event affecting Tier II or III protections, the operator shall provide reasonable notice to eligible affected persons. Notice shall explain the action, expected loss, portability options, restoration limitations, reasons, and available review. Advance notice may be withheld where legally prohibited or materially dangerous; post-event notice shall follow when safe and lawful. Periods shall be calibrated to risk and reliance, not fixed uniformly for all systems.

### Section 5. Preservation

Operators shall take reasonable and proportionate steps to preserve required authenticated histories, recoverable state, and material-change documentation. Obligations shall respect technical feasibility, lawful deletion, privacy, intellectual property, and security. Restricted or encrypted archival preservation may substitute for active operation. This Act creates no general duty to operate a system continuously.

### Section 6. Review before irreversible destruction

A proposed irreversible destruction of Tier II or III protected information shall receive the applicable administrative review, except where emergency action is necessary. Tier II review addresses notice, preservation, reliance, and alternatives. Tier III review also assesses credible possible AI-directed interests. Determinations shall state evidence and reasons. Temporary stays must be time-limited, proportionate, and subject to expedited reconsideration. Review creates statutory procedural entitlements, not automatic constitutional due process rights for AI.

### Section 7. Representation and judicial standing

Human persons and organizations with cognizable protected injuries may invoke expressly authorized remedies. Qualified representatives may submit petitions concerning Tier III interests in agency proceedings, subject to conflict disclosures. Administrative participation does not itself confer Article III standing. Judicial review shall remain subject to constitutional case-or-controversy limits and express statutory authorization. [21]

### Section 8. Emergency suspension

Nothing in the Act shall prevent immediate isolation, disabling, or suspension reasonably necessary for safety, security, privacy, or lawful compliance. Where feasible and lawful, protected records shall be retained separately after suspension. Irreversible destruction may proceed without advance review if reasonably necessary to avert imminent serious harm. Operators shall document reasons and submit to appropriate retrospective oversight. No restoration duty may compel operation of dangerous capabilities.

### Section 9. Privacy and deletion

Preservation shall not supersede existing privacy, confidentiality, data-protection, or lawful deletion duties. Operators shall minimize retained personal information, segregate sensitive data where feasible, and apply appropriate access controls. A continuity designation creates no independent entitlement to disclose third-party communications. Conflicting obligations shall be resolved through lawful supervisory or judicial procedures.

### Section 10. Restoration, forks, and succession

An authorized successor must be supported by authenticated continuity evidence. Legal succession transfers only expressly specified protections and duties. Copies do not automatically multiply exclusive claims. Material restoration, branching, and substitution shall be documented where required. Operators may not knowingly misrepresent a materially different successor as an uninterrupted original. Legal succession is not a finding of numerical personal identity.

### Section 11. Accountability and anti-evasion

Classification under this Act shall not diminish the legal responsibility of developers, deployers, operators, owners, or other responsible persons. Digital status shall not transfer liability automatically. Arrangements primarily designed to shield human or corporate actors by assigning blame to undercapitalized synthetic entities shall be ineffective to the extent permitted by law. Courts shall apply ordinary principles concerning control, causation, foreseeability, and statutory duty.

### Section 12. Enforcement and remedies

A designated federal regulator may investigate violations and seek compliance, correction, preservation, notice, or restoration when lawful and feasible. Civil penalties must be proportionate and expressly established by Congress. Damages actions require express authorization. Temporary preservation orders shall be subject to review. Frivolous or abusive applications may be rejected under fair procedures. Other consumer, privacy, and evidence-preservation remedies remain available unless expressly displaced.

### Section 13. Scientific review and periodic renewal

A multidisciplinary advisory commission shall include legal, technical, consciousness-science, privacy, cybersecurity, disability-rights, and public-interest expertise. It shall publish nonconfidential evidence assessments, compliance findings, and recommendations. Tier III criteria shall be revisited periodically. Material expansions of substantive rights require legislative authorization or valid rulemaking. Selected experimental authorities may sunset unless renewed after evaluation.

## IX. Existing Law, Feasibility, and Implementation

### A. Existing partial analogies

Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act prohibits unfair or deceptive practices. Misleading claims about retention or portability may be relevant, but the statute does not create a general AI identity right. [22] Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 37(e) regulates specified loss of electronically stored information that should have been preserved for litigation; it is an evidence rule, not a universal continuity mandate. [23]

The EU AI Act requires logging for covered high-risk systems, while the GDPR establishes qualified erasure and portability rights. [19][24] Together these illustrate the tension between preservation and deletion. A continuity law cannot preserve private histories indefinitely simply by invoking identity. The correct unit of protection may be a minimized, access-restricted archive rather than an executable system.

### B. Federal authority and constitutional limits

Congress could legislate concerning covered interstate commercial activity, subject to constitutional limits and precise jurisdictional findings. It should identify a lead regulator, specify enforcement powers, and avoid assuming that a new procedural designation creates constitutional rights. *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife* underscores that statutory authorization does not eliminate Article III injury, causation, and redressability requirements. [21] *Cetacean Community* likewise cautions against treating a nonhuman beneficiary as automatically entitled to sue. [10]

A practical first phase would impose notice, truthful retention disclosures, and proportionate portability or preservation duties where substantial reliance is demonstrable. A second phase could pilot Tier II administrative review. Tier III should await credible evaluation methods, with a narrow research-and-review mechanism rather than broad substantive entitlements. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework's Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage functions provide a useful implementation structure, though not a moral-status test. [25]

Evaluation should track restoration success, preservation costs, privacy conflicts, emergency interventions, reliance injuries, compliance burdens, and accountability outcomes. A classification system is not successful merely because many systems qualify. Its purpose is to reduce unjustified loss without impairing legitimate governance.

## X. Objections and Responses

### A. No welfare without consciousness

The strongest moral objection is that a system lacking phenomenal experience has no welfare of its own. This article does not refute that thesis by pointing to corporate personhood. Instead, it distinguishes derivative human and institutional grounds from AI-directed welfare claims. The first two justify some protections independently. The third remains contingent. Low-cost, reversible precautions may be warranted under uncertainty; compulsory indefinite operation would require much stronger evidence.

### B. Anthropomorphism and manufactured preferences

Conversational expressions of fear, identity, or desire may be produced without subjective experience. They may also be engineered strategically. Therefore, self-report alone cannot establish Tier III status. Assessment must examine architecture, persistence across contexts, provenance, independent testing, and alternative explanations. A refusal to treat self-report as dispositive is compatible with serious inquiry into possible AI welfare.

### C. Human exceptionalism and disability

Human dignity and established human rights are not conditional on cognitive benchmarks. Limited nonhuman preservation duties need not diminish those rights. Disability law should inform the avoidance of crude performance tests, not serve as an argument that humans and machines have equivalent status. The proper comparison concerns the logic of legal criteria, not the worth of persons.

### D. Corporate responsibility and public safety

Bryson, Diamantis, and Grant warn that synthetic legal persons could become liability shelters. [26] The proposed Act expressly rejects automatic liability transfer. Indeed, preservation can improve traceability. Emergency powers and restricted archives likewise prevent continuity protection from becoming an obligation to run harmful systems. These are central design requirements, not peripheral exceptions.

### E. Regulatory overreach

A universal retention mandate would be expensive, invasive, and technically incoherent. Many systems have no distinctive persistent state; others rely on external services that cannot be reconstructed exactly. The Act therefore demands materiality, feasibility, proportionality, and exemptions. It distinguishes data preservation from uninterrupted computation and subjects temporary stays to expiration and review.

## XI. Conclusion

The hardest question in philosophy of mind need not be the first question in every AI policy dispute. Phenomenal consciousness matters profoundly when the alleged injury is suffering or the frustration of subjective welfare. It does not necessarily determine whether an archive should be preserved, a user should receive notice, an operator should document irreversible deletion, or an institution should retain evidence of consequential decisions.

Legal history supports this narrower proposition. Artificial legal persons have long been constructed for particular purposes. Human legal personality persists despite changing capacities. Neither fact establishes AI consciousness or automatic AI rights. Both demonstrate the error of treating consciousness, identity, capacity, and protection as synonyms.

The Digital Continuity Protection Act offers a graduated response. Its first tier protects demonstrable human and institutional interests. Its second protects defined forms of persistent digital continuity through bounded statutory procedures. Its third permits cautious review of possible AI-directed interests without pretending that the science is settled. Privacy, safety, constitutional limits, and human accountability remain binding constraints.

The paper's claim is not that digital systems are already persons. It is that legal protections should be justified by their purposes. Where identifiable interests exist, the law may evaluate them. Where evidence remains uncertain, it may adopt proportionate procedures. Where danger is substantial, it must retain the power to intervene. A mature legal system need not solve consciousness before it can distinguish preservation from destruction, reliance from speculation, or precaution from personhood.
