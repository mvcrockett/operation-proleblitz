# The Poverty Premium Audit

What poor people overpay for the identical thing, with sourced figures (checked November 2026). University of Michigan researchers put the total premium at roughly $2,500 to $4,000 per household per year across financial services, insurance, food, and goods. The expanded audit lives in `poverty-premium-full-audit.md`. This is the core audit it grew from.

## Money access

- Check cashing: 1.5% to 3% of every check (FDIC context: 5.6 million unbanked households, 19 million underbanked). On $2,000 a month of wages, that is hundreds a year just to touch your own pay.
- Money orders: $1 to $5 each. Six bills a month paid this way costs $72 to $360 a year for what a checking account does free.
- Overdraft fees: $26 to $35 per hit. 9% of account holders pay 79% of all overdraft fees (CFPB 2024); affected households pay $250 to $400 a year.
- Prepaid card and out-of-network ATM fees: per-use tolls on every transaction.

## Credit

- Payday loans: about $15 per $100 for two weeks, roughly 391% APR (CFPB). 80% are rolled over or followed by another loan within 14 days. The average borrower pays about $520 in fees to borrow $375 over and over. Industry fees run about $2.4 billion a year (Center for Responsible Lending).
- The same $500 emergency costs about $15 to $20 through a credit union payday alternative loan. The product is not the emergency, it is the door you are forced through.
- Pawn loans: effective APRs of 120% to 300%.
- Rent-to-own: effective APRs of 75% to 250%, total paid often 2 to 3 times retail.
- Subprime auto loans: 15% to 25% APR, a $10,000 car becomes $15,000 in payments.
- Title loans, tax refund anticipation products, buy-here-pay-here lots: same shape, different signage.

## Insurance and housing

- Car insurance with a poor credit-based score: about $1,600 more per year for identical coverage.
- Insurance paid monthly instead of annually carries installment surcharges, the pay-more-because-you-cannot-pay-upfront premium.
- Utility deposits and reconnection fees for customers with thin credit or a past shutoff.
- Weekly rentals and motels as de facto housing at multiples of monthly rent.

## Goods and food

- No bulk buying: higher per-unit prices because the warehouse size and the warehouse club membership are both out of reach. The boots problem: the cheapest tier of products needs replacement 2.3 times more often than mid-tier (Consumer Reports analysis, 2023).
- Food deserts: convenience store markups where a supermarket will not build.
- Laundromats versus in-unit machines, coin-op everything.

## The state's own premiums

- Court fines and fees that compound into license suspensions that cost jobs that make the fines unpayable.
- Bail: freedom priced by wealth for the legally innocent.
- Benefits churn: eligible people lose SNAP, Medicaid, and childcare aid to paperwork deadlines and must reapply, paying in time, gaps, and reapplications.
