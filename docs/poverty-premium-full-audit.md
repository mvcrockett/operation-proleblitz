# Operation ProleBlitz: Poverty Premium Audit, Expansion

Draft only. Nothing published. Prepared November 17, 2026 under Victorias standing order that every red cent squeezed out of the poor gets audited, no category too small.

## What the first audit already covered

One line index so we do not double count: check cashing, money orders, overdrafts, payday loans, pawn loans, rent to own, subprime auto loans, credit scored car insurance, utility deposits and reconnection fees, court fines and fees, cash bail, benefits churn, food deserts, bulk buying penalties, and the boots problem where cheap goods wear out faster. That audit put the core financial premium at roughly $2,500 to $4,000 per household per year. Everything below is the rest of the stack, on top of that.

## 1. Dying: funerals and burial

**What it is.** The last purchase a family makes under grief, time pressure, and near zero price transparency. Funeral homes know families will not comparison shop while a body waits.

**Who charges it.** Funeral homes, cemeteries, and casket and vault sellers. Two large chains dominate many local markets after buying up family owned homes and keeping the old name on the sign.

**Typical cost.** The National Funeral Directors Association median for a funeral with viewing and burial is $8,300, and that figure excludes the plot at $1,000 to $4,000, grave opening and closing at $1,000 to $3,000, and a marker at $1,500 to $5,000. A realistic all in burial lands at $12,000 to $20,000. A funeral with viewing and cremation medians at $6,280, and direct cremation with no service costs far less, which is exactly why it is rarely the first option presented.

**The markup.** Caskets carry a 200 to 500 percent markup over wholesale. A casket a home buys for about $800 routinely invoices at $3,200 or more. The same 20 gauge steel casket with an NFDA median funeral home price of $2,500 sells retail direct for about $1,195. Embalming averages about $775 and is rarely required by law, yet it is often presented as standard. The non declinable basic services fee runs $1,500 to $3,500. The FTC Funeral Rule gives families the right to an itemized General Price List and the right to buy a casket elsewhere with no handling fee, and asking for that list typically saves $2,000 to $5,000, but one study found 89 percent of families arranging funerals did not know these protections exist.

**Yearly or lifetime hit.** This is a once or twice per decade hit, not an annual one, and that is what makes it brutal. A $5,000 overspend on one funeral equals more than a full year of the core poverty premium, extracted in about 72 hours.

Sources: NFDA 2023 General Price List Study and NFDA 2024 Cremation and Burial Report via funeralcostanalyzer.com and memorialmerits.com. FTC Funeral Rule, 16 CFR Part 453.

## 2. Locked up: the carceral fee stack

Having a loved one inside is one of the most expensive things that can happen to a poor family, and almost none of the cost is the sentence itself.

### Phone and video calls

**What it is.** A monopoly telecom contract inside every facility. Families pay per minute to talk to their own people.

**Who charges it.** Securus and JPay under Aventiv, and ViaPath also called GTL or Getting Out, under exclusive facility contracts. Facilities historically took site commissions that in some places exceeded 60 to 70 percent of call revenue, which is why the rates were so high.

**Typical cost.** The FCC interim caps that took effect April 6, 2026 under the Martha Wright-Reed Act cap audio at $0.11 per minute in prisons and up to $0.19 per minute in the smallest jails including the facility additive, and video at about $0.25 per minute in prisons. Before the caps, dollar per minute rates were common. JPay video still runs $0.25 to $0.50 per minute plus a $0.99 to $1.99 connection fee per session in many contracts, and its messages cost $0.25 to $0.50 each plus $0.35 per photo. One pricing breakdown puts an engaged family at $370 to $550 a year just to stay connected by messages, photos, video, and deposits.

**Yearly hit.** At the new caps, a 15 minute call from a state prison costs at most $1.65. At legacy rates, families doing daily 15 minute calls paid $2,000 to $5,000 a year. Six states and the federal programming model now provide some free minutes, which proves the old price was never about cost.

Sources: FCC Incarcerated Peoples Communications Services orders 2024 and 2025, 47 CFR 64.6030. Legal Clarity summaries of the 2026 caps. JPay fee breakdown via yardlinkz.com citing Prison Policy Initiative and Prison Legal News, March 2025.

### Commissary and money deposits

**What it is.** The inside store and the toll booth on every dollar sent in.

**Who charges it.** Single contracted commissary vendors and deposit processors like JPay and Keefe.

**Typical cost.** A 2024 analysis of 26 state systems found commissary prices up to five times community retail, with markups reaching 600 percent on some items, and food commonly marked up 40 to 65 percent over wholesale. Deposit fees run $2.95 to $3.95 on a $20 online deposit, a 15 to 20 percent surcharge on small amounts, and phone deposits cost more. In some systems restitution deductions take 50 percent or more of incoming funds before the balance is spendable, so a $100 deposit can become $40 to $50 of actual buying power.

**Yearly hit.** A family sending $50 a month and covering commissary basics at inside prices loses roughly $150 to $400 a year to fees and markup alone.

Source: Legal Clarity commissary analysis citing Federal Bureau of Prisons lists and the 2024 26 system analysis.

### Release debit cards

**What it is.** When you get out, your own money comes back on a mandatory prepaid card that charges you to touch it.

**Who charges it.** JPay and other release card issuers under contract with jails and prisons. Before these cards, people got cash or a check.

**Typical cost.** Documented fee schedules include $5.95 monthly maintenance, $2.95 per ATM withdrawal, $9.95 to close the account and get a paper check, $0.50 to check a balance, $0.70 per purchase, and a $2.99 inactivity fee after 60 days in one Michigan schedule. The CFPB action against JPay found the company siphoned gate money meant to help people restart, and ordered $4 million in redress plus a $2 million penalty. A separate class action covering more than 1,500 facilities settled for up to $11.6 million, paying claimants $15 plus three times the fees charged.

**Hit.** Small in dollars, $10 to $40 per release is typical, and enormous in principle. It taxes a person at the exact hour they have the least, on money that was already theirs.

Sources: CFPB consent order against JPay, consumerfinance.gov. Prison Policy Initiative model release card legislation. Release card settlement records via tapclaim.app.

### Probation supervision and electronic monitoring

**What it is.** Paying monthly rent on your own supervision, and daily rent on an ankle bracelet, as a condition of staying out of a cell.

**Who charges it.** Courts, probation departments, and private supervision and monitoring companies.

**Typical cost.** Supervision fees of $20 to $50 per month are common. Rhode Island charges $20 per month for standard supervision and $6 per day for electronic monitoring parole. Electronic monitoring charged to the wearer runs $5 to $25 per day in most jurisdictions in a 31 jurisdiction review, with extremes from under $1 to $40 per day, plus setup fees like a $50 hookup fee or North Carolinas $90 device fee. GPS monitoring typically runs $8 to $25 per day and radio frequency monitoring $5 to $15 per day. At $15 a day, six months of monitoring costs $2,700 in fees alone. A year at $25 a day passes $9,000. Nearly half of states have no law requiring the court to consider ability to pay before setting these fees.

**Yearly hit.** $1,800 to $9,000 for a monitored year, stacked on top of fines, restitution, and treatment costs the person also has to pay. Miss payments and the consequence is revocation, which converts a fee into a jail stay.

Sources: Rhode Island Department of Corrections fee table, 240 RICR 50 00 1.1. Legal Clarity house arrest cost review of 31 jurisdictions. Vera Institute ranges cited in ankle monitor cost guides.

## 3. Sending money home and crossing borders on paper

### Remittances

**What it is.** The fee for a worker in one country to send wages to family in another, priced as a percentage of money that was already earned and already taxed.

**Who charges it.** Banks, money transfer operators, post offices, and mobile money services. The World Bank Remittance Prices Worldwide survey prices a standard $200 transfer.

**Typical cost.** Global average 6.62 percent in the third quarter of 2024, easing to about 6.36 percent since. Banks are the most expensive channel at 13.64 percent. Money transfer operators average 5.40 percent, post offices 7.66 percent, and mobile operators 2.84 percent. Sub Saharan Africa is the most expensive receiving region at 7.73 percent. The United Nations target is under 3 percent.

**Yearly hit.** A worker sending $300 a month at the global average loses about $239 a year. At bank pricing the same habit loses about $491 a year. On $685 billion sent to low and middle income countries in 2024, every point above the 3 percent target is billions taken from the poorest households on earth as a group.

Source: World Bank Remittance Prices Worldwide Issue 51, Q3 2024, and World Bank KNOMAD estimates.

### Immigration filing fees and immigration bond

**What it is.** Paying the government for permission to ask the government for legal status, plus full cash bond to get a detained family member out.

**Who charges it.** USCIS and ICE, and private bond companies when families cannot post cash.

**Typical cost.** Since July 2025 under Public Law 119 21, asylum costs $100 to file plus $100 for every year the case stays pending, and these new fees cannot be waived. A first work permit for asylum, parole, and TPS categories costs $550, with renewals at $275. TPS registration jumped from $50 to $500. General work permit filings run $470 online to $520 on paper. An immigration delivery bond has a legal minimum of $1,500, commonly runs $5,000 to $15,000, and can reach $25,000 or more. Unlike criminal bail, the full amount goes to the government in cash. A bond company posts it for a non refundable premium of about 15 to 20 percent plus full collateral, so a $10,000 bond costs a family $1,500 to $2,000 that never comes back.

**Hit.** A detained parent with a pending asylum case can stack $1,500 to $2,000 in bond premium, $550 in work permit fees, and $100 a year in pending fees before any lawyer is paid.

Sources: USCIS fee schedule Form G 1055 and USCIS alert on fees under H.R. 1, July 2025. Immigration bond guides citing ICE CeBONDS practice.

## 4. Tax time: paying to pay the government

**What it is.** A private toll on a legal obligation. The government already has your wage data, yet filing a simple return through a paid preparer is the default for millions of low income workers because the forms for the credits they are owed are genuinely complex.

**Who charges it.** Paid preparers and chains, tax software upsells, and the banks behind refund products.

**Typical cost.** The National Association of Tax Professionals 2025 Fee Study puts a basic Form 1040 at about $280 with a CPA, $228 with an enrolled agent, and $185 with a non credentialed preparer. Add roughly $100 to $260 for a Schedule C. A refund anticipation check or refund transfer, which does nothing but route your own refund through a temporary account so fees can be skimmed, adds $30 to $60, with H and R Block charging $42 for the transfer and $25 more for a paper check. The National Taxpayers Union Foundation estimates the average filer spends about $290 out of pocket on compliance overall.

**The EITC toll.** The Earned Income Tax Credit is worth up to about $8,046 for a family with three or more kids, and the IRS Census match study found participation at only 78 percent of eligible tax units in tax year 2021. About 5.7 million eligible units did not participate, and unclaimed EITC dollars totaled an estimated $8.2 billion that year, including $5.6 billion belonging to people who never filed at all. Complexity is the toll collector. People either pay a preparer $200 to $600 to claim what they are owed, or they lose thousands by not claiming it.

**Yearly hit.** $185 to $600 in preparation and refund product fees for a working family, every year, for a return the IRS could pre fill. For a non filer who misses the EITC, the hit is the entire credit.

Sources: NATP 2025 Fee Study via Accounting Today. CFPB guidance on refund products. IRS and Census EITC participation study for tax year 2021, CES 24 75, and IRS Tax Year 2022 benefits gap estimate.

## 5. Paper identity: documents and license stacks

### Birth certificates and ID documents

**What it is.** Paying for certified proof that you exist, required before you can work, enroll, claim benefits, or replace anything else.

**Who charges it.** State and county vital records offices and third party ordering services that add their own fees on top.

**Typical cost.** Certified birth certificate copies generally run about $15 to $35 per copy from the issuing office, with expedited and third party ordering pushing a single copy toward $50 to $90. Flag: exact state by state pricing was not pulled for every state in this pass, so treat the range as an estimate to verify state by state. The real cost is the cascade. No birth certificate means no state ID, no state ID means no job paperwork, no bank account, and no benefits, and each step charges again.

### License reinstatement fee stacks

**What it is.** Getting your license back costs far more than the reinstatement fee on the poster, and fees stack per suspension.

**Who charges it.** State licensing agencies, courts, insurers through SR 22 filings, interlock vendors, and program providers.

**Typical cost.** The reinstatement fee alone runs $25 to $500 or more depending on the offense, and multiple suspensions mean multiple fees, all payable before restoration. New York adds a Driver Responsibility Assessment of $250 a year for three years, $750 total, separate from court fines. Florida stacks a $130 alcohol fee plus a $75 revocation fee. Illinois charges $250 for a first summary suspension and $500 for each later one. Then the gates behind the fee: SR 22 filing at $25 to $50 plus premiums that commonly double for one to three years, ignition interlock at $70 to $150 to install plus $60 to $150 a month, reaching $1,000 to $2,500 over a year, plus mandatory classes and every outstanding fine paid in full first. One analysis notes the advertised fee is typically under 10 percent of what a driver actually pays to get legal again, with a California first offense routinely totaling $2,000 to $4,000 all in.

**Hit.** For a worker whose suspension started with unpaid fines, the stack converts a few hundred dollars of debt into a few thousand, and driving anyway to keep a job risks a new criminal charge. This is the fines and fees trap with a steering wheel.

Sources: Legal Clarity reinstatement cost guide. FindLaw state reinstatement table. Real Cost Report 2026 state comparison citing DMV and statutory fee schedules.

## 6. The state as bookie: lotteries

**What it is.** A state run gambling monopoly that functions as the most regressive revenue system in the budget. States keep roughly 35 to 40 percent of sales as an implicit tax after prizes, retailer commissions, and administration.

**Who charges it.** State lottery commissions, with retailers concentrated in low income neighborhoods.

**Typical cost.** A Brookings cited analysis found households earning under $30,000 spend about 2.5 percent of income on tickets against 0.5 percent for households over $100,000. Another analysis puts households under $25,000 at 3.8 percent of income against 1.4 percent over $75,000. A Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis cited figure has players earning under $20,000 spending about $46 a month, roughly $550 a year, nearly double any other bracket. Payouts average only 50 to 65 percent of sales, and instant scratch games, the most heavily bought product in poor neighborhoods, carry the worst odds. Americans spend over $85 billion a year on tickets in total.

**Yearly hit.** About $550 a year for the lowest income regular players, sold as hope and budgeted by the state as revenue.

Sources: Governing, November 2026, citing lottery payout and income share research. Brookings Institution 2019 analysis. Tax Foundation zip code sales analysis.

## 7. Connection: phones, internet, and the end of the ACP

**What it is.** Connectivity is required for jobs, school, benefits, and court dates, and the discount that made it affordable was allowed to expire.

**Who charges it.** Internet and mobile providers. Prepaid mobile plans historically charge more per gigabyte than postpaid plans and require buying the phone at full price, a pattern documented widely. Flag: a fresh per gigabyte prepaid versus postpaid price pull was not completed in this pass, so that specific comparison is carried as a known pattern, not a sourced figure here.

**Typical cost.** The Affordable Connectivity Program gave up to $30 a month, or $75 on Tribal lands, to about 23 million households. It ended June 1, 2024 when funding ran out and has not been replaced. The surviving Lifeline program gives only $9.25 a month and cuts off at 135 percent of poverty where the ACP reached 200 percent. A household that paid $20 after the ACP credit now pays about $50 for the same service. An estimated 5 million households lost internet access entirely. Provider low cost plans run $10 to $25 a month where available, with eligibility and geography gaps everywhere.

**Yearly hit.** $360 a year in lost ACP benefit for every enrolled household, $900 on Tribal lands, ongoing since mid 2024.

Sources: FCC program records via Legal Clarity and FedLaws ACP summaries, 2026. Universal Service Administrative Company Lifeline figures.

## 8. Health extras: the bills around the bill

### Ambulances

**What it is.** You do not choose the ambulance, you cannot see a price, and ground ambulances were left out of the federal No Surprises Act.

**Who charges it.** Municipal and private ambulance companies, many of them for profit.

**Typical cost.** A ground ride commonly bills $1,000 to $3,000 or more before insurance, with base rates around $800 to $2,000 for basic life support and $1,200 to $3,500 for advanced life support, plus $10 to $30 per loaded mile. The Commonwealth Fund put the average commercially insured ground bill at $1,093 in 2021. About half of emergency ambulance patients with private insurance face a potential surprise bill, with a median balance bill around $450 and averages over $1,000 in some states, totaling about $129 million a year paid by insured patients. Only 22 states have patched the federal gap as of early 2026.

Source: U.S. PIRG report on ambulance surprise bills. Commonwealth Fund average via thepricer.org.

### Facility fees and hospital parking

**What it is.** A second charge for walking into the building, billed on top of the professional fee for the same visit, plus paid parking at the hospital where your family member is a patient.

**Typical cost.** Facility fees for clinic visits and outpatient departments commonly run from about $50 to several hundred dollars per visit and are a documented driver of surprise outpatient bills. Flag: a single national average was not verified in this pass because fees are set facility by facility and rarely published, so no headline number is claimed. Hospital parking at urban medical centers commonly runs $10 to $30 per day for visitors and patients. Flag: parking pricing is facility specific and was not systematically sourced in this pass. Both belong in the audit as real, recurring, and deliberately unpriced until you are already there.

### Dental, vision, and hearing

**What it is.** The three senses carved out of standard health coverage, sold back through separate plans with annual caps that have barely moved in decades.

**Hearing, the clearest markup.** A prescription hearing aid averaged $2,363 per device in the survey cited by the Presidents Council of Advisors on Science and Technology, about $4,726 a pair, while the Veterans Health Administration bought the same class of devices at about $400 per unit. Component costs were put under $100 in a 2010 study cited in the same report. Consumer Reports analysis found clinic markups averaging about 117 to 120 percent over wholesale, with services bundled so the device price cannot be seen. Original Medicare covers none of it. Since the FDA over the counter rule took effect in October 2022, OTC pairs average about $233 to $502 paid, and the White House estimated savings of about $2,800 a pair, which is a confession of what the markup was. Clinic pairs still average about $2,694 paid in recent HearingTracker survey data, with premium pairs reaching $7,500 or more.

**Dental and vision.** Flag: detailed national pricing for routine dental and vision was not pulled in this pass. The structural facts are well established and should be verified with figures before publication. Dental plans commonly cap annual benefits near $1,000 to $1,500, a level set in the 1970s in many plan designs, while a single crown commonly costs $1,000 or more out of pocket. Glasses through optical chains commonly run $200 to $400 a pair against online prices a fraction of that. Both are queued for a sourced follow up pull.

### Medical debt and its interest

**What it is.** Care billed at list price to the people with the least coverage, then collected with court costs, garnishment, and in some states judgment interest, or sold to collectors.

**Typical cost.** Flag: a verified national total for medical debt interest specifically was not pulled in this pass. The mechanism is the finding. Unpaid hospital bills become judgments, judgments in many states accrue statutory interest and add court costs and attorney fees, and wage garnishment converts a health event into a multi year pay cut. This entry stays in the audit as a mechanism with figures to be sourced, not as a headline number.

Sources for hearing: PCAST 2015 report via HearingTracker and Sonova and Demant margin reporting. HearingTracker purchaser surveys 2018 to 2026.

## 9. Kids: childcare, diapers, school, and the college door

### Childcare

**What it is.** The second rent. Licensed care is priced like housing because ratios are low and labor is real, and families pay it at the exact age when their earnings are lowest.

**Typical cost.** Child Care Aware of America put average full time center based infant care at $14,070 in 2023, with state averages from about $8,000 to $25,000. The national average across ages is about $13,128 per child per year. That is at least 10 percent of median family income in 48 states, about 20 percent in the least affordable states, and an average of 39 percent of income for single parents for infant care against a federal affordability benchmark of 7 percent. About 51 percent of Americans live in a childcare desert with more than three young children per licensed slot. Two kids in center care in Ohio average $24,670 a year.

**Yearly hit.** $13,000 to $15,000 per young child is the single largest line in this entire expansion, larger than rent in many counties, and it is the main reason a second income in a poor household can net close to zero.

Sources: Child Care Aware of America via Bankrate Cost of Infant Care Study. Center for American Progress desert data. Department of Labor National Database of Childcare Prices.

### Diapers and formula

**What it is.** Non negotiable consumables that SNAP and WIC largely do not cover. Diapers cannot be bought with food stamps at all.

**Typical cost.** An adequate diaper supply runs about $80 to $100 a month, roughly $1,000 a year per child, plus about $500 a year for wipes in one widely cited estimate. One in two U.S. families report struggling to afford diapers in National Diaper Bank Network reporting, and one in three mothers report cutting back on food, utilities, or care to buy them. Childcare centers require parents to supply 6 to 8 diapers a day, so diaper need directly blocks work. Formula runs about $1,200 to $2,400 a year depending on type, with specialty formulas higher and medically necessary.

**Yearly hit.** About $1,500 to $3,400 for a formula fed infant in diapers, before childcare itself.

Sources: National Diaper Bank Network figures via United Way and Baby2Baby program data. Healthline usage estimates.

### School fees and college door fees

**What it is.** Public school that is not quite free, and a college application process that charges per attempt.

**Typical cost.** Flag: a verified national average for K to 12 activity, supply, and course fees was not pulled in this pass. The components are standard and locally published. Supply lists, activity and athletic fees, instrument rental, and field trip charges commonly total $100 to $500 per child per year in district fee schedules, to be verified district by district. An AP exam costs about $99 per test in recent College Board schedules, flagged for verification at publication. College application fees commonly run $50 to $90 per school, so applying to six schools costs $300 to $540 before any campus visit, with fee waivers available but unevenly offered. Textbooks are verified: the College Board budgets about $1,200 a year, Education Data Initiative figures put average books and supplies at about $1,226 to $1,370 a year, and access codes that expire and kill the used market push single courses past $175 for a book plus code. Prices rose about 864 percent since 1978 by one widely cited measure, and about 70 percent of students report skipping a required book over price in Student PIRGs surveys.

Sources: College Board and Education Data Initiative via SoFi and Best Colleges summaries. Student PIRGs 2020 report.

## 10. Housing extras: the fees around the rent

### Application fees per hunt

**What it is.** A non refundable fee to be considered, charged per adult, per property, win or lose.

**Typical cost.** $25 to $75 per applicant is typical, over $100 in hot markets, against an actual screening cost to the landlord of about $15 to $50. Most renters apply to 3 to 5 properties, so a two adult household commonly spends $150 to $750 per move on applications alone. New York caps the fee at $20 and Massachusetts bans it, which proves the fee is a policy choice, not a cost of nature.

Sources: LeaseRunner 2026 state guide. Legal Clarity application fee analysis.

### Deposit alternatives that are not deposits

**What it is.** Insurance products sold as deposit freedom. You pay monthly or yearly, forever non refundable, and the company can still come after you for damages it pays out.

**Typical cost.** Rhino charges about $5 to $25 a month by credit profile, $360 over a two year lease on a $1,500 deposit at $15 a month. Jetty charges about 17 to 19 percent of the deposit amount per year, $255 to $285 a year on that same deposit, $510 to $570 over two years. LeaseLock style products run $19 to $89 a month in some buildings. A cash deposit comes back. These never do, so on any stay past about two years the alternative costs more than the typical deposit deduction it replaced.

Sources: Rhino and Jetty pricing summaries, Q2 2026 renter guides.

### Mobile home lot rent

**What it is.** You own the home. An investor owns the dirt. Moving the home costs $5,000 to $15,000, so you cannot leave, and the rent knows it.

**Typical cost.** Lot rents run $300 to $1,000 a month, national average near $380, and investor owned parks raise them about 3.9 percent a year on average against 0.9 percent in resident owned communities, with commercial park averages near 7 percent in recent data. Median lot rents rose about 45 percent in a decade by Census figures. The extreme case is documented in a Massachusetts attorney general suit: one park went from $302 a month in 2022 to $535 by 2025, then terminated every tenancy and offered $703, a 133 percent total jump, before a court capped it at $385.

**Yearly hit.** A single $75 a month increase is $900 a year taken from a household that by definition chose the cheapest housing in America.

Sources: ROC USA increase comparisons. Census lot rent trend via Legal Clarity. Massachusetts AG complaint, Willow Terrace, Taunton, via southcoast360.com.

### Storage units

**What it is.** Renting a small room for your things after a move, an eviction, or a downsizing, on a month to month contract with one sided price power.

**Typical cost.** National average about $89 a month across sizes, about $80 for a 10 by 10, with $20 to $35 admin fees, $15 to $25 locks, and $10 to $30 a month in often mandatory protection plans. The trap is the increase cycle: a first hike inside 3 to 9 months and 5 to 15 percent yearly after, with 10 to 20 percent in the first year common, and no state caps increases outside emergencies. Late fees run $10 to $50, and after 30 to 90 days unpaid the unit contents can be liened and auctioned, with sale costs added to the balance. A $99 advertised unit realistically costs $1,400 to $1,600 in year one.

Sources: SpareFoot May 2026 pricing data. Legal Clarity storage increase guide.

### Lease to own and rent to own housing

**What it is.** Contracts where the tenant pays above market rent plus an option fee of about 1 to 5 percent of the price, does the repairs, and forfeits everything on a single default or a balloon they cannot finance.

**Typical cost.** Flag: national forfeiture and failure rates for lease option housing are not tracked in one verified source pulled in this pass. The structure is the finding and it mirrors the rent to own goods math from the first audit. Most contracts convert at low rates because the same credit profile that forced the contract also blocks the mortgage, and every payment above market rent is lost on failure. Queued for a sourced pull on contract for deed outcomes before publication.

## 11. Transport extras

### Towing and impound

**What it is.** A non consensual tow converts a parking mistake into a daily meter you did not agree to, and the car you need for work is the hostage.

**Typical cost.** Hookup $75 to $250, mileage $3 to $7 per mile, storage $20 to $75 a day in typical ranges and $30 to $100 in others, plus $25 to $100 in admin or release fees and $40 to $100 after hours. A three day city impound commonly totals $345 to $725. A 30 day DUI hold in San Diego pricing reaches $2,530 to $2,710. At $65 a day, 30 days of storage alone is $1,950, after which the car is often lien sold, so the owner loses the car and still owes the difference in some places.

Sources: USATowFinder and Legal Clarity 2026 fee surveys. San Diego impound breakdown via cadriversafety.com citing California Vehicle Code caps.

### Toll by plate

**What it is.** Cashless tolling charges drivers without a transponder a higher toll plus a billing fee, which is to say it charges the unbanked and the occasional driver more for the same road.

**Typical cost.** North Carolina bill by mail adds about 50 percent over the transponder rate, plus a $6 processing fee on a second invoice and a $25 civil penalty on a third. Pennsylvania Toll By Plate runs about 45 percent over E-ZPass plus $2 per invoice for residents and $5 for out of state drivers. Florida adds a $2.50 per invoice fee on tolls 25 to 50 percent higher, escalating to a $50 violation fee per unpaid toll. Massachusetts pay by plate roughly doubles many tolls plus a $0.60 billing fee. Rental car drivers get the worst version, with PlatePass charging the highest undiscounted toll plus $9.99 per day of toll use through Hertz.

Sources: NC Quick Pass, Pennsylvania Turnpike, Florida Toll By Plate, and MassDOT rate summaries via usaezpass.com state guides. Hertz PlatePass terms via Legal Clarity.

### Inspections, emissions, and gas price zones

**What it is.** State inspection and emissions fees of roughly $10 to $40 where required, and station level price zoning where the same brand charges more in poor neighborhoods.

**Typical cost.** Flag: neither a national inspection fee average nor a sourced gas price zone differential was verified in this pass. Both are real and locally documented, and both are queued rather than claimed. Gas zone pricing in particular needs a market by market study citation before it runs as a number.

## 12. The small humiliations tier

These are the charges too small to fight and too constant to ignore. Each one is priced under the cost of complaining about it.

**Laundromats.** Wash $2.00 to $4.00 and dry $1.50 to $2.50, so $3.50 to $6.00 per load, and up to $4 to $8 in higher cost markets. A family doing 5 loads a week pays $70 to $120 a month in machine fees alone, $840 to $1,440 a year, plus detergent, transport, and about two hours per trip. The same laundry at home costs roughly $300 a year in water and energy for four loads a week. Sources: LaundroMaps and St. Pete Laundry Rentals 2026 price guides.

**Bottled water where the tap is distrusted.** Flag: household level spending was not verified with a single strong source in this pass. The pattern is documented in Flint and Jackson coverage and in bottled water industry sales data, where a family buying drinking water by the case or jug pays many times the per gallon price of tap water it already pays a utility bill for. Queued for a sourced pull. Do not publish a number until then.

**Coin op and pay per use everything.** Car wash bays, vacuums, air for tires that used to be free at gas stations and now commonly runs $1 to $2 per use, flagged as an observed retail price rather than a surveyed figure.

**Late fees on everything.** Library fines are being abolished in many systems precisely because they taxed the poor and blocked access, while private late fees persist across storage, rentals, and utilities at $10 to $50 per event as documented in the sections above. The pattern across this file is the figure: a household that is late twice a year on four different bills pays $80 to $400 for the crime of being short in the wrong week.

**Notary fees.** State capped per signature fees commonly run $5 to $25, while mobile and jail and hospital notaries and signing services charge $50 to $200 for the same stamp delivered to someone who cannot travel. Flag: the mobile premium range is an observed market range, not a surveyed average.

**Check printing and paper statements.** A box of personal checks commonly costs $20 to $40 through bank vendors against a fraction of that at discount printers, and paper statement and cashier check fees of $5 to $35 each persist at major banks. Flag: bank by bank fee schedules were not pulled in this pass. Queued for the banking fee schedule pull that pairs with the overdraft work.

**Wiring and sending money in person.** Covered in dollars under remittances for cross border transfers. Domestic wire fees at major banks commonly run $25 to $30 outgoing, flagged as a standard published bank fee to verify in the same banking pull.

**Gift card breakage.** Americans leave an estimated $21 to $23 billion in gift card value unredeemed, with typical breakage of 2 to 6 percent of value and 43 percent of adults holding at least one unused card averaging about $244 per holder in one Bankrate cited survey. Starbucks alone reported $212 million in breakage revenue in 2022. It is the friendliest looking item in this file and one of the largest totals. Sources: Bankrate 2024 gift card survey via 99minds.io and California Senate Bill 22 analysis.

## 13. Scam adjacent: selling hope and help

### Credit repair and debt settlement

**What it is.** Charging monthly fees to dispute credit report items anyone can dispute free, and charging 15 to 25 percent of enrolled debt to settle debts, in an industry where advance fees are flatly illegal and still common.

**Typical cost.** Debt settlement firms charge 15 to 25 percent of enrolled debt, so $1,500 to $2,500 on $10,000 enrolled, plus the interest and late fees that pile up while the program tells you to stop paying. The FTC Telemarketing Sales Rule, 16 CFR 310.4, bans any fee before a debt is actually settled, agreed in writing, and paid on at least once, and bans front loading fees across enrolled debts. The FTC shut down a $100 million debt relief scam targeting seniors and veterans in 2025. Nonprofit debt management plans do the legitimate version for under $75 setup and under $60 a month.

Sources: FTC Telemarketing Sales Rule guidance and enforcement summaries via consumersweek.com and getoutofdebt.org.

### MLMs and recruitment economics

**What it is.** A business opportunity sold hardest to people who need income, where the product is the recruiting.

**Typical cost.** The AARP Foundation 2018 nationally representative survey found 47 percent of participants lost money and 27 percent broke even, so about three in four made nothing or less. Jon Taylors analysis submitted to the FTC estimated about 99 percent lose money once expenses count, a figure that is widely cited and contested, and this audit carries both numbers side by side on purpose. The FTCs own 2024 staff review of 70 income disclosures found most participants made $1,000 a year or less, and in at least 17 companies most made nothing at all, before expenses like starter kits, monthly minimum orders, tools, and conventions.

Sources: AARP Foundation 2018 MLM study. FTC September 2024 consumer alert on MLM income disclosures. Taylor analysis as characterized in FTC comment coverage.

### For profit colleges

**What it is.** Schools that spend on recruiting what public colleges spend on teaching, priced at two to five times community college cost, funded by federal loans the student owes regardless of outcome.

**Typical cost.** Students pay about $9,200 more per year than at public institutions by a Senate cited figure. Six year graduation for four year for profit programs runs about 20 to 30 percent against 60 to 65 percent at publics. Median bachelor degree debt was $31,190 for for profit graduates against $7,960 at publics in Education Trust analysis of federal aid data. For profits enroll about 8 percent of students and account for about 30 percent of federal loan defaults. Two year for profit graduates earned about $4,000 less per year than community college graduates in Center for Analysis of Postsecondary Education and Employment findings, and some programs left graduates earning less than high school graduates nearby.

**Lifetime hit.** A $23,000 debt gap plus a $4,000 annual earnings gap compounds to six figures over a working life, for the students with the least margin to absorb it.

Sources: NCES IPEDS and College Scorecard via colledgecounselor.com. Senate counselor warning letter, April 2026. Education Trust, Subprime Opportunity.

### Timeshares

**What it is.** A week of vacation sold as an asset, with maintenance fees that rise yearly and a resale market where listings at $1 are common.

**Typical cost.** Flag: no verified national figures were pulled in this pass. The exit industry that has grown up to free owners, charging thousands to escape a contract they already paid for once, is itself the audit finding. Queued for a sourced pull on average purchase price, maintenance fee growth, and resale values before any number runs.
