# Insurance Footholds and Overdraft Law

Project Mass Disruption, Lane 4 armory draft. Researched and written November 2026.
This is a research draft for later attorney review. It is not legal advice, and nothing here should be filed or published as is.

How to read the ratings: STRONG means a court or regulator has already accepted the theory on similar facts. PLAUSIBLE means the theory is sound but fact dependent or untested in Arkansas. WEAK means it usually loses and should only ride along as support.

## Part 1. The thread Victoria asked to pull: undisclosed insurance surcharges

The question: if an insurer charges a fee or surcharge that is not spelled out in the policy you signed, is that a breach of contract?

The honest answer: sometimes, and the winning version of the claim is usually not framed as pure breach of contract. It is framed as charging a rate the insurer never filed with the state, or as a deceptive practice. Here is each theory, rated.

### 1A. Breach of contract, standing alone. Rating: PLAUSIBLE, and it lives or dies on the paperwork

A policy is a contract. If the declarations page states one premium and the insurer quietly collects more, or the policy defines premium one way and bills another, that is a real contract claim. The failure mode is just as real. In In re Insurance Installment Fee Cases (Cal. Ct. App. 2012), policyholders sued State Farm over monthly installment fees of $1 to $3 that were not on the declarations page, arguing the fees were disguised extra premium. They lost. The court held the fees were payment plan charges, not premium, and because the policy required payment in full up front, offering installments for a fee did not breach anything. Lesson for the armory: a breach claim needs a policy term the fee actually contradicts, not just a fee the policyholder dislikes. Get the declarations page, the policy's definition of premium, and any separate billing or payment plan agreement before rating any individual case.

Source: https://www.insurereinsure.com/2012/12/21/california-court-finds-for-insurer-in-service-fee-case/

### 1B. Charging a rate or fee that was never filed with the state. Rating: STRONG where the filing law covers the charge

Most states require insurers to file their rates and rating rules with the insurance department and to charge what they filed. Arkansas is a file and use state for property and casualty lines. Ark. Code Ann. 23-67-211 requires insurers to file all rates and supplementary rate information, and subsection (c) says it plainly: insurers must adhere to filings made under this section until the filings are amended or withdrawn. A charge that appears in no filing is a charge the insurer had no authority to make.

The best case on point is Villanueva v. Fidelity National Title Co., 11 Cal.5th 104 (2021). A title insurer charged escrow, courier, and deed preparation fees that had never been filed with the California Insurance Commissioner. The California Supreme Court held that consumers could sue over the unfiled charges, that a statutory immunity for acts done under the rate filing laws did not shield charging unauthorized rates, and that the Commissioner did not have exclusive jurisdiction. Charging an unfiled rate is not ratemaking. It is just charging without permission.

The Arkansas caveat, stated honestly: whether Arkansas courts recognize a private lawsuit for a rate filing violation, as opposed to a complaint to the Arkansas Insurance Department, has not been verified in this research pass. The regulatory complaint route is certain. The private suit route is the test case an attorney would need to build.

Sources: https://supreme.courts.ca.gov/sites/default/files/supremecourt/default/2022-08/S252035.pdf and https://webftp.blr.arkansas.gov/Home/FTPDocument?path=Bills%2F2007%2FPublic%2FSB228%2FSB228-012120051448.pdf (text of 23-67-211, including the adherence rule)

### 1C. The filed rate doctrine, which is the shield on the other side. Rating: STRONG, for the insurer

If the fee was in the approved filing, most courts will not let a jury second-guess whether it was reasonable. That is the filed rate doctrine: a filed and approved rate is treated as reasonable per se, and challenges to its reasonableness belong to the regulator. See Coll v. First American Title Insurance Co. (10th Cir. 2011), dismissing damages claims that attacked regulator set title insurance premiums, and Francese v. American Modern Insurance Group (D.N.J. 2019), dismissing force placed insurance kickback claims on the same ground.

The crack in the shield matters more than the shield. The doctrine bars attacks on the rate itself. It does not bar claims that the insurer lied about the rate or charged something different from what it filed. In McCarthy Finance, Inc. v. Premera, 328 P.3d 940 (Wash. 2014), the Washington Supreme Court held that the filed rate doctrine does not necessarily bar consumer protection claims based on fraud or misrepresentation, even when damages are measured by premiums paid. Practical rule for every complaint and lawsuit in this armory: never argue the filed rate is too high. Argue the charge was never filed, was never disclosed, or does not match what the company told the customer. Those arguments survive the doctrine.

Sources: https://app.midpage.ai/document/mccarthy-finance-inc-v-premera-4763357 and https://www.studicata.com/summaries/court-of-appeal-of-california/in-re-insurance-installment-fee-cases-2012-kd2s2r/

### 1D. Implied covenant of good faith, and the Arkansas bad faith tort. Rating: WEAK for fee gripes, STRONG only on ugly facts

Every insurance contract carries an implied promise that the insurer will deal fairly. In Arkansas, that idea mostly lives inside the tort of bad faith, and Arkansas sets the bar high on purpose. Under Aetna Casualty and Surety Co. v. Broadway Arms Corp., 281 Ark. 128 (1984), a policyholder must show affirmative misconduct by the insurer, without a good faith defense, that is dishonest, malicious, or oppressive in an attempt to avoid liability under the policy. A good faith denial, a compromise offer, negligence, or an honest mistake is not enough, and later cases such as Switzer v. Shelter Mutual Insurance Co., 362 Ark. 419 (2005), repeat the point. An undisclosed $5 surcharge, without more, will not clear that bar. A pattern of knowingly billing charges the company knew were unauthorized, documented in its own records, starts to look like the Regions Bank fact pattern in Part 3 and is a different conversation.

Flag: this pass did not verify whether Arkansas recognizes a standalone implied covenant claim in insurance separate from the Broadway Arms tort. Treat the tort as the vehicle unless an Arkansas attorney says otherwise.

Source: https://ezel.ai/templates/insurance-bad-faith-demand/ar (collecting Broadway Arms and its progeny)

### 1E. Unfair and deceptive practices statutes. Rating: PLAUSIBLE in Arkansas, STRONG in the right state, STRONGEST as an Attorney General complaint

There are two Arkansas statutes and they behave very differently.

The Arkansas Insurance Trade Practices Act, Ark. Code Ann. 23-66-201 and following, with the key list at 23-66-206, makes misrepresentation, unfair discrimination, and unfair claims settlement practices unlawful. It has no private right of action. Only the Insurance Commissioner can enforce it, which is exactly why the complaint strategy in Part 2 matters. A policyholder cannot sue under it, but a policyholder can hand the Department a documented violation and ask it to act.

The Arkansas Deceptive Trade Practices Act, Ark. Code Ann. 4-88-101 and following, does allow private suits, but Act 986 of 2017 narrowed it hard. A private plaintiff must now prove an actual financial loss, defined as the difference between what was paid and the actual market value of what was provided, proximately caused by reliance on the deceptive practice. Private class actions are barred. Treble damages are gone for private plaintiffs. The Attorney General kept the bigger weapons, including civil penalties of up to $10,000 per violation under 4-88-113(a). There is also a safe harbor at 4-88-101(3) for actions or transactions permitted under laws administered by a regulator, and insurers argue it swallows insurance conduct wholesale. Federal courts in Arkansas have split on how far that harbor reaches, as the briefing in Willsey v. Shelter Mutual Insurance Co. (W.D. Ark. 2013) shows.

Proof the AG route has teeth in this exact space: in February 2025 the Arkansas Attorney General sued General Motors and OnStar for collecting driving data and selling it to data brokers who resold it to insurers, which then used it to raise rates and deny coverage. That is the state using deceptive practices law against an insurance adjacent extraction scheme.

In states with a strong consumer statute, this theory is the lead claim, not the backup. Villanueva itself was won under the California Unfair Competition Law, and Ruiz v. CSAA, 222 Cal. App. 4th 596 (2013), shows a disguised premium case, installment charges labeled as finance charges, surviving into a class settlement posture.

Sources: https://www.alfainternational.com/wp-content/uploads/Arkansas-2024-Insurance-Compendium.pdf and https://www.findlemonlaw.com/states/arkansas/ and https://www.privacylawnetwork.com/states/arkansas.html

### 1F. Unfair discrimination in rates. Rating: PLAUSIBLE as complaint content, WEAK as a private lawsuit

Arkansas rate law says rates shall not be excessive, inadequate, or unfairly discriminatory, Ark. Code Ann. 23-67-208. A rate is unfairly discriminatory when it does not equitably reflect differences in expected losses and expenses between similar risks. The Trade Practices Act separately defines unfair discrimination at 23-66-206(13), including discrimination between risks of the same class and essentially the same hazard. Enforcement runs through the Commissioner, who can disapprove rates under 23-67-213 after the procedures there. A policyholder who can show two like risks priced differently without a loss based reason has excellent complaint material and, on current Arkansas law, a poor standalone lawsuit. Feed it to the Department and the AG.

Source: https://codes.findlaw.com/ar/title-23-public-utilities-and-regulated-industries/ar-code-sect-23-67-213/

### 1G. Credit based insurance scores. Rating: STRONG as a compliance checklist, because the rules are specific and violations are provable

Arkansas regulates credit scoring in personal lines at Ark. Code Ann. 23-67-405 to 23-67-409, built by Act 1452 of 2003. Per the NAIC summary of those sections, an insurer in Arkansas may not use income, gender, ethnic group, religion, marital status, address, ZIP code, or nationality as scoring factors. It may not base rates solely on a credit score, may not consider the absence of a credit history, and may not consider medical collection accounts. It must recalculate the score after three years, must disclose on the application or when the application is taken that credit information will be used in underwriting or rating, must give up to four specific reasons that primarily influenced an adverse decision based on credit, and must file its scoring models with the Department. Federal law adds a second layer: a premium increase based on a consumer report is an adverse action under the Fair Credit Reporting Act, and the insurer must give notice, identify the reporting agency, disclose the score used, and honor dispute rights, 15 U.S.C. 1681a(k) and 1681m.

For comparison and for coalition work in other states, California, Hawaii, Massachusetts, and Michigan ban credit based scoring in auto insurance outright, and Maryland bars it in homeowners rating and limits it to new auto policies only.

Every one of these Arkansas duties is a yes or no question a complaint can ask. Did you disclose. Did you give four reasons. Did you use a medical collection. Did you re-score after three years. That is why this is strong: it does not ask anyone to weigh fairness, only to check boxes the insurer either checked or did not.

Sources: https://content.naic.org/sites/default/files/model-law-chart-mc-20-use-of-credit-reports-scoring-in-underwriting.pdf and https://www.claimsjournal.com/news/southcentral/2003/04/29/28386.htm and https://www.4homeownersinsurance.com/guides/insurance-scores-and-credit/

### 1H. Installment fees and payment plan surcharges. Rating: PLAUSIBLE, and it turns on state law and the filing

The honest national picture is split. California treated installment fees as lawful non premium charges in the State Farm cases above. The District of Columbia code shows the opposite drafting choice: D.C. Code 31-2231.07(b) bars collecting any premium or charge above the amount specified in the policy under the classifications and rates filed with and approved by the Commissioner, while expressly allowing a reasonable fee for a premium payment plan. So in DC style regimes the question becomes whether the plan fee is reasonable and whether anything beyond it was charged. In Arkansas, whether a given installment or billing fee had to be inside the 23-67-211 rate filing is the pivotal question, and this pass did not find an Arkansas case answering it. That gap is itself useful. An AID complaint asking the Department to say whether the fee was filed forces the issue into the open at no cost to the policyholder.

Source: https://code.dccouncil.gov/us/dc/council/code/sections/31-2231.07

### 1I. Ambiguity read against the drafter, and the illusory policy argument. Rating: PLAUSIBLE to STRONG as a supporting argument, WEAK alone

Arkansas follows contra proferentem with real conviction. Insurance policies are construed liberally in favor of the insured and strictly against the insurer, and ambiguities are resolved for the insured. See Smith v. Prudential Property and Casualty Insurance Co. (Ark. 2000) and Home Indemnity Co. v. City of Marianna (Ark. 1989), where the insurer's own drafting confusion was held against it. If the policy's premium and fee language can reasonably be read two ways, one allowing the surcharge and one not, Arkansas law picks the reading without the surcharge. This rarely wins a case by itself, but it tilts every close contract question in Part 1A toward the policyholder, and it belongs in every demand letter. The related claim that hidden charges make the policy illusory almost never wins on its own and is not rated above WEAK.

Source: https://www.studicata.com/summaries/supreme-court-of-arkansas/smith-v-prudential-property-casualty-insurance-company-2000-33vt2x/

### Bottom line on the thread

Undisclosed surcharges are most dangerous to an insurer when the charge was never filed, never disclosed as the credit and fee rules require, or described to the customer in a way that does not match the paperwork. Frame it that way and the claim is strong. Frame it as "the price was unfair" and the filed rate doctrine eats it.

## Part 2. Foothold map: the levers a poor policyholder actually has in Arkansas

Ranked by cost and leverage, cheapest first.

### Lever 1. The Arkansas Insurance Department complaint. Cost: free. Leverage: high

File at the AID Consumer Services Division, online at https://insurance.arkansas.gov/consumer-assistance/consumer-services/file-a-complaint/ or by phone at 1-800-282-9134. The complaint goes into the NAIC tracking system, the insurer must respond to the Department, and complaint patterns feed market conduct examinations. AID's stated mission is consumer protection through market conduct regulation. One complaint gets a letter answered. A documented pattern of identical complaints is how examinations and enforcement start. This is the natural delivery vehicle for the Part 1B unfiled charge theory, the Part 1F discrimination theory, and the Part 1G credit scoring checklist. Practical tip from AID's own rules: complaints become public records, so write them like exhibits, accurate and sourced.

### Lever 2. The 12 percent penalty and attorney fee statute, Ark. Code Ann. 23-79-208. Cost: a demand letter. Leverage: very high on unpaid claims

When a covered loss happens and the insurer fails to pay within the time the policy specifies after demand is made, the insurer owes the amount of the loss plus 12 percent damages on the loss plus all reasonable attorney fees for collecting it. No proof of bad faith is required. This is a strict penalty for slow or non payment, and the fee shifting is what makes small and mid size claims economically real for a plaintiff's lawyer. Two precision points. First, the policyholder keeps the penalty and fees if the amount recovered comes within 20 percent of the amount demanded, and for attorney fees on a homeowner's policy the window is 30 percent, so the demand number must be honest and documented, not inflated. Second, Gafford v. Allstate Insurance Co., 2015 Ark. 110, holds that attorney fees in an insurance contract action are available exclusively under 23-79-208, not under the general contract fee statute, so plead this section by name.

Statute text: https://codes.findlaw.com/ar/title-23-public-utilities-and-regulated-industries/ar-code-sect-23-79-208/

### Lever 3. External review for health insurance denials. Cost: free. Leverage: high, and the decision binds the insurer

Federal rules at 45 CFR 147.136 give a denied health plan member an internal appeal, due within 180 days of the denial notice, and then an external review by independent medical reviewers, requested within four months of the final internal denial. The plan must accept the external reviewer's decision. Arkansas runs its process through the Insurance Department under AID Rule 76, with requests to the External Review Division at the Department in Little Rock, a standard decision window of about 45 days, and expedited review when delay would seriously jeopardize health. This lever is for medical necessity and similar judgment based denials. It will not help with a benefit the plan plainly excludes, and self funded employer plans follow the federal process instead, so the first step is always reading the denial letter for which process applies.

Sources: https://portal.insurance.arkansas.gov/LegalPubsPublic/Documents/Rules/rule_76.pdf and https://www.blackhealth.org/articles/how-to-appeal-health-insurance-denial-by-state/

### Lever 4. Claims handling deadlines under AID Rule 43. Cost: free. Leverage: quiet but real

Arkansas Rule 43, recodified at 23 CAR 15-108, sets minimum claims handling standards, including acknowledgment and investigation timelines and, for total loss auto settlements, a rule that any deduction in a settlement that deviates from the approved methods must be measurable, discernible, itemized, and specified as to dollar amount. An adjuster's unexplained deduction is a Rule 43 problem the Department can act on, and citing the rule by number in a complaint changes the conversation. Flag: the specific day counts in this paragraph come from secondary summaries of Rule 43 and should be confirmed against the rule text before any filing relies on them.

Source: https://secondappraisal-standby.onrender.com/states/arkansas

### Lever 5. The bad faith tort. Cost: a lawsuit. Leverage: high ceiling, high bar

Broadway Arms bad faith, Part 1D above, supports compensatory damages including consequential loss and emotional distress, and punitive damages on egregious facts. The limitations period is three years for the tort and five years for the contract claim. Reserve this lever for documented, knowing misconduct. Overpleading it on ordinary disputes hurts credibility with the same regulators the rest of this map depends on.

### Lever 6. Appraisal clauses for valuation fights. Cost: low. Leverage: situational

Most property and auto policies let either side demand appraisal when the fight is only about the amount of loss. In Arkansas, appraisal is treated as voluntary and nonbinding, per AID guidance collected at Ark. Code Ann. 23-79-203 and Bulletin 19-89, so it is a pressure tool rather than a final answer, and it pairs well with a 23-79-208 demand when the insurer lowballs. Flag: this characterization comes from secondary sources in this pass and needs confirmation before reliance.

### Lever 7. The Attorney General. Cost: free. Leverage: pattern dependent

The AG enforces the ADTPA with civil penalties up to $10,000 per violation and is not bound by the reliance and actual financial loss limits that Act 986 put on private plaintiffs. The GM and OnStar suit in Part 1E is the template: individual complaints rarely move an AG, an organized file of identical complaints with documents attached is an investigation memo that writes itself. Consumer Protection Division complaints can be filed through arkansasag.gov.

### Lever 8. Small claims court for fee refunds. Cost: a filing fee. Leverage: direct

For a discrete undisclosed charge, small claims is the fastest refund mechanism and the insurer's cost of defending often exceeds the fee. Flag: Arkansas small claims jurisdictional limits and current filing fees were not verified in this pass. Confirm before building any tool around them.

## Part 3. Overdraft law grounding, for the complaint generator tool

### 3A. The opt in rule is the spine. Regulation E, 12 CFR 1005.17

Since 2010, a bank or credit union may not charge an overdraft fee for paying an ATM withdrawal or a one time debit card purchase unless the customer affirmatively opted in to the overdraft service first, after receiving the required notice and the model disclosure (Form A-9). Consent can be revoked at any time, and the bank must honor the revocation as soon as reasonably practicable, 1005.17(f). Checks, ACH transfers, and recurring debit payments sit outside the opt in rule, which is the gap most surprise fees walk through. The underlying statute never changed, and two later developments must be stated honestly in any tool copy: the CFPB's December 2024 rule capping overdraft fees at the largest banks was disapproved by Congress and repealed, Public Law 119-10, signed May 9, 2025, and the CFPB withdrew its overdraft guidance circulars, including Circular 2022-06 on surprise fees and Circular 2024-05 on opt in documentation, on May 12, 2025. Withdrawn guidance is history, not authority. The opt in regulation itself remains in force.

Sources: https://www.loopholedb.com/us/banking-and-credit/overdraft-opt-out-reg-e and https://www.botminds.ai/solutions/udaap-risk-assessment/cfpb-junk-fees-guidance

### 3B. Error resolution and the private lawsuit. Regulation E, 12 CFR 1005.11, and the EFTA, 15 U.S.C. 1693f and 1693m

A customer who reports an error within 60 days of the statement that shows it triggers a duty to investigate. The bank generally must finish within 10 business days or provisionally credit the account and take up to 45 days, longer in defined cases. If the bank fails to provisionally credit on time and fails to investigate in good faith, treble damages are available under 1693f(e). Any failure to comply with the EFTA supports a private suit under 1693m for actual damages plus statutory damages of $100 to $1,000, plus attorney fees and costs, with a one year deadline to sue. The fee shifting is the point for this project: it makes a $35 fee case carryable, the same way 23-79-208 does on the insurance side.

Source: https://legalclarity.org/do-i-have-to-pay-back-provisional-credit/

### 3C. Surprise fees on transactions that were covered when approved. APSN and the UDAAP statute

Authorize positive, settle negative means the bank approved a debit purchase when the balance covered it, then charged an overdraft fee when the purchase settled after other items posted. The CFPB treated those fees as unfair under the Consumer Financial Protection Act, 12 U.S.C. 5531 and 5536, and the enforcement record is the strongest material in this section. On September 28, 2022, the CFPB ordered Regions Bank to pay $191 million, about $141 million in consumer redress plus a $50 million penalty, for APSN fees the Bureau said the bank knew were unlawful and kept charging while it looked for replacement fee revenue. On December 20, 2022, the CFPB ordered Wells Fargo to pay over $2 billion in consumer redress and a $1.7 billion penalty in an order that included surprise overdraft fees among the violations. The 2022 circular announcing the APSN theory was later withdrawn, as noted above, but consent orders are not guidance. They are adjudicated outcomes, and they define what regulators have already accepted as unfair.

Sources: https://law.justia.com/cases/delaware/court-of-chancery/2025/c-a-no-2023-1284-ksjm.html and https://legalclarity.org/what-was-the-hash-vs-first-financial-bank-case-about/

### 3D. Posting order. Gutierrez v. Wells Fargo and the lesson inside it

Banks once reordered each day's debit transactions from largest to smallest, draining the account early and multiplying fees. In Gutierrez v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A., 704 F.3d 712 (9th Cir. 2012), the trial court found the bank adopted high to low posting to maximize fees and misled customers about it, and ordered $203 million in restitution. The Ninth Circuit held that federal banking law preempted state attempts to regulate the posting order itself, but that fraud and misrepresentation claims were not preempted. On remand the full $203 million was reinstated on the misrepresentation theory and affirmed in 2014. The drafting lesson for every complaint this project generates: plead what the bank said and what the disclosures promised, not only what the bank did. Statements can be policed where mechanics cannot.

Source: https://legalclarity.org/wells-fargo-overdraft-settlement-cases-amounts-and-payouts/

### 3E. State law angles for the generator

File in three directions at once. One, the CFPB complaint portal, which banks answer on a clock and which builds the federal record. Two, the customer's prudential regulator for charter specific issues, OCC for national banks and the FDIC or state banking department otherwise, a routing detail the tool should ask about rather than assume. Three, the state Attorney General under the state UDAP statute. In Arkansas that is the ADTPA as narrowed in Part 1E, so expectations should be set honestly: the AG complaint is a pattern building exercise, while the customer's own money claim usually runs through Regulation E error resolution and, if the bank mishandles it, an EFTA suit under 1693m.

### 3F. What the complaint should allege and request, checklist form

Allege, with dates and amounts pulled from statements:
1. Each fee, the transaction that triggered it, and the balance shown to the customer at authorization and at settlement.
2. Whether the customer ever opted in under 1005.17, and if the bank claims so, a demand for the bank's proof of affirmative consent, its date, and its form.
3. For APSN fees, that the transaction was authorized against a sufficient balance, tracking the Regions order's theory.
4. For posting order issues, the specific disclosure or statement the customer relied on, tracking Gutierrez.
5. Any error report already made, the bank's response dates, and any missed Regulation E deadline, which converts a fee gripe into an EFTA claim with fee shifting.
6. Harm beyond the fee where true: cascading fees, closed accounts, ChexSystems reporting, missed rent or utility payments.

Request:
1. Refund of every identified fee, with the total stated as a number.
2. Written confirmation of opt out or revocation, effective immediately.
3. Correction of any negative reporting to ChexSystems or similar systems caused by the fees.
4. Preservation of records, including authorization logs, posting order rules in effect, and the opt in record.
5. A statement that copies are being filed with the CFPB and the state Attorney General.

2. Whether Arkansas treats installment and billing fees as part of the rate that must be filed.

4. Exact current day counts in AID Rule 43 and the binding status of the appraisal characterization in Lever 6.
