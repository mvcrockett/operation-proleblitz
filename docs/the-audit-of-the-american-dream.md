## A note on method

This audit began as one woman's ledger of grievances against the American social contract, 108 titled items written from lived experience. It now stands at 500 items by the author's count: the originals, rewritten and fact-checked in their original order, plus 350 new items built the same way.

Every load-bearing claim in this book, every headline statistic, every dollar figure, every named study, agency, law, and year, has been checked against public sources. A final fact check sweep in November 2026 checked 1,131 claims across all 500 items. 1,070 verified. 45 were corrected, and every correction is recorded in an open log that travels with this work, showing the text before and after and the source relied on. 16 claims could not be verified. They stay in the text where the author put them, flagged in the log, never silently presented as settled fact.

That is the deal this book makes with its reader. The argument is the author's, and it does not flinch. The numbers underneath it have been made to earn their place.

This is a draft. It has not been published or submitted anywhere.

## PART 1: HEALTHCARE & INSURANCE (1–15)





### 1. Insulin Price Gouging

In the United States, the average manufacturer price of insulin is $98.70 per standard unit. In Canada it is $12. In the United Kingdom it is $7.52, and the patient pays nothing at the point of care. Those figures come from a RAND Corporation study prepared for the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services using 2018 prices across 33 countries. The same medicine, made by the same companies, costs more than ten times as much here as the average price in the rest of the developed world. The gap is not based on medical innovation or production cost. It is built on patent gaming, market exclusivity, and lobbying power.



Insulin was discovered over a century ago. In 1923 its discoverers sold the patent to the University of Toronto for $1 each, because they believed no one should profit from a drug that keeps people alive. Today, small molecular tweaks let manufacturers win fresh patents on updated versions and keep generic competitors off the market. Three corporations, Eli Lilly, Novo Nordisk, and Sanofi, control over 90 percent of the global insulin market and dominate pricing in the United States.



The predictable result is rationing. A 2022 study in the Annals of Internal Medicine, using federal survey data from 2021, found that 16.5 percent of American insulin users, about 1.3 million adults, rationed their insulin because of cost. They skipped doses, took less than prescribed, or delayed buying more. Rationing was worst among the uninsured, at 29 percent, but it was common even among people with private insurance. Rationed insulin leads to kidney failure, amputations, blindness, strokes, and death, all of it preventable, all of it billed back to the public through emergency rooms and disability rolls. The American Diabetes Association put the total cost of diagnosed diabetes at $327 billion in 2017 alone, $237 billion in direct medical care and $90 billion in lost productivity.



Pressure finally moved the edges of the problem. The Inflation Reduction Act capped insulin copays at $35 a month for people on Medicare, more than half the states have passed caps of their own, and in 2023 the three big manufacturers announced deep list price cuts under public pressure. None of that changes the underlying design. The caps protect some patients, some of the time, while the companies keep the power to set the underlying price, and the pharmaceutical industry still spends more than $300 million a year on lobbying to protect that power.



Conclusion:

The insulin pricing crisis shows how economic policy, corporate lobbying, and healthcare design converge to produce avoidable loss of life and substantial economic waste. It is not merely a health issue. It is a macroeconomic and moral failing. When a nation lets monopoly profits override access to a century-old medicine that its inventors sold for a dollar, the suffering that follows is not an accident of the market. It is a policy choice, renewed every year, and it can be unmade the same way.





### 2. Medical Bankruptcy

In the United States, medical debt remains the leading cause of personal bankruptcy. A 2019 study in the American Journal of Public Health found that 66.5 percent of all bankruptcies are tied to medical expenses, whether through the cost of treatment itself or the income lost while sick. That study estimated roughly 530,000 families a year file for bankruptcy because of medical bills or illness. Crucially, this holds true even for people who had health insurance when they got sick.



Insurance provides only partial protection. High deductibles, copays, out-of-network charges, denied claims, and surprise bills can overwhelm a stable middle-income family in a single hospital stay. For people with chronic illness or disability, the costs stack up year after year until the math no longer works. Nationally, the bill is enormous. An analysis by the Peterson Center on Healthcare and KFF, using Census Bureau data, put the total medical debt held by American adults at $195 billion. Research has also linked bankruptcy following a cancer diagnosis to significantly higher mortality, because financial ruin interrupts treatment and grinds patients down.



The damage spreads outward. Families in medical bankruptcy drain savings, cut spending, lose homes, and delay care, which worsens their health and raises the eventual public cost. Nations with universal healthcare systems, including Canada, France, and the United Kingdom, report virtually no medical bankruptcy at all. That comparison settles the argument about whether this is inevitable. It is not. It is a design choice.



Conclusion:

Medical bankruptcy in the United States is both a public health failure and an economic inefficiency. It shortens lives, deepens poverty, and weakens national productivity through avoidable financial trauma. No functioning democracy can call itself solvent while its healthcare system makes bankruptcy the price of surviving an illness.





### 3. Private Insurance Denials

In the United States, coverage does not guarantee care. Private insurers routinely deny medically necessary treatment, medication, and procedures that a patient's own doctor has ordered. KFF's analysis of federal transparency data found that Marketplace insurers denied 17 percent of in-network claims in 2021, and the rate rose to 19 percent in 2024, when those insurers denied about 85 million in-network claims in a single year. Denial rates vary wildly by company, from 3 percent to 36 percent, which tells you the denials are not about medicine. They are about business models.



The denials do not stop at paperwork. In March 2023, ProPublica revealed that Cigna had built a system, known internally as PXDX, that let its medical directors deny claims in bulk without opening patient files, spending an average of 1.2 seconds on each review. Other reporting has shown insurers using algorithms to cut off rehabilitation and home care for elderly patients. The gatekeeper to care is increasingly not a doctor who has examined you. It is a cost-optimization program that has never seen your name.



The system counts on patients giving up. KFF found that consumers appealed less than two-tenths of 1 percent of denied in-network claims in 2021, and fewer than 1 percent in 2024. Appeals are confusing, slow, and exhausting by design, and even when patients fight, insurers uphold their own denials about two thirds of the time. Every unappealed denial is pure profit, care the insurer was paid to cover and never had to deliver.



Conclusion:

Private insurance denials expose the central fraud of the American model. People pay premiums for years believing they have bought protection, and discover at the sickest moment of their lives that they bought a lottery ticket instead. A system that profits by refusing care is not a healthcare system with flaws. Refusing care is the system, working exactly as it was built to work.

### 4. Mental Health Parity Violations

Federal law has required equal treatment of mental and physical health in insurance plans since the Mental Health Parity and Addiction Equity Act of 2008. On paper, an insurer cannot impose stricter limits on therapy or addiction treatment than it imposes on comparable medical care. In practice, parity remains largely aspirational, because enforcement is weak and noncompliance is the industry norm.



Insurers skirt the law through what regulators call non-quantitative treatment limitations. They require prior authorization for psychiatric care but not for comparable medical care. They reimburse therapists so poorly that providers leave the networks, leaving patients with ghost directories of doctors who are not taking patients. They deny residential addiction treatment that has no real equivalent on the medical side. When the Departments of Labor, Health and Human Services, and the Treasury issued their 2022 parity report to Congress, they found that none of the comparative analyses plans initially submitted to justify these limits were sufficient. Not some. None.



The human ledger is brutal. More than half of American adults with a mental illness receive no treatment at all, and about 60 percent of young people with major depression get no care. The average delay between the first symptoms of mental illness and the first treatment is 11 years. Suicide kills more than 48,000 Americans a year and is the second leading cause of death for people ages 10 to 34. People with serious mental illness die 10 to 25 years earlier than everyone else, mostly from treatable medical conditions nobody coordinated care for.



The economic ledger is just as bad. Economic analyses put the annual burden of untreated mental illness in the United States at roughly $280 billion, counting medical costs, lost productivity, disability, and criminal justice involvement. Depression alone has been estimated to cost employers $44 billion a year in lost productive time. Meanwhile the Department of Labor, the main parity enforcer for employer plans, has historically lacked even the authority to fine insurers for violations, which means the penalty for breaking the law is being asked to comply with it.



Conclusion:

Mental health parity violations are a convergence of regulatory failure, economic waste, and avoidable death. The law has promised equal treatment for over fifteen years. What it has delivered is a two-tier system where a broken leg gets a network and a broken mind gets a waiting list. In a nation where half of all adults will experience a diagnosable mental illness in their lifetime, parity is not an option. It is a prerequisite for a functioning country, and the government has failed to enforce its own promise.





### 5. Surprise Medical Billing

A surprise medical bill lands when a patient does everything right. They check that the hospital is in their network, they go there for care, and then a bill arrives from an anesthesiologist, radiologist, or emergency physician who was out of network without the patient's knowledge or consent. Before federal reform, research found that roughly one in five emergency room visits involved an out-of-network provider at an in-network facility, and KFF polling found that about two thirds of Americans worried about being able to afford an unexpected medical bill, more than any other household expense.



The practice was not an accident. For-profit hospital systems outsourced their emergency and anesthesia departments to private staffing firms that deliberately stayed out of insurance networks, because out-of-network status meant billing leverage. The patient, unconscious or bleeding, was the leverage.



Congress finally acted with the No Surprises Act, signed in December 2020 and in effect since January 1, 2022. The law banned balance billing for emergency care and for out-of-network providers at in-network facilities, and it covered air ambulances. But Congress left ground ambulances out of the law entirely, and KFF has found that about half of emergency ground ambulance rides for privately insured patients still produce an out-of-network charge. The ride you cannot refuse, in the vehicle you cannot choose, remains unprotected. Enforcement gaps and low public awareness leave other patients exposed as well, and every surprise bill feeds the $195 billion pool of American medical debt, pushing families toward credit cards, emptied savings, and bankruptcy court.



Conclusion:

Surprise medical billing is not a side effect of the healthcare system. It is a feature of a fragmented, profit-driven structure that bills people for choices they never made. The No Surprises Act proved the practice could be outlawed when Congress chose to act, and the ground ambulance loophole proves just as clearly whose interests still come first. A functioning healthcare system gives patients predictability and consent. This one gives them a bill for the ambulance and calls it freedom.

### 7. Employer-Dependent Insurance

In the United States, health insurance is tied to employment. More than 150 million Americans get their coverage through a job, their own or a family member's. The arrangement began as a workaround for World War II wage controls, when employers competed for workers with benefits instead of pay. It never ended. It hardened into a system where access to medical care depends not on medical need but on job status.



The flaw shows itself at the worst possible moment. Lose the job and you lose the coverage, often immediately. An analysis published in Health Affairs estimated that 5.4 million workers lost employer-sponsored insurance in the first months of the COVID-19 pandemic alone, at the exact moment a virus was filling hospitals. COBRA, the federal stopgap, lets the newly unemployed keep their old plan if they pay the entire premium themselves plus a fee, hundreds of dollars a month demanded from people who just lost their income. Most cannot. As of 2023, the Census Bureau counted about 26 million Americans with no insurance at all, many of them between jobs, working part time, or working gigs that offer nothing.



The system also traps the people it covers. Workers stay in jobs they hate because a child's medication or a spouse's treatment depends on the plan, a drag economists call job lock. It suppresses new business formation, because quitting to start something means gambling your family's health. And the uninsured delay care until conditions become emergencies, which is the most expensive and deadliest way to deliver medicine. A widely cited 2009 Harvard Medical School study estimated that lacking insurance was associated with about 45,000 preventable deaths a year, and American Cancer Society research has repeatedly found that uninsured patients are diagnosed later and die more often from treatable cancers.



Conclusion:

Employer-dependent insurance is a structural relic that no longer fits an economy of job changes, contract work, and automation. It ties survival to employment, fails exactly when the economy fails, and punishes anyone whose life does not fit the shape of a full-time job with benefits. A person's access to a doctor should depend on the fact that they are a person. Everything else is an accident of history we keep choosing to keep.





### 8. Medicaid Gap

The Medicaid coverage gap is one of the cruelest loopholes in American law. The Affordable Care Act assumed every state would expand Medicaid to cover adults earning up to 138 percent of the federal poverty line. In 2012, the Supreme Court's ruling in NFIB v. Sebelius made expansion optional. Ten states still refuse. In those states, millions of poor adults earn too much to qualify for traditional Medicaid and too little to qualify for subsidies to buy private insurance. They are not denied help because they failed some test of worthiness. They are denied because of their zip code.



KFF estimates that roughly 1.5 million uninsured adults sit in the coverage gap, most of them in Texas, Florida, and Georgia. Many earn only a few thousand dollars a year in jobs like food service, caregiving, and construction, the work every community depends on. About six in ten of them are people of color, which means a policy sold as fiscal restraint lands with surgical precision on Black and brown workers in the South.



The consequences are measurable in lives. A large body of research finds that Medicaid expansion significantly reduced mortality among low-income adults, prevented medical debt, and kept rural hospitals alive, while nonexpansion states absorbed higher uncompensated care costs that get passed to taxpayers and insured patients anyway. The refusal does not even save money. It moves the cost from a budget line to an emergency room, a bankruptcy filing, and a funeral.



Conclusion:

The Medicaid gap is not a policy oversight. It is a deliberate choice by state governments, renewed every legislative session, and it produces avoidable death, deepened poverty, and suppressed local economies. In a wealthy nation, whether a poor person can see a doctor should not depend on which side of a state line they live on. The gap is a violation of the social contract written in the plainest possible terms, and everyone involved knows it.





### 9. Dental Care Exclusion

In the United States, the mouth is legally separate from the body. Traditional Medicare covers no routine dental care at all. Many state Medicaid programs cover little or nothing for adults. Private plans treat teeth as an add-on luxury. The result, according to the CareQuest Institute, is that more than 76 million American adults have no dental insurance, in the richest healthcare system on Earth.



The body does not recognize the distinction the law invented. The CDC reports that more than 1 in 5 adults has untreated tooth decay and nearly half of adults over 30 have some form of gum disease. Untreated infections spread to the heart and the brain. The American Dental Association estimates about 2 million emergency room visits a year are for dental conditions, at a cost of billions, and the ER can offer antibiotics and painkillers but almost never the extraction or filling that would solve the problem. The patient is sent home to get reinfected on schedule.



In 2007, a twelve-year-old Maryland boy named Deamonte Driver died after bacteria from an abscessed tooth spread to his brain. An $80 extraction would likely have saved him. His family had lost their Medicaid coverage in a paperwork shuffle, and finding a dentist who would take what coverage they had proved impossible until it was far too late. His name belongs in this audit because his death was not a medical mystery. It was an administrative outcome.



The economic damage runs alongside the human damage. The CDC estimates that untreated oral disease costs the country more than $45 billion a year in lost productivity, and the Surgeon General has reported 164 million work hours lost annually to dental problems and visits that come too late. People in pain cannot work, cannot eat properly, cannot sleep, and learn to hide their smiles in job interviews.



Conclusion:

The exclusion of dental care from mainstream health coverage is an artificial divide with deadly consequences. Teeth are not cosmetic accessories. They are part of the body, connected to its blood supply and its survival, and a system that treats them as optional will keep producing preventable infections, preventable poverty, and preventable deaths like Deamonte Driver's. The divide exists because it is profitable and traditional, and neither of those is a medical reason.

### 10. Lack of Preventative Coverage

Preventive care is the cheapest medicine there is. A screening catches cancer when it is a procedure instead of a death sentence, and a blood pressure check costs a tiny fraction of the stroke it prevents. Yet the United States runs its healthcare system backward, paying lavishly for catastrophe and stingily for prevention. The country spends more than $4 trillion a year on healthcare, and only about 3 percent of it goes to public health and prevention.



The gaps are not subtle. The CDC reports that about 1 in 3 adults in the screening age range are not up to date on colorectal cancer screening, even though colorectal cancer is one of the leading causes of cancer death and one of the most preventable. Uninsured women are screened for breast and cervical cancer at far lower rates than insured women, so their cancers are found later, when treatment is harsher and survival is worse. The CDC has estimated that more than 100,000 lives could be saved every year if everyone in America received the recommended preventive care. That is the size of the failure, counted in funerals.



The economics are as damning as the medicine. The CDC notes that chronic and mental health conditions account for 90 percent of the nation's $4.9 trillion in annual healthcare spending, and many of those conditions are preventable or manageable when caught early. Instead, high deductibles punish people for using the coverage they nominally have, narrow networks and provider shortages put clinics out of reach, and hourly workers cannot take unpaid time off for a mammogram. The system waits until the patient is expensive, then expresses surprise at the bill.



Conclusion:

The lack of comprehensive preventive care in the United States is both a public health failure and a refusal to do simple arithmetic. It produces avoidable deaths, avoidable disability, and runaway costs that land on families, employers, and taxpayers. Early intervention is not charity. It is cost containment, lifesaving policy, and fiscal common sense, and a system that spends 97 percent of its money after people get sick has chosen the most expensive possible way to fail them.





### 12. Disabled People Denied Care

The Americans with Disabilities Act is more than three decades old, and disabled Americans are still routinely denied medical care. The National Council on Disability has documented discrimination across the healthcare system, in transplant decisions, in crisis standards of care, and in ordinary offices that lack an accessible exam table or a scale a wheelchair user can use. One in four American adults lives with a disability, according to the CDC. This is not a marginal population being failed at the margins. It is a quarter of the country.



The attitudes inside the profession are part of the problem. A 2021 study in Health Affairs found that 82 percent of physicians surveyed believed people with significant disability have a worse quality of life than nondisabled people, and only about 41 percent were very confident they could provide the same quality of care to a disabled patient. Those assumptions turn lethal at decision points. The National Council on Disability's work on organ transplantation documented patients with intellectual and developmental disabilities being denied transplant evaluations based on stereotypes about their worth and their ability to follow treatment plans, even with caregivers ready to help. During the COVID-19 pandemic, several states' crisis standards of care openly deprioritized disabled people for ventilators until federal civil rights complaints forced revisions.



The pattern shows up in outcomes. Disabled adults die at higher rates from preventable conditions, not because their disabilities are untreatable but because screening is skipped, symptoms are dismissed as part of the disability, and referrals never happen. Every missed cancer screening and waved-off complaint compounds into the early deaths the statistics record.



Conclusion:

Denying care to disabled Americans is a violation of civil rights, medical ethics, and economic logic. It produces early death and forced dependence, not because of disability itself but because of how the system chooses to respond to it. In a country that claims to value life and equality, the medical abandonment of disabled people is not rare malpractice. It is a chronic structural failure, and it undermines both justice and solvency at once.





### 13. Life-Saving Drug Patents Block Generics

A drug patent is supposed to last 20 years. In the American pharmaceutical market, that number is a floor, not a ceiling. Companies build what critics call patent thickets around successful drugs, filing dozens or hundreds of additional patents on minor variations, delivery devices, and formulations, then daring generic competitors to litigate their way through. The strategy is known as evergreening, and its purpose is not innovation. It is monopoly preservation.



The case study is Humira, for years the best-selling drug in the world. Its manufacturer, AbbVie, filed more than 250 patents around it. Europe got competing biosimilars in 2018. The United States, fenced in by litigation and settlements, did not get them until 2023, five years of extra monopoly pricing extracted from American patients and insurers. Research on the industry has found that most patents filed on top-selling drugs protect medicines that already exist rather than new inventions. Meanwhile RAND has documented that brand-name drug prices in the United States average about 2.5 times what other high-income countries pay.



Patients absorb the cost in their bodies. KFF polling has found that about 3 in 10 American adults report not taking their medication as prescribed because of cost, skipping doses or splitting pills. For insulin, cancer drugs, HIV treatment, and hepatitis C cures, interruption is not an inconvenience. It is disease progression and death, purchased at monopoly prices. Medication nonadherence driven by cost also boomerangs into hospitalizations and emergency care that analysts estimate cost the system $100 billion or more every year.



Conclusion:

Patent abuse in the pharmaceutical industry is a breakdown of regulatory integrity. The system was built to reward invention, and it has been captured to reward obstruction instead. If a life can be saved for $5 and we charge $500, the system is not broken. It is complicit. Blocking generics for profit is not an intellectual property dispute. It is a matter of avoidable death, and the country pays for it twice, once at the pharmacy counter and once at the graveside.





### 14. Inaccessible Medical Devices

A hearing aid is not a luxury. Neither is a wheelchair, a prosthetic leg, or a CPAP machine. These devices are the difference between working and not working, between independence and institutional care, between participating in life and watching it. In the United States they are priced and rationed like luxury goods.



Start with hearing. Federal law excludes hearing aids from traditional Medicare coverage by statute, and a pair bought privately has traditionally cost between $3,000 and $6,000. About 48 million Americans have hearing loss, yet only about 1 in 5 who could benefit from hearing aids actually use them, with cost the leading barrier. Untreated hearing loss is linked by the Lancet Commission on dementia to higher risks of dementia, depression, falls, and hospitalization. The FDA's 2022 decision to allow over-the-counter hearing aids helped at the entry level, but the coverage wall for prescription devices still stands. Prosthetic limbs run from $5,000 to $50,000 and wear out every few years, while some insurers impose limits as tight as one device per lifetime, as if limbs were not replaced because bodies change. Medicare will help pay for a power wheelchair only if it is needed for use inside the home, a rule that strands people at their own front doors. CPAP machines cost $500 to $1,000 and come wrapped in compliance rules that let suppliers take them back from the patients who struggle most.



The downstream costs dwarf the device prices. The CDC puts the annual medical cost of older adult falls at about $50 billion, much of it preventable with proper mobility support. People denied devices end up in emergency rooms, in nursing facilities, and out of the workforce, generating public costs many times what the equipment would have cost.



Conclusion:

The exclusion and underfunding of medical devices is not a gap in insurance. It is a denial of dignity, autonomy, and human potential. These tools are the foundation of independent living, and a nation that claims to value independence cannot keep treating the ability to move, hear, and breathe as privileges for people who can pay cash. Freedom means very little to a person who cannot get out of their own house.

## PART 2: HOUSING & HOMELESSNESS (16–30)





### 16. Housing as a Commodity

In the United States, housing is treated less as a basic human need than as a speculative asset class. Real estate is a primary vehicle for wealth building, retirement security, and institutional investment, and housing policy has been written to serve that function first. The result is a market organized around investor returns, exclusionary zoning, and artificial scarcity, while the human need for shelter comes second, if it is considered at all.



The numbers tell the story of a rigged decade. Home prices rose more than 40 percent between 2020 and 2022 while wages barely moved, and in the most expensive metro areas the median home now costs seven or more times the median income. Institutional investors own a single-digit share of single-family rentals nationally, roughly 3 percent by the Government Accountability Office's count, but they concentrate their buying in specific Sun Belt and suburban markets, where they can dominate whole neighborhoods and set the rent for everyone else. At the same time, luxury apartments sit empty as investments while working families sleep in cars, because the market produces what is profitable rather than what is needed.



The health consequences are documented and severe. Research links housing instability to higher mortality, worse management of chronic disease, and developmental harm to children. People who experience homelessness die decades earlier than the housed population, with average life expectancy around 50, cut down by exposure, untreated illness, and infections that a stable address would have made manageable. Studies of chronically homeless individuals estimate $30,000 to $50,000 a year in emergency room visits, jail stays, and institutional care per person, which is more than the supportive housing that would end the cycle costs.



Conclusion:

When housing is treated as a speculative game, people become collateral. Millions are forced into overcrowding, precarity, and homelessness not because shelter does not exist but because it is unaffordable by design. This is not a market failure. It is a policy choice. A nation where shelter is auctioned to the highest bidder cannot claim to guarantee life, liberty, or the pursuit of happiness, and restoring the American Dream starts with admitting that housing is a foundation for health and dignity before it is an investment.





### 17. Corporate Landlords

Some of the largest landlords in America are no longer people. They are investment firms. After the 2008 foreclosure crisis, when government-backed entities sold off foreclosed homes in bulk at a discount, companies like Blackstone, Invitation Homes, and Pretium Partners bought single-family houses by the tens of thousands, concentrating in Sun Belt cities like Atlanta, Phoenix, and Charlotte. The Government Accountability Office counted roughly 450,000 single-family rental homes in institutional hands by 2022, and in the hottest markets investors accounted for a quarter or more of all home purchases, bidding against families with cash the families could not match.



The business model is extraction. A 2022 investigation by the House Financial Services Committee into the largest corporate landlords documented steep rent increases, layers of junk fees, and aggressive eviction filings, along with maintenance operations that left tenants living with mold, pests, and broken systems while service requests disappeared into call centers. Then came the algorithms. In 2024 the Justice Department sued the property software company RealPage, alleging that landlords used its pricing software to coordinate rent increases across supposedly competing properties. Rent was no longer set by a local market. It was set by a shared machine, and the machine only moves in one direction.



Every dollar of that extraction comes out of a working family's budget and out of the wealth they will never build. A renter paying an algorithm's rent is not accumulating equity, not stabilizing a school enrollment, not putting down roots. They are funding dividends for investors who will never drive down their street.



Conclusion:

Corporate landlords have transformed housing into a high-frequency extraction economy, where a home is a line on a profit and loss statement and a family is a revenue stream. A stable society cannot exist where whole neighborhoods are owned by absentee firms with no stake in the community's survival. The American Dream was never meant to be leased back to the people who built it, one algorithmic rent hike at a time.





### 18. Homelessness Criminalization

Rather than house people, American cities increasingly arrest them. Laws against sleeping in public, camping, loitering, and panhandling have spread across the country, with the National Homelessness Law Center documenting camping bans in well over 100 cities. Encampment sweeps destroy tents, medications, identification documents, and the fragile networks people build to survive, then order the survivors to move along to nowhere in particular.



In June 2024, the Supreme Court removed the last major legal obstacle. In City of Grants Pass v. Johnson, the Court ruled that cities may enforce bans on sleeping outside even when no shelter beds are available, rejecting the argument that punishing a person for sleeping when they have nowhere to sleep is cruel and unusual. The ruling handed every city council in America a permission slip, and many used it within the year.



The policy fails on its own terms. Criminalization does not reduce homelessness. It cycles people through jails, courts, and emergency rooms at public expense, while fines and arrest records make it harder to qualify for the housing and jobs that would end the homelessness. People experiencing homelessness already die decades early, with an average life expectancy around 50, and sweeps make it worse by scattering people away from outreach workers, clinics, and each other. Study after study finds that permanent supportive housing costs the public less than the emergency services, jail time, and hospital visits it replaces. Punishment is the expensive option. It is chosen anyway, because it moves the visible evidence of failure out of sight.



Conclusion:

Criminalizing homelessness is not a policy. It is an abdication, one that prioritizes appearances over outcomes and optics over lives. No one chooses to sleep under a bridge, and no one should be punished for surviving there. A functional society does not penalize survival. It provides shelter, care, and dignity, and it does not need a Supreme Court ruling to know the difference.





### 19. Section 8 Waiting Lists

The Housing Choice Voucher program, known as Section 8, is the federal government's main tool for helping low-income families, seniors, and disabled people afford rent. It works when people can get it. The scandal is how few can. According to the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities, only about 1 in 4 households eligible for federal rental assistance actually receives any, because Congress funds the program as a ration rather than a guarantee. The rest join waiting lists measured in years, when the lists are open at all.



The scale of the rationing is hard to overstate. Many housing authorities keep their lists closed for years at a stretch because the backlog is already hopeless. When New York City reopened its Section 8 waiting list in June 2024 for the first time in fifteen years, more than 600,000 applications poured in within a week, competing for 200,000 spots on the list itself, not for apartments, just for a place in line. Even the lucky few who receive a voucher then discover that landlords in many states may legally refuse it, and that the voucher's value often cannot reach the rents in neighborhoods with good schools and safe streets.



While families wait, they wait in overcrowded units, in unsafe housing, in shelters, in cars. Children change schools, asthma worsens in moldy apartments, and seniors choose between rent and medication. The public pays for all of it anyway, through emergency rooms, shelters, and law enforcement, at rates that exceed what the housing would have cost.



Conclusion:

The Section 8 program is not broken because it fails to work. It is broken because the country refuses to fund it at the scale of the need it was built to meet. Denying vouchers to eligible families does not save money. It transfers the cost to emergency rooms, shelters, police, and the next generation. A nation's solvency should not be judged by the wealth of its billionaires but by how long its poorest citizens must wait for a roof. A waiting list measured in decades is not a backlog. It is a breach of contract.

### 21. Rent Inflation Without Wage Growth

The core transaction of American working life no longer clears. Median gross rent has climbed roughly 70 percent since 2000, while typical wages, adjusted for inflation, grew by less than a fifth over the same period. Harvard's Joint Center for Housing Studies reported that in 2022 half of all renter households in the country, a record 22.4 million of them, spent more than 30 percent of their income on rent and utilities, and 12.1 million spent more than half. Half is not a housing budget. It is a slow-motion emergency.



The National Low Income Housing Coalition's Out of Reach report calculates that a full-time worker needs to earn $33.63 an hour to afford a modest two-bedroom apartment at fair market rent without being cost-burdened. The federal minimum wage is $7.25. Between those two numbers live the nurses' aides, teachers, paramedics, cooks, and cashiers who keep every town in America running and who cannot afford to live in the towns they serve. They commute farther, double up with relatives, skip medication, and fall one car repair away from eviction at all times.



The damage is not just financial. Rent burden drives utility shutoffs, food insecurity, and the chronic stress that physicians link to hypertension, depression, and stroke. Children change schools midyear when families are displaced, losing months of learning and every adult connection they had built. Employers in high-cost regions cannot hire, because the workers they need cannot live within reach of the job. The Federal Reserve itself has flagged housing unaffordability as a drag on labor mobility and regional economies.



Conclusion:

The American Dream was built on the idea that hard work could earn you a roof over your head. In today's economy the rent rises faster than the ladder. Housing has become a structural trap that keeps working families one missed paycheck from crisis, and a society that treats shelter as a luxury to be bid on will eventually find itself bankrupt in both wealth and morality. This is not just unjust. It is unsustainable, and everyone doing the math honestly knows it.





### 25. Veterans Left Homeless

The United States made a covenant with the people it sent to war. It is breaking that covenant on the street. HUD's 2023 point-in-time count found 35,574 veterans homeless on a single night. The 2024 count improved to 32,882, the lowest since counting began, which matters for two reasons. It proves the problem can be moved when money and coordination show up, and it strips away every excuse for the tens of thousands still outside.



The causes are documented and none of them are mysterious. Combat trauma and PTSD, physical disability, traumatic brain injury, substance use disorders that began as self-medication, and a benefits bureaucracy so dense that eligible veterans give up before the paperwork clears. An estimated 1.5 million veterans live at risk of homelessness, one rent spike or one denied claim away. Female veterans, including survivors of military sexual trauma, are among the fastest-growing groups in the homeless veteran population and among the least served by a system designed around single men.



The VA's own data count an average of about 17 veteran suicides a day. Homeless veterans cycle through emergency rooms, jails, and shelters at public costs estimated at $30,000 to $50,000 per person per year, the most expensive possible way to abandon someone, while the supportive housing that actually works costs less and is chronically underfunded. Every year of delay compounds the damage, because chronic homelessness worsens every condition these veterans carried home.



Conclusion:

No nation that sends its citizens to war should leave them sleeping in alleys and under bridges. Veteran homelessness is not a tragedy of fate. It is a policy failure with a body count, produced by neglect and fiscal apathy rather than malice, which makes it worse, not better. The 2024 numbers prove the country can house veterans when it decides to. The covenant does not expire, and neither does the debt.





### 26. Housing Assistance Ignores Inflation

Federal housing assistance runs on 1980s math. The rule that a household should spend no more than 30 percent of its income on rent dates to 1981, and the machinery built around it has not kept pace with the market it operates in. HUD's Fair Market Rents, which set how much a voucher can pay, are built from survey data that trail actual rents, sometimes by years. In a market where rents jumped by double digits in a single year, a voucher calculated from old data is a coupon for an apartment that no longer exists at that price.



The consequences play out quietly. Families who survive the waiting list and finally receive a voucher then have a limited window, often 60 to 120 days, to find a unit that passes inspection, rents at or below the payment standard, and has a landlord willing to participate. In tight markets a large share of them fail and hand the voucher back unused, not because housing help does not work but because the help was denominated in yesterday's dollars. Those who succeed often remain confined to the poorest neighborhoods, because the voucher cannot reach anywhere else, which concentrates poverty and calls it choice.



Meanwhile the gap compounds. Every year that payment standards lag the market, the same federal dollar buys less housing, landlords exit the program, and the families the program exists for absorb the difference in overcrowding, substandard conditions, or homelessness. The public then pays the inflated emergency costs documented throughout this audit, in shelters, hospitals, and courts, and pretends the savings were real.



Conclusion:

We cannot fight twenty-first-century housing insecurity with twentieth-century formulas. When assistance programs ignore real-time market conditions, they become performative bureaucracy, promises printed on paper that cannot shelter anyone. Updating payment standards to track actual rents is not a technical nicety. It is the difference between a housing program and a housing story the government tells itself, and it is a moral and fiscal imperative.





### 29. Public Housing Disrepair

Public housing is the government's own landlord operation, home to more than a million households, and the government is a slumlord. HUD has estimated the backlog of needed capital repairs across the public housing stock at more than $70 billion, a figure that grows every year Congress underfunds it. The New York City Housing Authority alone, the nation's largest, put its twenty-year capital needs at more than $78 billion in its 2023 assessment. Behind those numbers are the conditions residents live with daily: mold, lead paint, broken boilers in January, elevators that strand disabled tenants on upper floors, sewage backups, and repair requests that age into folklore.



The health consequences are the predictable output of neglect. Children in deteriorating units breathe mold and pest allergens that drive asthma, the leading chronic disease of childhood. Lead exposure steals cognitive potential that never comes back. Elderly and disabled residents are trapped when elevators fail, and families heat apartments with ovens and space heaters when boilers die, which is how fires start. In January 2022, a fire in a Bronx affordable housing building killed 17 people, including 8 children, after a malfunctioning space heater filled the building with smoke through doors that failed to close. That building was privately owned, but the conditions that produced the fire, deferred maintenance and improvised heating, are the daily reality across underfunded assisted housing.



Residents have little recourse. Complaining can bring retaliation or nothing at all, and there is nowhere else to go, because the waiting lists documented in this audit stand between every tenant and every alternative. Deferred maintenance is also fiscal malpractice, since every small repair ignored becomes a structural crisis billed at emergency prices, and every unit lost to decay is a unit the public must replace at full cost.



Conclusion:

Public housing was meant to be a safety net, not a trap. The federal government chose to build housing for the poor and then chose, year after year, not to maintain it, which is a slower way of demolishing it. We are already paying for this neglect in emergency services, health crises, and human suffering. The only question is whether we pay to fix the buildings or keep paying more, in money and in lives, to watch them collapse brick by brick.

## PART 3: FOOD INSECURITY (31–45)





### 32. Food Stamp Restrictions

The Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program serves more than 41 million Americans, and it comes wrapped in rules written for a world that no longer exists. Federal law bars SNAP benefits from buying hot or prepared food, so the rotisserie chicken sitting under a heat lamp, often the cheapest cooked protein in the store, is forbidden, while the raw chicken beside it is allowed. SNAP also cannot buy diapers, soap, toothpaste, menstrual products, cleaning supplies, or toilet paper, because the program pretends that food is the only necessity poverty produces.



The cruelty concentrates on the people least able to work around it. A homeless person has no kitchen, no stove, and no refrigerator, so a benefit that can only buy ingredients is a benefit designed to be unusable, softened only slightly by the small Restaurant Meals Program that a handful of states run for elderly, disabled, and homeless recipients. A parent working two jobs is told to cook from scratch every night or be judged. And the hygiene gap is its own health crisis. The National Diaper Bank Network found in 2023 that nearly half of American families with young children struggle to afford enough diapers, and parents without them report reusing soiled diapers, missing work because childcare requires a diaper supply, and watching their babies suffer rashes and infections that a few dollars would prevent.



The costs do not vanish because the program refuses to see them. They reappear in Medicaid bills for infections and dental disease, in missed workdays, and in the developmental damage of children raised in households forced to choose between soap and supper.



Conclusion:

A nutrition program that denies hot food to the homeless and toothpaste to toddlers is not a modern solution. It is a bureaucratic relic. SNAP's restrictions do not protect taxpayers. They endanger health, waste public money downstream, and grind down the dignity of the families the program claims to serve. Meeting basic human needs should not be controversial, and a policy that cannot tell the difference between nourishment and everything nourishment requires has failed at arithmetic and at mercy alike.





### 34. School Lunch Shaming

In the richest country in the world, children are punished at the lunch line for their parents' poverty. School Nutrition Association surveys have found that about three quarters of school districts carry unpaid student meal debt, and the collection tactics have made national news for years: trays taken from children and thrown in the trash in front of their classmates, stamps on hands, wristbands, substitute meals of a plain cheese sandwich, and in one infamous 2019 case a Pennsylvania district warning parents that unpaid lunch bills could land their children in foster care. The debt in question is often less than the cost of the letters sent to collect it.



The pandemic briefly proved another way was possible. From 2020 to 2022 the federal government funded free meals for every student, no forms, no tiers, no debt, and hunger and stigma fell together. Then Congress let the waivers expire and restored the means-tested maze. A growing list of states, beginning with California and Maine and now including Minnesota, Colorado, New Mexico, Vermont, Massachusetts, and Michigan, have refused to go back and made school meals free for all students permanently with state money. Their cafeterias report the obvious: when every child eats, no child is marked.



Hungry children cannot learn at the level fed children can, and the research connecting childhood food insecurity to worse health, worse behavior, and worse academic outcomes is deep and consistent. Researchers have estimated the total economic cost of hunger in America at well over $160 billion a year in healthcare spending and lost productivity, a figure that dwarfs the meal debt districts hire collectors to chase.



Conclusion:

We do not deny prisoners meals, yet we allow children to be singled out and shamed for being poor. School lunch shaming is a barbaric relic of a system that values debt collection over child welfare, and the states that abolished it have demonstrated that the cruelty was always a choice. If a nation's future is its children, then feeding them without stigma is the bare minimum contract of civilization.





### 35. Child Labor in Food Processing Plants

Federal law bans children from hazardous work. America's slaughterhouses did not get the message, or got it and calculated the fine. In February 2023, the Department of Labor announced that Packers Sanitation Services, a contractor cleaning meatpacking plants for some of the biggest names in food, had employed at least 102 children, ages 13 to 17, to clean slaughterhouse equipment overnight in 13 plants across 8 states. The children worked with caustic chemicals and dangerous machinery, including bone saws and brisket shears. Some were burned by the chemicals. The company paid $1.5 million in penalties, a rounding error against its revenues, and the plants it served largely pointed at the contractor.



Investigative reporting by the New York Times in 2023 documented the wider system: migrant children, many who arrived alone, working overnight shifts in food production and other dangerous industries across the country, sponsored into debt and silence. Federal enforcement data show the trend line bending the wrong way. The number of minors found employed in violation of child labor laws rose 88 percent between 2019 and 2023, to 5,766 children, with violations more than doubling. In 2023 a sixteen-year-old was killed cleaning machinery at a Mississippi poultry plant. These are not accidents of a functioning system. They are the output of a labor model that treats vulnerable children as disposable inputs and prices the fines as overhead.



The long-term theft is generational. Children working nights fall asleep in class or leave school entirely, surrendering the education that was their one route out, while the injuries and chemical exposures follow their bodies for life.



Conclusion:

The use of child labor in America's food system is not an isolated scandal. It is a systemic rot, running through staffing agencies and supply chains that were built to keep the brands clean and the labor cheap. No sandwich is worth a severed finger, and no paycheck is worth a stolen childhood. If the measure of a nation is how it treats its most vulnerable, then a food system fattened on the night shifts of thirteen-year-olds has already been weighed.





### 39. Food Price Volatility

Between 2020 and 2023, the price of eating rose faster than at any time in four decades. Food-at-home prices climbed roughly 25 percent over those years, with the annual increase peaking near 12 percent in 2022, and egg prices nearly doubled at their worst point. Wages did not follow. For wealthy households this was an annoyance absorbed by bulk buying and substitution. For poor households, where food already claims a far larger share of the budget, every spike is a triage decision among groceries, rent, and medication.



Some of the increase was real cost. Much of it was opportunity. The Federal Trade Commission's 2024 report on grocery supply chains found that the largest retail chains used the disruption years to widen their margins, with revenues growing faster than costs, and that the elevated profit levels persisted after supply chains healed. When three or four companies dominate a market, a crisis becomes a pricing umbrella under which everyone raises prices together and blames the weather. Rural communities and urban food deserts, where one store may be the only store, have no exit from the arrangement at all.



The health consequences arrive on a delay. Families trade down to the cheapest calories available, which are the ones engineered to be filling and nutritionally empty, and diet-related disease does the rest. Seniors on fixed incomes skip meals to cover prescriptions. Parents skip meals so children do not have to, and carry the stress chemistry of that arithmetic in their bodies for years.



Conclusion:

Food should be a stable foundation of life, not a speculative surface for margin expansion. Price volatility at the grocery store is a policy failure with a body count, produced by consolidation the government permitted and oversight the government declined to perform. When markets fluctuate, Wall Street gets a bailout. When food fluctuates, children go hungry. That is not a sustainable economy. It is a slow-motion collapse, measured one skipped meal at a time.





### 41. No Cap on Prison Food Contracts

The United States incarcerates more people than any nation on Earth, and it feeds many of them through private contractors whose profits rise as the food gets worse. Companies like Aramark and Trinity Services Group hold contracts across dozens of state and local systems. There is no meaningful federal standard capping what they can cut, and the people eating the food have no alternative, no complaint process with teeth, and no choice at all.



The documented record is stomach-turning. When Michigan used Aramark, state monitors and journalists documented maggots in food service areas, spoiled and underportioned food, and kitchen workers smuggling contraband, and the state terminated the contract in 2015. Impact Justice's 2020 report Eating Behind Bars, drawing on surveys of formerly incarcerated people, found that almost everyone reported not getting enough food to feel full, and about three quarters reported being served food that was spoiled, rotten, or inedible. In some systems the per-meal food budget has fallen to around a dollar or less, a figure that cannot purchase nutrition and can barely purchase calories. Religious and medical diets are routinely ignored until a court intervenes.



Malnutrition behind bars is not contained by the walls. It worsens diabetes, hypertension, and mental illness, raises tensions inside facilities, and then walks out the gate with every released person, where the healthcare costs land on Medicaid and public hospitals. The contractor keeps the margin. The public keeps the bill, twice.



Conclusion:

Food is not a luxury. It is a human right, and it does not evaporate at a prison gate. A food system that profits from hunger and spoilage reveals the deeper truth running through this audit: profit without oversight always comes at the expense of human dignity and life, and the people least able to resist are billed first.





### 42. Anti-Hunger Programs Undermined by Red Tape

America does not primarily have a hunger funding problem. It has a hunger paperwork problem. The aid exists, appropriated by Congress and announced in press releases, and then it is placed behind application mazes, verification loops, interview requirements, and recertification cliffs that shake eligible people off the rolls by the millions.



The USDA's own participation studies show that roughly 1 in 6 people eligible for SNAP never receive it, worn down by application complexity, technology barriers, and stigma. WIC, the nutrition program for mothers and small children, reaches only about half of the people eligible for it, according to USDA coverage estimates, even though its health benefits for infants are among the best-documented in all of public policy. Benefits lapse over missed appointments and unreturned forms, not changed circumstances. Seniors lose aid because the renewal notice went to an old address or a portal they cannot navigate. The 2022 expiration of universal school meal waivers shoved millions of families back into tiered eligibility paperwork the moment inflation hit their grocery budgets hardest.



The waste is measurable in both directions. USDA research estimates that every dollar of SNAP benefits generates about $1.50 in economic activity, so every eligible family bureaucratically excluded is a small local recession as well as a private hunger. Malnourished children learn less, earn less, and cost more in healthcare across their lifetimes. Caseworkers drown in churn, processing the same families off and back on, while the hunger the programs were funded to end continues on schedule.



Conclusion:

In the richest country on Earth, hunger persists not because of scarcity but because of systems designed to withhold help under the guise of efficiency. We do not have a funding problem. We have a bureaucracy problem, one that treats every eligible family as a fraud suspect until proven otherwise, and it costs lives, health, and economic output every single day it operates.





### 43. Factory Farm Pollution in Poor Areas

Concentrated animal feeding operations produce more than 500 million tons of animal waste a year in the United States, and they are not sited randomly. They cluster where land is cheap and political power is scarce, which in practice means beside low-income communities and communities of color, whose residents breathe the hydrogen sulfide and ammonia, drink from wells shadowed by nitrate runoff, and watch their property values and health decline together. The waste sits in open-air lagoons that leak in ordinary times and breach in storms, as dozens did when Hurricane Florence flooded North Carolina in 2018, sending animal waste across the same neighborhoods that had hosted it for decades.



The health evidence is not speculative. A 2018 study published in the North Carolina Medical Journal found that residents of zip codes with the densest hog operations had higher rates of infant mortality, kidney disease, anemia, and emergency room visits than demographically similar communities without them. Neighbors of these operations report asthma, headaches, nausea, and nosebleeds as features of daily life, and right-to-farm laws, written by and for the industry, strip them of the nuisance lawsuits that were once their only leverage. The companies, meanwhile, are often structured so the integrator owns the animals and the profits while the local contract grower owns the debt and the waste.



The ledger closes the same way it always does in this audit. The meat is cheap because the costs were invoiced to somebody else's lungs, water, and land values, and the public absorbs the healthcare and cleanup bills.



Conclusion:

Factory farms do not just produce cheap meat. They produce disease, displacement, and despair, sited deliberately among the people least able to fight back. The right to breathe clean air and drink safe water should not depend on your zip code, and a food system that requires sacrifice zones has confessed what it is.





### 45. Price Fixing by Food Conglomerates

Behind the grocery inflation of recent years sits an older, quieter crime. America's food giants have been caught, repeatedly and in writing, conspiring to fix the prices of the most basic foods on the shelf. These are not market fluctuations. They are felonies and civil violations proven in guilty pleas, verdicts, and settlements, and the fines keep landing below the profits.



In the chicken industry, Pilgrim's Pride pleaded guilty in 2021 to federal charges of fixing prices and rigging bids on broiler chickens and paid a $107 million criminal fine, while Tyson Foods agreed to pay more than $220 million to settle civil claims from buyers who said the industry coordinated supply to keep prices high. In eggs, a federal jury in 2023 found major producers, including Cal-Maine Foods and Rose Acre Farms, liable for conspiring to restrict supply and inflate prices in the 2000s. In Canada, the Weston empire admitted in 2017 that it had participated in an industry-wide arrangement fixing the price of packaged bread for some fourteen years. Bread. The pattern repeats in pork, in tuna, in one staple after another, because the expected penalty is a cost of doing business and the expected profit is not.



The victims are not abstractions. Low-income families spend a far larger share of their income on food, so every fixed cent on eggs or chicken comes directly out of a budget with nothing left to cut. The cascade runs from the grocery cart to the utility bill to the pharmacy counter to the rent, and it lands hardest on children and the elderly.



Conclusion:

Price fixing is not just a business crime. It is a betrayal of public trust with human consequences measured in hunger, sickness, and shortened lives. When corporations conspire to raise the price of bread, the poorest Americans pay the invoice, and a legal system that fines them less than the take has not deterred the conspiracy. It has joined it as a silent partner.





## PART 4: EDUCATION (46–60)





### 46. Underfunded Public Schools

In the United States, the quality of a child's education is still largely determined by zip code. Public schools in low-income neighborhoods consistently receive less funding per student than schools in wealthier areas, despite serving students with greater needs. Analyses by the Education Trust have found funding gaps running into the thousands of dollars per student between districts serving the most and the fewest students of color, and in some states the gap between the richest and poorest districts exceeds $10,000 per student per year.



The mechanism is not mysterious. America funds schools substantially through local property taxes, which means a child's education budget is set by the property wealth of the child's neighbors. Affluent districts thrive. Poor districts, carrying the compounded weight of redlining and disinvestment, get overcrowded classrooms, outdated materials, underpaid staff, and buildings with failing ventilation, lead in the water, and mold in the walls. Teachers in these schools routinely spend hundreds of dollars of their own money on basic supplies, and burnout and turnover run highest exactly where stability matters most.



The damage is physical as well as academic. Unsafe buildings, contaminated water, and absent school nurses and counselors translate into asthma, absenteeism, untreated trauma, and untreated illness. Beyond the schoolhouse door, poor educational outcomes track with higher incarceration, lower earnings, and shorter lives across decades of public health research. The Economic Policy Institute and others have estimated that closing achievement gaps would add trillions to national output over time, which means the underfunding is not even frugal. It is expensive cruelty dressed as savings.



Conclusion:

In a country that claims to value opportunity, the very foundation of that opportunity, education, is rationed by wealth. If we truly believe in equality, the path starts with funding every child's future, not just the ones born into affluence.





### 47. School-to-Prison Pipeline

Across the United States, zero-tolerance discipline, excessive suspensions, and armed police on campus have built a channel that runs from the classroom to the courtroom, and it does not draw students at random. Federal civil rights data show that Black students make up about 15 percent of public school enrollment but roughly 31 percent of students referred to law enforcement or subjected to school-related arrest. Students with disabilities are suspended and expelled at about twice the rate of their peers and are more likely to be criminalized than accommodated. In underfunded districts the spending pattern tells the story by itself: metal detectors and security guards are funded while counselor and social worker positions sit empty.



The consequences compound for life. A student pushed out of school faces dramatically higher risks of incarceration, unemployment, poverty, and early death, and incarcerated youth endure elevated rates of suicide, assault, and untreated mental illness. The economics are equally perverse. Researchers estimate that each high school dropout costs the public roughly $292,000 over a lifetime in lost taxes and increased public costs, while the Justice Policy Institute has put the cost of incarcerating a single young person at well over $100,000 a year in most states, money that would fund counselors, mentors, and college access many times over.



Conclusion:

When schools mirror prisons more than sanctuaries, we are not failing children. We are targeting them. The school-to-prison pipeline is a civil rights crisis, a public health emergency, and an economic own goal, and breaking it means choosing investment over punishment and hope over handcuffs.





### 48. Student Loan Crisis

More than 43 million Americans owe over $1.7 trillion in student loan debt, an average of roughly $37,000 per borrower, with many owing six figures. What was sold as the path to security has become a generational anchor, delaying homeownership, parenthood, business formation, and retirement saving for millions. The United States is nearly alone among wealthy nations in financing higher education primarily through individual debt rather than public investment, and it layered on interest rates of roughly 5 to 8 percent that let balances grow even while borrowers pay.



The burden does not fall evenly. Black borrowers are hit hardest by the compound of the racial wealth gap and higher borrowing: research has found that the typical Black borrower still owes nearly the full original balance two decades after starting college, while the typical white borrower has nearly paid it off. A large share of borrowers never graduate at all, leaving them with the debt and without the degree that was supposed to make it payable. The health toll is documented and grim. Chronic debt tracks with anxiety, depression, and cardiovascular strain, and in one widely reported survey by Student Loan Planner, 1 in 15 borrowers said they had considered suicide because of their student loans.



The drag is national, not just personal. The Levy Economics Institute estimated that canceling student debt would raise real GDP by roughly $86 billion to $108 billion per year by freeing household spending and business formation. Instead, the country runs a system in which the federal loan portfolio collects interest from the very workforce the economy needs to flourish.



Conclusion:

America's student debt crisis is not a fluke. It is the logical outcome of a system that monetized ambition and auctioned opportunity. A society cannot thrive when its most educated citizens are its most financially shackled.





### 50. Teachers Paid Poverty Wages

In the richest country on Earth, the people who educate its children are paid so little that many qualify for the same public assistance programs their students' families use. The National Education Association put the average teacher salary at $72,030 in 2023-24, but starting pay in many states still hovers around $35,000 to $40,000, and in high-cost regions even veterans are priced out of the communities they serve. Nearly one in five teachers works a second job, driving rideshare, waiting tables, tutoring, and selling plasma, and teachers routinely spend $500 or more of their own money each year on classroom supplies the district did not fund.



The pay structure is not an accident of tight budgets. It is a penalty the labor market documents precisely. The Economic Policy Institute has found that teachers earn roughly 24 percent less than other college-educated professionals with comparable experience, a gap that has widened for decades and that no amount of pizza parties offsets. The predictable results are burnout, attrition, and shortages concentrated in math, science, and special education, which land as larger classes and weaker instruction in the schools that can least afford either.



The bitterest line in the ledger is the subsidy. When a full-time professional qualifies for SNAP or Medicaid, taxpayers are quietly covering the difference between what the district pays and what survival costs, and the district books the savings as fiscal responsibility.



Conclusion:

When we underpay the people who teach our children, we devalue not just the profession but the future we claim to care about. No society thrives when its architects of knowledge are treated like expendable labor.





### 52. Banned Books & Curriculum Censorship

America is in the middle of the largest wave of school book bans in its recorded history. PEN America counted more than 3,300 instances of books banned in public schools in the 2022-23 school year alone, and the pace accelerated afterward, passing 10,000 instances in 2023-24. The targets are not random: books about race, American history, and LGBTQ+ lives lead the lists, including works like The Bluest Eye and Maus. At the same time, Education Week's tracking counts more than a dozen states, 18 by its tally, that have enacted laws or policies restricting how teachers may discuss race, gender, and history, enforced through vague language that punishes teachers for guessing wrong.



The human costs concentrate where they always do. Students who lose the books that reflect their lives lose a documented protective factor against isolation and bullying, and LGBTQ+ youth, already at elevated suicide risk, are told by policy that their existence is a controversy. Teachers exit the profession rather than teach under threat, and health education, including consent and mental health literacy, gets quietly skipped by educators afraid of complaints. A generation taught a sanitized history is a generation disarmed against propaganda, because citizens who were never taught how rights were won rarely notice how they are lost.



Conclusion:

Democracy dies in silence, and when books are banned, futures are too. Suppressing truth does not protect children. It disarms them. A free and open society requires free and open minds, and we cannot afford to raise a generation that does not know what freedom is, or how easily it can be taken.





### 60. College as a Class Filter

The American college admissions system is sold as a meritocracy and functions as a class filter. The research of economist Raj Chetty and colleagues found that students from families in the top 1 percent of income are 77 times more likely to attend an Ivy League college than students from the bottom fifth. Legacy applicants have historically been admitted at rates several times higher than non-legacy applicants with comparable files, a preference Harvard's own litigation data exposed in numbers. Test scores track family income closely, and wealth buys the preparation, the retakes, the consultants, and the extracurriculars that admissions offices then reward as merit. Meanwhile, large numbers of high-achieving low-income students never apply to selective colleges at all, for lack of information, guidance, and any reason to believe the door is open to them.



The stakes are written in life expectancy. Educational attainment is one of the strongest predictors of how long a person lives, and the Georgetown University Center on Education and the Workforce calculates that a bachelor's degree is worth roughly $1.2 million in additional lifetime earnings over a high school diploma. Gatekeeping college by class therefore distributes health, wealth, and years of life by parentage. There are signs of movement: the Supreme Court's 2023 decision ending race-conscious admissions sharpened scrutiny of legacy preferences, and several states have moved to ban legacy preferences in admissions, Colorado first in 2021 and Virginia, Maryland, and California in 2024. The filter is being challenged. It has not been removed.



Conclusion:

College is supposed to be the ladder out of poverty, but for many it is a locked gate behind ivy-covered walls. A system that rewards legacy over merit does not produce the best thinkers. It recycles power. If America truly values grit and intelligence, we must stop confusing privilege with potential, and start investing in the minds we have overlooked.





## PART 5: ENVIRONMENTAL CATASTROPHES (61–75)





### 61. Flint Water Crisis Still Unresolved

The Flint water crisis began in April 2014, when state-appointed emergency managers switched the city's water source to the Flint River to save money and failed to add the corrosion controls that would have kept lead out of the pipes. What followed was a mass poisoning of a majority-Black, working-class city of roughly 100,000 people. Independent researchers found that the share of Flint children with elevated blood lead levels doubled after the switch. An outbreak of Legionnaires' disease during the same period sickened dozens and killed at least 12 people. Residents reported rashes, hair loss, and brown water for over a year while officials at every level assured them it was safe.



The aftermath defines accountability in America. A settlement of roughly $626 million was approved for victims in 2021, most of it for the children. Flint's lead pipes were dug up and replaced under court pressure over the following years. But the criminal cases collapsed. Charges against the former governor and top health officials were dismissed in 2022 on procedural grounds, and no high-ranking official has served a day in jail for poisoning a city. The children, meanwhile, carry the lead's effects on developing brains for life, in special education needs, attention and behavior struggles, and futures quietly narrowed before they began.



Conclusion:

Flint was not a natural disaster. It was a man-made collapse of ethics, infrastructure, and accountability. It exposed the rot beneath the American promise: that some lives are protected, while others are calculated into the budget as collateral damage. The crisis may have faded from the headlines, but its consequences are ongoing, in the bloodstreams of children, the silence of empty courtrooms, and the permanent erosion of public trust. No jail sentence can undo the harm, but the absence of justice guarantees its repetition elsewhere.





### 64. Lead Pipes Nationwide

More than a century after lead's dangers became medically indisputable, the EPA estimates that about 9 million American homes still receive drinking water through lead service lines. There is no safe level of lead exposure, according to the CDC. Lead attacks the developing brains of children, lowering IQ, shortening attention, and raising the risk of behavioral disorders, and it threatens pregnant women and the kidneys and cardiovascular systems of adults. The lines are concentrated in older, poorer cities and neighborhoods, which means the exposure follows the same map as every other burden in this audit.



For decades the federal government knew and declined to act at scale. The Bipartisan Infrastructure Law of 2021 finally dedicated $15 billion specifically to lead service line replacement, and the EPA's 2024 Lead and Copper Rule Improvements require most lead pipes to be replaced within ten years. Even that progress comes with a catch documented in city after city: partial replacements and homeowner-side costs can leave the poorest households, and renters with no control over their plumbing, at the end of the line again.



Conclusion:

That any child in America still drinks from a lead pipe is not a reflection of ignorance. It is a reflection of indifference. We have the science. We have the funds. What we lack is the political will to stop poisoning the most vulnerable for the sake of delayed budgets and regulatory convenience. Until every pipe is replaced and every tap made safe, lead remains not just in our infrastructure, but in our conscience.





### 65. Cancer Alley in Louisiana

Along an 85-mile stretch of the Mississippi River between Baton Rouge and New Orleans sits one of the most polluted industrial corridors on Earth. Residents named it Cancer Alley. More than 150 petrochemical plants and refineries operate there, releasing benzene, formaldehyde, ethylene oxide, and chloroprene into communities that are disproportionately Black, low-income, and descended from people enslaved on the same land. Federal air toxics assessments have placed the cancer risk in some neighborhoods near the Denka Performance Elastomer plant in St. John the Baptist Parish among the very highest in the nation, driven by chloroprene emissions from the only plant in America that makes it.



The government's own conduct is part of the indictment. In 2023 the EPA sued Denka over the risk its emissions posed to nearby residents, including an elementary school a few hundred feet from the fenceline, and opened a civil rights investigation into whether Louisiana's permitting practices discriminated against Black communities. The civil rights investigation was later dropped under political pressure, a sequence that tells residents exactly how much their protection is worth when it becomes inconvenient. The plants hold the permits. The parish holds the funerals.



Conclusion:

Cancer Alley is not a natural consequence of industry. It is the outcome of zoning policy, environmental deregulation, and a long legacy of racism disguised as economic development. In this corridor, corporations flourish while communities bury their dead. The poison is not only in the air. It is in the contracts, the permits, the silence. Until we stop treating Black communities as disposable buffers for profit, the American economy will keep growing on a foundation of suffering, and that suffering, though concentrated, is national in scale.





### 70. FEMA Fails to Rebuild

When disaster strikes, Americans are told FEMA will be there. For working-class families, that promise too often arrives late, arrives short, or does not arrive at all. After hurricanes, floods, and fires, survivors meet a system that demands documentation disaster victims are least able to produce: clear property deeds, internet access, English fluency, and the stamina for appeals. In the rural South, generations of families have lived on heirs' property, land passed down without formal title, and for years FEMA denied their claims outright for lack of paperwork proving ownership, a policy the agency only loosened in 2021 after public exposure. Denial rates for individual assistance have run high even in catastrophic events, average grants cover a small fraction of real losses, and families have spent years in temporary housing, including the formaldehyde-tainted trailers that became a scandal after Hurricane Katrina.



The pattern is consistent enough to be structural. Recovery research and government audits have repeatedly found that aid flows faster and fuller to whiter, wealthier communities, while FEMA itself operates with chronic staffing shortages that stretch every timeline. For the rich, private insurance fills the gap. For everyone else, the federal safety net is the only net, and its holes are measured in mold exposure, displacement, bankruptcy, and the slow abandonment of towns that never come back.



Conclusion:

FEMA is supposed to be the nation's lifeline in crisis, but for too many it is a broken promise wrapped in red tape. Disaster does not discriminate, but recovery does, and when aid depends on internet access, deed ownership, or zip code, it is not emergency relief. It is triage by privilege. If help arrives too late to save a life, it is not help. It is hindsight.





### 71. Poor Air Quality in Urban Areas

Clean air is described as a universal right. Its distribution in America follows the redlining maps. Low-income urban neighborhoods are disproportionately sited beside highways, freight corridors, industrial zones, and waste incinerators, and their residents breathe higher concentrations of nitrogen dioxide, ozone, and fine particulate matter every day of their lives. The American Lung Association's State of the Air reports have repeatedly found that people of color are far more likely than white Americans to live in counties with failing air quality grades, and that well over 100 million Americans live in places with unhealthy levels of at least one major pollutant.



The body keeps the receipt. Asthma rates in Black children run roughly double those in white children, and asthma attacks are a leading cause of missed school days and emergency room visits in poor neighborhoods. Fine particulate pollution is linked in study after study to heart attacks, strokes, dementia, and tens of thousands of premature deaths in the United States each year. Children who cannot breathe cannot learn at their potential. Workers who are sick cannot earn. The medical bills land on the families and on Medicaid, while the emissions are booked as someone else's cost of doing business.



Conclusion:

Pollution does not float randomly. It follows redlines, freeways, and economic boundaries. Poor air quality in America's cities is not an unfortunate coincidence. It is the legacy of planning decisions, zoning laws, and environmental policy made without regard for the people who live closest to the exhaust. When a child cannot breathe, they cannot learn. When an entire neighborhood is forced to inhale corporate waste, what is happening is not urban development. It is environmental eviction. Clean air is not a luxury. It is the bare minimum of justice.





### 73. Utility Companies Spark Disasters

In November 2018, a faulty Pacific Gas and Electric transmission line ignited the Camp Fire, which destroyed the town of Paradise, California, and killed 85 people, the deadliest wildfire in state history. PG&E pleaded guilty in 2020 to 84 counts of involuntary manslaughter. It was not an aberration. The utility's equipment had already been tied to a string of earlier fires, and in 2021 its lines sparked the Dixie Fire, the second largest in California history. A century-old infrastructure, deferred maintenance, and executive bonuses turned out to be a combustible combination.



Follow the money through the wreckage. PG&E passed through bankruptcy, paid a $13.5 billion settlement to fire victims, much of it in stock rather than cash, and continued raising rates on the same customers its equipment endangered, with Californians' bills climbing steeply to fund wildfire mitigation the company had neglected for decades. No senior executive went to prison for the dead of Paradise. The corporation, a legal person for purposes of profit, pleaded guilty to manslaughter, and the actual people who made the maintenance decisions retired on schedule. That is the accountability model on offer: the public absorbs the risk, the ratepayer absorbs the cost, and the executive absorbs nothing.



Conclusion:

A utility is a monopoly granted by the public to serve the public. When it burns a town instead, and its punishment is a restructuring plan and higher rates, the lesson every other utility learns is that safety is optional and consequences are negotiable. Justice that never reaches a decision-maker is not justice. It is a fee.





### 74. Bottled Water Privatization

Water falls from the sky and flows under public land. Then a corporation bottles it and sells it back to the public at a markup of thousands of percent. For years, Nestlé and its successor BlueTriton pumped water from the San Bernardino National Forest in California under a federal permit that had expired in 1988, paying little more than a few hundred dollars a year in fees, while surrounding communities endured drought restrictions. In Michigan, the company paid a $200 annual administrative fee for a permit to pump hundreds of millions of gallons of groundwater a year, in a state where the residents of Flint were afraid to drink from their own taps. California regulators eventually ordered BlueTriton to stop unauthorized diversions, and public pressure forced the company out of some contested sites, but the model remains intact across the country.



The model deserves naming. A private firm extracts a public resource at near-zero cost, adds plastic and a label, and captures the profit, while the public keeps the depleted aquifers, the truck traffic, and the bottles, most of which are never recycled. The deepest irony is the market itself: bottled water sells best where public water has been allowed to fail, which gives the industry a standing interest in the failure continuing. Every community that cannot trust its tap is a captive customer, and captivity is the business plan.



Conclusion:

Water is not a product invented by a bottling company. It is a commons, held in trust for everyone, and a government that leases it for pennies while its own citizens buy it back by the case has sold something that was never its to sell. The theft is legal, permitted, and renewed on schedule, which makes it not less a theft but a policy.





### 75. Climate Change Denial Funded by Lobbyists

The fossil fuel industry understood the climate science before the public did. Internal company research in the 1970s and 1980s, later published and peer-reviewed, projected global warming with striking accuracy. The industry's public strategy ran the other way: fund doubt, fund friendly voices, and fund the politicians who would repeat both. Oil and gas interests still spend on the order of $100 million a year lobbying the federal government, according to OpenSecrets tallies, and the return on that investment is measured in delayed regulation, preserved subsidies, and decades in which the world's largest economy treated a known emergency as a debate topic.



The invoice for the delay has come due in the currency this audit uses. NOAA counted a record 28 separate billion-dollar weather and climate disasters in the United States in 2023 alone, costing more than $90 billion, and the human costs run past property: heat deaths climbing year by year, smoke seasons swallowing whole regions, flood maps dissolving under the weather they failed to predict. The people least responsible for emissions, the poor in uninsulated housing, outdoor workers, the residents of flood-prone land nobody else wanted, pay first and most. The industry's defense is that everyone used the energy. Everyone did not write the memos, fund the denial, or buy the delay.



Conclusion:

A lie purchased at industrial scale is still a lie, and this one was purchased with the habitability of the future as the currency. The companies that knew, and lied, and lobbied, owe a debt that dwarfs every other entry in this audit, and collecting it begins with refusing to call their strategy a difference of opinion. It was a business decision. Its victims are everyone now living with the weather it bought.





## PART 6: WORKPLACE EXPLOITATION (76–90)





### 76. Sub-Minimum Wage for Tipped Workers

The federal tipped minimum wage has been frozen at $2.13 an hour since 1991. Employers are allowed to pay servers, bartenders, and delivery drivers that amount and let customers make up the difference through tips, on the legal theory that gratuities are wages. In practice, tips are weather: they collapse in slow months, vanish with rude tables and bad sections, and disappear entirely in an economic downturn, which is exactly when workers can least afford it. The Economic Policy Institute has found that tipped workers face poverty rates roughly twice those of non-tipped workers, and servers, who are overwhelmingly women, endure some of the highest rates of workplace sexual harassment in the economy, a dynamic researchers tie directly to depending on the customer's goodwill for rent money.



The natural experiment has already been run. Seven states, including California, Oregon, and Washington, require the full minimum wage before tips, and their tipped workers earn more and fall into poverty less, while their restaurant industries continue to exist, a fact that refutes the industry's central prophecy. The two-tier wage is a relic of the post-slavery era, when tipping was imposed on formerly enslaved workers so employers would not have to pay them at all. It survives because the restaurant lobby defends it and because its costs are paid quietly, shift by shift, by the workers themselves.



Conclusion:

A wage that requires begging strangers for the difference is not a wage. It is a subsidy to restaurant owners, collected from the pockets of the women least able to fund it. The federal government has left the tipped wage untouched for more than three decades, which is not neglect. It is a standing decision about whose rent matters.





### 77. No Federal Paid Family Leave

The United States is the only wealthy nation on Earth with no guaranteed paid leave for new parents. Among all the countries tracked by global policy researchers, it sits in a tiny handful offering zero weeks of paid maternity leave by law. What federal law does provide, the Family and Medical Leave Act of 1993, is twelve weeks of unpaid leave, and only for workers who clear its eligibility gates: a year of tenure, enough hours, and an employer with at least 50 employees, which together shut out roughly 44 percent of the workforce. The rest improvise. About a quarter of American mothers return to work within two weeks of giving birth, not because they are healed but because the rent is due.



The consequences are medical before they are economic. Research across wealthy nations links paid leave to lower infant mortality, higher breastfeeding rates, and better maternal mental health, and the United States, alone among its peers, posts infant and maternal mortality rates that embarrass its GDP. Thirteen states and the District of Columbia have now built their own paid family leave programs, proof that the policy works at American scale, and their existence makes the federal failure a choice rather than a mystery. A country that calls itself pro-family in every speech and funds family in almost none of them has announced its real position.



Conclusion:

Every other rich nation decided that a newborn's first weeks and a worker's recovery are not luxuries to be financed by credit card. America decided employers' convenience outranks both. The unpaid leave law protects a job, sometimes. It does not protect a family, ever, and the difference is measured in hospital bills, lost mothers, and infants whose first lesson is that their country was not expecting them.





### 78. "Right to Work" Laws Undercut Unions

Twenty-six states have enacted so-called right-to-work laws, which allow employees in unionized workplaces to take the benefits of a union contract, the wages, the grievance process, the safety enforcement, without paying dues or fees toward the cost of providing them. The predictable result is the intended one: unions starved of resources represent everyone worse, bargain weaker, and organize less, until the workplace has a union in name and a suggestion box in fact. The Economic Policy Institute's research finds that wages in right-to-work states run about 3.1 percent lower than in comparable states without them, a difference of roughly $1,670 a year for a typical full-time worker, flowing from every worker in the state, union or not, because unions raise the floor for entire labor markets.



The framing is the tell. No law was needed to create a right to work, because the term was invented by lobbyists to make a funding restriction sound like a freedom. What these laws guarantee is a right to free-ride, enforced by the state, against the one institution that has ever consistently raised working-class wages. Union membership in the United States has fallen to about 10 percent of workers, 6 percent in the private sector, and the states that engineered the decline now advertise their low wages to relocating corporations as an attraction, which is the policy working exactly as designed.



Conclusion:

Strip the slogan and the law reads plainly: the state forbids workers from pooling their resources effectively, then congratulates itself on their liberty. Wages, safety enforcement, and bargaining power fall together, and the savings are delivered to employers as a location incentive. That is not a right. It is a transfer, from the many who work to the few who hire.





### 79. Wage Theft Is Common and Unpunished

If a worker stole $15 billion from cash registers in a year, America would declare a crime emergency. Employers steal at least that much from workers' paychecks annually, and it is processed as a paperwork matter. The Economic Policy Institute's landmark study of just the ten most populous states found 2.4 million workers paid below the minimum wage, losing about $8 billion a year, and estimated minimum wage violations alone at roughly $15 billion nationally. That figure counts only one kind of theft. Add unpaid overtime, off-the-clock work, stolen tips, illegal deductions, and misclassification, and the true total climbs far higher. For scale, the FBI puts the combined losses from all robberies, burglaries, and car thefts in America below what employers take from paychecks by this one method.



The enforcement gap is the policy. The Wage and Hour Division recovers a few hundred million dollars a year in back wages, a fraction of what is taken, and it does so with an investigator corps smaller, relative to the workforce, than at any point in generations. Most victims never file, because the workers most often robbed, immigrants, tipped staff, warehouse and home care workers, are the ones with the most to lose from complaining, and the penalties for getting caught are so light that payroll departments can rationally budget for them.



Conclusion:

Wage theft is the largest property crime in America, and it is committed in the open, on letterhead, against the people least able to absorb the loss. A legal system that imprisons shoplifters and fines payroll departments has not failed to notice the difference. It has chosen it. Every stolen paycheck is rent unpaid, medicine unbought, and a lesson taught to every watching employer: the money is there for the taking, and the taking is nearly free.





### 80. Disabled People Paid Pennies

Federal law still permits employers holding Section 14(c) certificates to pay workers with disabilities less than the minimum wage, in some documented cases less than a dollar an hour, and in investigations over the years, mere cents. The program dates to 1938 and was defended as training. In practice it built a sheltered workshop system in which tens of thousands of disabled Americans sorted, assembled, and packaged for wages that could not support any part of an independent life. Roughly 40,000 workers remain under these certificates today, even as state after state, more than a dozen so far, has abolished the practice within its own borders, and even as study after study finds that supported employment in ordinary workplaces produces better wages and better lives at comparable cost.



The defense of the subminimum wage always arrives dressed as concern: these workers, it is said, would have no jobs at all without it. The states that ended it tested that claim, and their disabled workers did not vanish from employment. They moved into real jobs at real wages, because the binding constraint was never their productivity. It was the imagination of the system that warehoused them and the convenience of the employers who profited from the discount.



Conclusion:

Paying a person pennies because their body or mind works differently is not charity and it is not training. It is a legal statement about the value of a class of citizens, made in the one language employers and governments both speak fluently: the paycheck. A nation that measures worth in wages and then sets the wage at twenty-two cents has told on itself completely.





### 83. Gig Workers Lack Benefits

The gig economy's founding trick is a label. By classifying drivers, couriers, and shoppers as independent contractors rather than employees, companies like Uber, DoorDash, and Instacart avoid paying into Social Security and Medicare on the employer's side, avoid unemployment insurance, avoid workers' compensation, avoid overtime, and avoid the minimum wage itself. Every protection built into American labor law over a century attaches to the word employee, so the industry's lawyers simply declined to use the word. The work, meanwhile, looks exactly like a job: the company sets the price, controls the assignment, disciplines by algorithm, and can terminate at will, a power it calls deactivation.



The costs land where they always land. A driver injured in a crash discovers there is no workers' comp. A courier whose hours evaporate discovers there is no unemployment insurance, because no one paid in. Studies of driver pay in major markets have repeatedly found earnings at or below local minimum wage once vehicle expenses are counted, and the companies defend the arrangement with flexibility, as if paid sick days and schedule control were a package no worker could hold at once. When California tried to legislate the classification honestly, the industry spent more than $200 million on Proposition 22, the most expensive ballot campaign in American history, to purchase its own exemption from the law.



Conclusion:

The gig model is not innovation. It is subtraction: of benefits, of protections, of the employer's share of the social contract, performed at scale and marketed as freedom. The flexibility workers are praised for choosing is the flexibility to absorb every risk the company declined. A business that can only profit by mislabeling its workforce has not disrupted an industry. It has looted a century of labor law and sold the pieces back as an app.





### 86. Retaliation Against Whistleblowers

Every safety law in this audit depends on one fragile mechanism: a worker willing to speak. The system punishes that willingness with remarkable consistency. Federal whistleblower protections are a patchwork spread across dozens of statutes, many with filing windows as short as 30 days, and the oldest of them, the Occupational Safety and Health Act's anti-retaliation provision, gives workers no right to sue on their own behalf and caps the remedy at reinstatement and back pay, a prize hardly worth losing a job over. Surveys of workers who report violations routinely find that a large share experience retaliation, from cut hours and bad shifts to blacklisting and termination dressed in pretext.



The industries where silence is most dangerous are the ones where retaliation is most documented: meatpacking plants where line speeds injure, warehouses where injury logs and discipline systems intertwine, hospitals where nurses who report unsafe staffing find their schedules rewritten. During the pandemic, workers who spoke to reporters about missing protective equipment were fired in cases that made national news, teaching every coworker watching the real curriculum. A regulator cannot be on every floor. The law knows this, relies on worker reports to target inspections, and then under-protects the reporters it depends on.



Conclusion:

A whistleblower is the smoke alarm of the entire regulatory system, and America fines the alarm for making noise. Until retaliation costs employers more than compliance does, unsafe conditions will keep their most reliable shield: the rational fear of the people who see them first. The workers are not failing to report. The country is failing to make reporting survivable.





### 88. No Federal Minimum Wage Increase Since 2009

The federal minimum wage has been $7.25 an hour since July 24, 2009. It is the longest period without an increase since the minimum wage was created in 1938. A full-time, year-round worker at the federal minimum earns about $15,080 a year, below the poverty line for a family of two, let alone the family of four the wage was once expected to help support. Inflation has done quietly what no Congress had to vote for: the minimum wage has lost roughly a third of its purchasing power since 2009, and it buys far less than it did at its 1968 peak, when it was worth the equivalent of roughly $12 to $14 today.



Thirty states and the District of Columbia have raised their own floors above the federal level, which is an admission, by governments of both parties, that $7.25 is not a survivable wage. Twenty states still default to it, and in those states the floor set in Washington is the wage in the warehouse, the diner, and the nursing home. The political failure is bipartisan and long-running: bills are introduced, praised, and buried, while the value of the wage performs the cut on its own, year after year, with no fingerprints on it.



Conclusion:

A minimum wage that falls every year without a vote is not stability. It is a slow repeal, outsourced to arithmetic. The workers paid at the floor are not asking for luxury. They are asking the number to mean what it meant when their grandparents were paid it, and a Congress that will not even maintain the value of its own promise has told them precisely what the promise is worth.





### 90. Workers' Comp Denials & Delays

Workers' compensation was the original grand bargain: injured workers gave up the right to sue, and in exchange they were promised medical care and partial wages, quickly and without a fight over fault. A century later, the fight is the system. State after state has spent decades cutting benefits, narrowing which injuries count, and raising the proof burdens on workers, a demolition documented in detail by ProPublica and NPR's 2015 investigation, which found that many states had slashed benefits so deeply that workers injured in identical accidents received wildly different compensation depending on which side of a state line they fell on. Insurers deny claims as a business practice, delay treatment authorizations while injuries harden into disabilities, and settle for pennies against workers who cannot survive the wait.



The scale of what goes uncompensated is the quiet scandal underneath. Research on the true cost of workplace injury and illness has estimated that workers' compensation covers only about a fifth of it. The rest is paid by injured workers themselves, by their families, and by public programs, Medicare, Medicaid, and disability insurance absorbing costs that employers' insurance was created to cover. With more than 5,000 Americans killed on the job each year and millions injured or sickened, that cost shift runs to the tens of billions, invoiced to everyone except the party the bargain assigned it to.



Conclusion:

The grand bargain is broken on one side only. Workers still cannot sue. They simply also cannot count on the care and wages they traded their rights for. An injured worker begging an insurer for an authorized MRI is not experiencing a glitch. They are experiencing the system as redesigned, by legislatures, over decades, at the request of the industries that fund them.





## PART 7: DIGITAL HARM & SOCIAL MEDIA (91–105)





### 91. Addictive Algorithm Design

The major social platforms are not neutral pipes. They are engagement machines, engineered with variable-ratio rewards, infinite scroll, autoplay, and algorithmic feeds tuned by thousands of experiments to maximize the time each user, including each child, spends inside. This is not speculation about their business model. It is their business model, described in their own internal documents. When the Wall Street Journal published Meta's internal research in 2021, it revealed that the company knew Instagram made body image issues worse for a substantial share of teenage girls, roughly one in three of those who already struggled, and kept the finding internal while publicly minimizing the harm. In 2023 the U.S. Surgeon General issued a formal advisory that social media presents a profound risk of harm to children and adolescents, citing links to anxiety, depression, disordered eating, and sleep disruption, and warning that the country cannot wait for perfect evidence while the experiment runs on its children.



The legal structure protects the experiment. Section 230 shields platforms from liability for hosted content, the design choices that amplify it are treated as the platforms' own protected judgment, and the few state laws attempting age-appropriate design rules have been tied up in industry litigation. Meanwhile the product reaches children years before their brains finish developing the impulse control the product is engineered to defeat. Adults lose hours they did not consent to lose. Children lose something worse: the baseline of what attention, boredom, and self-worth are supposed to feel like.



Conclusion:

A casino may not seat a twelve-year-old at a slot machine, but a platform may hand her a more sophisticated version of one and call it connection. The addiction is not a side effect. It is the revenue model, and the generation being mined for it will pay with their mental health for what shareholders collected in quarters. When an industry profits from a child's compulsion and lobbies against every guardrail, the compulsion is the product, and the child is the raw material.





### 101. Fanfiction Platforms Hosting Pedophilic Content

The large fanfiction archives are built on a principle of minimal gatekeeping, and their content shows it. Alongside the harmless and the genuinely creative sits a large body of explicit sexual material involving underage fictional characters, searchable by tags that describe the acts and ages in plain terms. Access controls on the major archives amount to little more than a self-declared click confirming the visitor claims to be old enough, a lock that stops no one and is not designed to. The platforms' position, defended by their parent organizations, is that fictional text harms no real child and is protected expression, and under current American law that position largely wins: purely written fiction is generally protected speech, and federal law targets visual abuse imagery of real children, not prose about invented ones.



The audit's concern is what the legal answer leaves out. These archives are where young fans actually live online, and a teenager who arrives to read about her favorite characters can reach explicit material about characters her own age in a handful of clicks, normalized by community conventions and tagged like a library catalog. The platforms resist external regulation as an existential threat to fan culture, and they have built their governance so that almost nothing short of a court order can impose standards on what they host. Between the First Amendment on one side and platform absolutism on the other, the people with no seat at the table are parents, and the children browsing the stacks.



Conclusion:

The question is not whether every story on an archive is harmful. It is whether a space designed for the young should host sexual content about the young at all, behind a lock made of a checkbox. A library card was never the issue. The issue is an industry of archives that calls its refusal to moderate a principle, while the audience it refuses to protect is the one it was built to attract.





## PART 8: JUSTICE SYSTEM FAILURES (106–120)





### 106. Two-Tiered Justice System

The Constitution promises the accused a lawyer. What it delivers depends on the accused's bank account. A wealthy defendant buys a legal team: investigators, experts, jury consultants, and attorneys with a handful of cases. An indigent defendant gets a public defender carrying a caseload that would be comical if it were not measured in human freedom. National standards have long said a defense attorney should handle no more than about 150 felony cases a year, and workload studies keep finding defenders at two, three, or four times that, meeting clients at the courtroom door and pleading them out in minutes. The 2023 National Public Defense Workload Study, backed by the American Bar Association, concluded that acceptable caseloads are far lower than what most offices actually carry, which is a scholarly way of saying the right to counsel is being rationed past the point of meaning.



The second tier runs deeper than criminal court. In civil cases, eviction, deportation, custody, there is generally no right to a lawyer at all, so the tenant faces the landlord's attorney alone and the asylum seeker faces the government's. Money does not merely buy better arguments. It buys time, evidence, experts, and the credible threat of trial, which is what makes prosecutors and plaintiffs offer better deals. Poverty buys the opposite: pressure to plead, settle, or surrender quickly, regardless of the merits.



Conclusion:

Justice in America is not blind. She reads invoices. A system in which the quality of your defense is set by your wealth has not malfunctioned. It has been funded to exactly the level its operators intend, and the underfunding of public defense is not an oversight in the budget. It is the budget, expressing the country's real valuation of a poor person's liberty.





### 107. Cash Bail Criminalizes Poverty

On any given day, hundreds of thousands of people sit in American jails who have not been convicted of the charge against them, roughly seven in ten of everyone behind bars locally, held because they cannot afford bail. The median bail in felony cases runs around $10,000, an amount half the country could not produce in an emergency, which converts a judge's dollar figure directly into months of pretrial detention. The accused loses the job, then the housing, then custody arrangements, then the ability to help their own defense, and then, overwhelmingly, pleads guilty to get out, including people the evidence would have acquitted. The plea is not a confession. It is a ransom payment.



The system's cruelty has a name attached: Kalief Browder, arrested at sixteen in New York on an accusation of stealing a backpack, spent three years at Rikers Island awaiting a trial that never happened, much of it in solitary confinement, and after the charges were finally dropped, he took his own life. His case is famous because it was documented. The structure that produced it processes anonymous thousands every week. A small number of jurisdictions, Illinois most notably in 2023, have abolished cash bail outright, and the sky has not fallen, which reframes every other jurisdiction's system as a preference rather than a necessity.



Conclusion:

Cash bail does not sort the dangerous from the safe. It sorts the poor from the funded, and then punishes the poor for the sorting with lost jobs, lost homes, and coerced pleas. Liberty before trial is the stated rule of American law. The bail schedule is the actual one, and it prices freedom like a commodity, because to the people running the system, that is what it is.





### 108. Racial Disparities in Sentencing

The federal government's own statisticians keep measuring the same gap and the gap keeps being there. The U.S. Sentencing Commission has found, across successive studies, that Black male defendants receive sentences roughly 13 to 19 percent longer than similarly situated white male defendants convicted of the same offenses, after controlling for criminal history and other legal factors. The most recent report, covering 2017 through 2021, put the figure at 13.4 percent. These are not comparisons of different crimes. They are comparisons of like cases, and the difference is measured in months and years of human life, extracted by race.



The disparity is compounded upstream and down. Charging decisions, mandatory minimum triggers, and plea offers all sit inside prosecutorial discretion, largely unreviewable, and the crack cocaine sentencing structure spent decades punishing a drug associated with Black users at 100 times the severity of the pharmacologically identical powder associated with wealthier ones, a ratio Congress reduced to 18 to 1 in 2010 and has still never equalized. Downstream, every extra month is a month of lost earnings, lost housing, and children raised at a distance, in the same communities already carrying the weight of items throughout this audit.



Conclusion:

A sentencing gap that survives every control researchers apply is not a rounding error and it is not an accident. It is the measured output of a system whose discretion flows downhill onto the same people at every stage. Equality before the law is the inscription on the courthouse. The Sentencing Commission's tables are the fine print, and they say otherwise, in the government's own hand.





### 109. Overcriminalization of Minor Offenses

America processes more than 10 million misdemeanor cases a year, a shadow justice system larger than the felony system that receives all the attention, and it runs on conduct that in a saner legal order would be handled with a warning, a fine scaled to income, or nothing at all. People are arrested and jailed over traffic debt, marijuana possession, loitering, sleeping in public, and the accumulated fees of earlier encounters. The conviction itself is often the least of it. The arrest triggers license suspension, and more than 11 million licenses nationwide have been suspended over unpaid fines and fees, which manufactures the next offense, driving without a license, out of the punishment for the first one. Each contact risks the job, the housing application, and the record that follows a person into every future screening.



Marijuana is the emblem. Even as state after state legalizes it, hundreds of thousands of arrests for possession continued annually into the legalization era, falling overwhelmingly on Black users, whom the ACLU found nearly four times as likely to be arrested for possession as white users despite similar usage rates. The records those arrests created do not evaporate when the law changes. They sit in databases, denying employment and housing to people convicted of conduct their states now tax.



Conclusion:

A legal system that manufactures criminals out of poverty and paperwork is not keeping order. It is farming its own caseload, and billing the crop. The minor offense mill grinds fine, and what it grinds is the time, records, and prospects of the poor, for conduct the affluent commit with impunity every weekend of the year.





### 110. Juvenile Solitary Confinement

The United States holds children in solitary confinement in juvenile facilities across the country: locked alone in cells for 22 or more hours a day, sometimes for days or weeks, as discipline for behavior that in a school would earn detention. The federal government banned the practice for juveniles in its own custody in 2016, acknowledging what the research had already established, but the ban reached only federal facilities, and states remained free to continue. Many have.



The psychiatric evidence is not ambiguous. Adolescent brains are still developing the systems that regulate impulse, emotion, and reality itself, and isolation attacks exactly those systems. Studies and oversight bodies have linked juvenile solitary to sharply elevated risks of self-harm and suicide, and federal data have found that a disproportionate share of the young people who die by suicide in custody were in isolation or had recently been. The United Nations has called for the practice to be prohibited for children outright, classifying it alongside torture when applied to the young. Facilities defend it as safety management, which invites the obvious question of what emergency lasts for weeks and ends with a child talking to a wall.



Conclusion:

Solitary confinement breaks adults. Applied to children, it is the state deliberately damaging a developing mind and then, years later, expressing surprise at the damage. The practice survives because its victims are the least sympathetic population in the country, accused children, and because its harms ripen slowly, off camera. A justice system that tortures the young in the name of discipline has confessed its purpose, and the purpose is not rehabilitation.





### 111. Private Prisons Incentivize Incarceration

About 8 percent of America's prisoners, roughly 90,000 people, are held in prisons run for profit by corporations such as GEO Group and CoreCivic, each collecting billions in revenue, the great majority of it from taxpayers. The business model requires what no public institution should ever require: a reliably full building. Investigations of private prison contracts have found occupancy guarantees written directly into the deals, clauses obliging states to keep beds filled to quotas as high as 90 or even 100 percent, or pay for the empty ones. A state that signs such a contract has converted sentencing policy into a purchasing obligation, and every reform that empties beds becomes a breach of contract.



The incentives then do what incentives do. The industry spends millions lobbying and contributing to campaigns, opposing sentencing reform and supporting the enforcement policies that feed its census, including immigration detention, which has become its fastest growing revenue line. Cost comparisons keep failing to find the promised savings, and oversight reports keep finding the predictable economies: fewer staff, less training, worse medical care, higher violence. The profit has to come from somewhere, and in a prison it can only come out of the care, feeding, and guarding of the people inside.



Conclusion:

A company whose revenue rises with the prison population will never lobby for a smaller one, and a government that guarantees it full beds has sold a piece of its sentencing power to shareholders. Incarceration for profit is not a partnership. It is a conflict of interest with a stock ticker, and every person inside is inventory that depreciates only when freed.





### 112. Prison Labor = Legal Slavery

The Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery and involuntary servitude in the United States, except as punishment for a crime. That exception is not a footnote. It is a working business model. The ACLU's 2022 report Captive Labor documented that roughly 800,000 incarcerated people work inside America's prisons, about two thirds of the prison population, producing more than $2 billion in goods and over $9 billion in services annually. Their average pay for ordinary prison jobs runs between 13 and 52 cents an hour, and in several states, including Alabama, Arkansas, Florida, Georgia, Mississippi, and Texas, most prison jobs pay nothing at all. Refusal to work is punished with solitary confinement, loss of good time, and loss of family contact, which is what makes the word voluntary a fiction.



The work itself is not make-work. Prisoners fight wildfires in California, manufacture furniture and license plates, process food, and maintain the very facilities that hold them, jobs that would command real wages outside and that in some cases displace free workers and undercut outside employers. Firefighters trained at taxpayer expense inside prison then discover on release that licensing rules bar many of them from the profession they practiced in chains. A handful of states have begun removing the exception language from their own constitutions, and voters have approved the changes, but the federal exception stands, and so does the economy built on it.



Conclusion:

Call it by its structure rather than its stationery: compelled labor, unpaid or paid in coins, under threat of punishment, authorized by the constitutional text that supposedly ended it. The exception clause is the confession. A country that abolished slavery with an asterisk should not be surprised that the asterisk became an industry, and the industry will defend its labor supply exactly as fiercely as the last one did.





### 113. Lack of Prison Healthcare

The Supreme Court held in 1976 that deliberate indifference to a prisoner's serious medical needs is cruel and unusual punishment. Nearly fifty years later, deliberate indifference reads less like a legal standard than like an operations manual. Care behind bars is largely delivered by private contractors, Wellpath, NaphCare, and their predecessors, whose revenue depends on how little treatment they authorize. The results surface in oversight reports and wrongful death suits with metronomic regularity: treatable conditions progressing to emergencies, medications interrupted at intake, sick call requests answered in weeks, and specialists denied until a court or a corpse intervenes.



The hepatitis C scandal showed the system in miniature. When curative drugs arrived, states rationed them by disease stage, treating only prisoners whose livers were already badly damaged, a policy that saved money per budget cycle while letting a curable infection spread and worsen, until litigation forced state after state to treat earlier. The Justice Department's investigations of state systems, Alabama's most famously, have documented medical and mental health care so deficient as to be unconstitutional, in facilities where suicide and preventable death are recurring line items. And the bill is not avoided, only deferred: nearly everyone inside eventually comes home, carrying untreated illness into the public health system this audit has already priced.



Conclusion:

The state takes total control of a person's body when it imprisons them, down to whether they may see a doctor, and then disclaims responsibility for what the control produces. That is not a healthcare gap. It is a duty assumed and abandoned, and the deliberate indifference standard has become the measure of how much abandonment the courts will absorb before naming it.





### 114. Police Kill Without Consequence

American police kill well over a thousand people a year. The Washington Post's database of fatal shootings has recorded roughly 1,000 annually for a decade, and broader counts that include other forms of force put the total higher still. Accountability for those killings is statistical noise. Criminologist Philip Stinson's long-running research at Bowling Green State University, tracking on-duty shootings since 2005, has found that only around 1 to 2 percent of officers who kill are charged with a crime, and a smaller share still are convicted, usually of lesser offenses than murder. The gap is not explained by the lawfulness of the shootings. It is explained by the machinery: prosecutors who work daily with police deciding whether to charge them, qualified immunity shielding officers from civil suits, union contracts that delay interrogations and purge disciplinary files, and juries instructed to judge the killing through the officer's fear.



The rare prosecution that succeeds proves the rule by its exceptionalism, and even it usually arrives only when video contradicts the official account so completely that the machinery cannot absorb it. Families, meanwhile, discover that the civil system prices a life in settlement dollars paid by taxpayers, not by the officer or the department's leadership, so the financial consequence of a wrongful killing lands on the same public that suffered it.



Conclusion:

A one or two percent charge rate is not a filter for justified killings. It is a license, understood as such on both sides of the badge. When the state kills its citizens and investigates itself, the verdict is written before the inquiry opens, and every community that has watched an officer return to duty after a killing has learned the lesson the system teaches: the monopoly on violence includes a monopoly on its forgiveness.





### 115. Civil Asset Forfeiture Abuse

Civil asset forfeiture allows police to seize cash, cars, and homes from people who have never been charged with a crime, and then requires the owners to prove their own property innocent to get it back. The burden of proof runs backward, the property is named as the defendant, and the cost of fighting often exceeds the value seized, which is the business model. The Institute for Justice's Policing for Profit research tallied roughly $68.8 billion in forfeitures by federal and state governments between 2000 and 2019, and federal auditors have found that enormous sums, billions in cash seizures by the Justice Department alone over a single decade, were taken in cases where no criminal charges followed.



The incentive is structural and admitted by the design: in most jurisdictions the seizing agency keeps some or all of what it takes, funding its own budget from the roadside. Predictably, seizures concentrate on people carrying cash who look like they cannot afford lawyers, which in America describes the poor, the immigrant, and the traveler of color, while the genuinely wealthy move money in forms no trooper can stuff into an evidence bag. Reform has come in patches, some states requiring convictions, federal policy swinging with administrations, but the default machinery remains available to every agency that wants it.



Conclusion:

The Fourth Amendment was written against exactly this: the government helping itself to a citizen's property on suspicion and keeping it by default. Forfeiture without conviction is not law enforcement financing itself. It is taxation by accusation, collected from the people least able to contest it, and a practice that pays the seizer will never lack for seizures. The property is presumed guilty, the owner is presumed rich enough to fight, and both presumptions are the point.





## PART 9: VETERANS & DISABILITY NEGLECT (121–135)





### 121. VA Backlogs Leave Vets Waiting Years

The Department of Veterans Affairs runs on delay. Its disability claims backlog, claims pending longer than the VA's own 125-day standard, has repeatedly swollen into the hundreds of thousands, surging again as veterans filed under the PACT Act, and veterans whose claims are denied and appealed can wait years for resolution. The pattern is old enough to have a body count with a date on it: in 2014, investigators exposed secret wait lists at the Phoenix VA, where schedulers falsified records to hide that veterans were waiting months for appointments, and veterans died on those lists. The scandal forced resignations and reforms, and the underlying arithmetic survived them both: a system sized for peacetime paperwork processing a wartime generation's injuries.



Every month of delay is a month a veteran with a ruined back, a traumatic brain injury, or a mind that will not stand down goes without the rating that unlocks healthcare, housing help, and income. The private sector analog would be an insurer that profits by outwaiting the claimant. The VA does not profit, which somehow makes it worse: the delay is pure institutional indifference, generated by understaffing and process, aimed at the one population the country swore it would never leave waiting.



Conclusion:

A promise deferred for years is a promise being broken in installments. The backlog is not an act of God. It is a staffing and priority decision renewed in every budget, and its costs are paid in untreated injuries, lost homes, and veterans who learn that the gratitude of their country has a processing time.





### 122. Homeless Veterans Forgotten

The 2024 federal point-in-time count found 32,882 veterans homeless on a single night in America. That number is the lowest since counting began and it is still a national disgrace with a flag on it. These are people who signed the contract in its most literal form, offering their bodies to the state, and the state's side of the bargain, care and support for life, turns out to have fine print: waitlists, eligibility tiers, and housing programs that reach a fraction of the need in the places veterans actually are. Veteran homelessness has fallen because of sustained investment in programs like HUD-VASH, which proves the problem is solvable at exactly the scale the country chooses to fund it, and no faster.



The causes read like the rest of this audit concentrated in one population: service-connected injuries and trauma, the disability backlog of item 121 paying too late, rents outrunning benefits, and a civilian labor market that thanks veterans for their service and declines to house them. Women veterans, the fastest growing group, face added barriers and are often invisible to systems designed around single men.



Conclusion:

Every homeless veteran is a receipt for a broken contract, presented by the creditor in person. The country that found trillions for the wars can find the housing money. It simply ranks the promise below the parade, and until the last veteran is housed, the phrase "support our troops" is an advertisement, not a policy.





### 123. Denial of Disability Claims

America's disability systems run on a presumption of fraud and a business model of denial. For Social Security disability, roughly two thirds of initial applications are denied, including applications documenting conditions that later win on appeal at high rates once a judge actually hears them, which tells you the initial review is not finding the truth. It is rationing by exhaustion. The appeals process then consumes a year or more at each stage, during which applicants cannot reliably work, cannot collect the benefit they are seeking, and deteriorate. The human price is a matter of record: reporting based on Social Security's own data found that nearly 110,000 people died between 2008 and 2019 while waiting for decisions on their disability appeals.



The VA system rhymes. Veterans' claims are denied at substantial rates for missing paperwork, disputed service connection, and examinations so cursory that oversight bodies keep finding error rates that would shame a private insurer, and each denial restarts the clock described in item 121. Both systems share the design logic: make the process long, technical, and lonely, and a predictable share of legitimate claimants will give up, die, or be rendered ineligible by the poverty the delay creates. The savings are real. They are simply extracted from the sick.



Conclusion:

A denial-first system does not protect the treasury from fraud. It protects it from obligation, by converting the applicant's endurance into the eligibility test. When a hundred thousand people die waiting for hearings, the wait is not a backlog. It is a policy outcome, and it should be owned as one, in the same plain language this audit uses for every other line item.





### 125. Burn Pit Exposure Ignored for Decades

In Iraq and Afghanistan, the American military disposed of its waste in open burn pits, burning plastics, electronics, medical waste, fuels, and worse, on bases where its own people lived and breathed. Service members came home with rare cancers, constricted lungs, and illnesses that did not fit the VA's templates, and for years the system's answer was a registry, a study, and a denial. VA data reported before Congress showed that roughly 78 percent of disability claims tied to burn pit exposure were being denied. The parallel to Agent Orange was exact and instructive: a generation exposed by its own government, required to prove the unprovable case by case, while the government studied the question at the pace of a glacier.



What changed the policy was not the science arriving. It was the dying becoming organized and impossible to ignore. The PACT Act, signed in August 2022, finally established presumptive service connection for a list of respiratory illnesses and cancers for veterans of the post-9/11 wars, shifting the burden from the sick veteran to the state that burned the pit. It is among the largest expansions of veterans' benefits in American history, and its necessity is the indictment: the care was owed from the first diagnosis, and it arrived decades and funerals late, only after the exposed did the government's evidentiary work for it.



Conclusion:

The pattern is now three generations old: expose, deny, study, and concede when the survivors become a constituency. Burn pits are this century's entry, and the veterans who won the PACT Act won it for the next war's exposed as much as for themselves. The debt was never in dispute. Only the payment date was, and the government chose the latest one it could get away with.





### 126. Veterans Suicide Crisis

The VA's annual suicide prevention report counted 6,392 veteran suicides in 2021, an average of about 17 veterans a day. The often-quoted figure of 22 a day comes from an earlier, cruder estimate, and the correction does not soften the fact: veterans die by suicide at a rate roughly one and a half times that of comparable civilians, year after year, in numbers that would fill a brigade. Most of the dead were not recent combat veterans in VA care. Many had never enrolled, had been discharged under conditions that cut them off from benefits, or had fallen through the seams between the military that used them and the agency meant to catch them.



The crisis is not a mystery awaiting a cause. It is the sum of this section of the audit: untreated trauma meeting the backlog of item 121, denied claims in item 123, homelessness in item 122, chronic pain managed with pills until the pills became their own emergency, and a transition system that hands a 22-year-old a discharge form and a pamphlet and calls it reintegration. The VA's prevention programs reach the veterans inside its walls. The walls are the problem.



Conclusion:

Seventeen flags a day are folded for deaths the country could have interrupted and largely did not. A nation that can track a missile in flight can find its veterans before the crisis line rings, and the fact that it does not is not a capacity problem. It is the final entry in the ledger of the contract: the state spends without limit to make the soldier, and rations everything required to unmake the war inside him.





### 130. Disability Benefits Below Poverty Line

The maximum federal Supplemental Security Income payment in 2024 was $943 a month. The federal poverty line for a single person was about $1,255 a month. Read those two numbers together and the program's design is confessed in arithmetic: America's benefit of last resort for disabled people who cannot work is set, by law, below the line the government itself draws for poverty. Average Social Security disability benefits, roughly $1,500 a month, hover barely above it. SSI's asset limit completes the trap: $2,000 in savings, a figure set in 1989 and never meaningfully updated, which makes saving for a car repair, a security deposit, or any emergency a punishable offense against eligibility. Marriage penalties cut benefits further for couples, as if disability were cheaper shared.



The predictable result is that disability in America is a sentence to managed destitution: housing that consumes the entire check, food insecurity, skipped medication, and dependence on the charity of family, in a country that spends more on healthcare than any nation alive. The recipients are not suspected of laziness by anyone who has met the eligibility standard, which requires proving an inability to work so total that most applicants are denied first, per item 123. Having proven they cannot earn, they are then paid too little to live. Both halves of that sentence are policy.



Conclusion:

A benefit set below the poverty line is not a safety net. It is a holding pattern at altitude zero, and the asset limit is its cruelest clause, forbidding the poor the one tool, savings, that every financial advisor in America prescribes. The country decided what a disabled life is worth and wrote the number down: less than poverty, on purpose, in the statute.

# The Pentagon Papers: An Audit of the American Dream — Audit 2



By Mary-Victoria Crockett



This document is part of a series that presents the complete audit from "Not Yours To Take: An Audit Of The American Dream," rewritten and fact-checked, items 1 through 500. Audit 1 holds Parts 1 through 9 (items 1 to 130). This document continues the audit in the original numbering.











## PART 10: CLIMATE INACTION (136–150)





### 136. U.S. Fails to Meet Emissions Targets

The United States is the largest historical emitter of greenhouse gases on Earth, and it has made a habit of announcing targets it does not hit. The country pledged under the Paris Agreement to cut emissions 50 to 52 percent below 2005 levels by 2030. Independent analyses, including the Rhodium Group's annual assessments, have consistently found the country off that pace, projecting reductions closer to 37 to 42 percent even with the Inflation Reduction Act's investments counted, and the gap widens or narrows with each administration's appetite for rollback. The United States has now withdrawn from the Paris Agreement twice, in 2020 and again in 2025, the only nation to treat its own signature as a revolving door.



The consequences of the shortfall are not abstract and they are not evenly shared. They arrive as the disasters counted in items 70 and 75, as the heat of item 139, and as insurance markets abandoning entire states. Every missed target is also a transfer: the costs of emissions produced disproportionately by American industry and consumption land on Bangladeshi farmers, Pacific islanders, and the residents of the Gulf Coast alike, while the emitters book the profit in the present tense. A contract framework makes the failure legible. The atmosphere is the ultimate commons, and the world's largest historical polluter has defaulted on its share of the maintenance while lecturing others on responsibility.



Conclusion:

Missing a target once is a failure. Building a national rhythm of pledge, underdeliver, withdraw, and rejoin is a policy, and the policy is delay, purchased by the industry of item 75 and paid for by everyone who will live in the climate the delay creates. The physics does not negotiate, does not accept the filibuster as an excuse, and does not care which party held the pen. The bill is compounding, and it is denominated in coastlines.





### 138. No Climate Resettlement Plan

Americans are already being displaced by the climate the country failed to stabilize, and there is no national plan for where they are supposed to go. The precedents exist in miniature and they are damning by their smallness. Isle de Jean Charles in Louisiana, home to a Biloxi-Chitimacha-Choctaw community watching its land dissolve into the Gulf, received a $48 million federal grant in 2016 to become the first federally funded climate relocation in the country, and the move still took years of friction. The Yup'ik village of Newtok, Alaska, eroding into the Ninglick River, has been trying to complete its move to Mertarvik for decades. These are the success stories. They involve a few hundred people, heroic effort, and timelines measured in generations.



The main federal tool for everyone else is the FEMA buyout, which purchases flood-damaged homes so families can leave, and studies of the program have found the process takes an average of roughly five years from disaster to closing, a timeline that selects for families wealthy enough to wait and strands the rest in damaged homes in the same floodplain. Meanwhile the National Flood Insurance Program's own data show the trap tightening: repetitive loss properties, about 1 percent of insured properties, have historically accounted for a quarter or more of the program's claim payments, homes flooding and being rebuilt in place, again and again, because no system exists to move their owners anywhere better.



Conclusion:

A country that can plan a weapons system forty years out has no plan at all for the coastal towns, river bottoms, and fire corridors it knows are being lost. The displaced will move anyway. The only question the missing plan answers is how: with dignity, resources, and a destination, or as refugees in their own country, discovering that the social contract does not cover the weather it helped create.





### 139. Wet Bulb Temperature Blind Spot

Heat is the deadliest weather in America, killing more people in an average year than hurricanes, tornadoes, and floods, and the country has no comprehensive federal strategy for the threshold scientists warn about most: wet bulb temperature, the combination of heat and humidity at which the human body can no longer cool itself by sweating. The theoretical survivability limit is a wet bulb reading of 35 degrees Celsius, about 95 degrees Fahrenheit. Research from Penn State published in 2022 found the practical limit for healthy young adults is lower, around 31 degrees in humid conditions, and lower still for the elderly and the sick. Above it, shade and water stop working. A healthy person sitting still dies in hours.



The Gulf Coast, the Southeast, and increasingly the Midwest already brush against dangerous wet bulb values during heat waves, and the exposure is stratified by income with lethal precision: outdoor workers, prisoners in uncooled facilities, the residents of heat island neighborhoods and top floor apartments without air conditioning. Phoenix endured 31 consecutive days at or above 110 degrees in 2023, and Maricopa County recorded 645 heat-associated deaths that year, a municipal statistic with no parallel in the developed world. Against all of this, the federal apparatus offers advisories. OSHA proposed the first national workplace heat standard in 2024, decades into the emergency, and its fate remains uncertain, while most states have no heat rules of their own.



Conclusion:

A government that can model the throw weight of a warhead to the meter has not built a plan for the day the air itself becomes unsurvivable in its own southern tier. The blind spot is not scientific. The science is published, replicated, and worsening on schedule. The blind spot is political: heat kills the poor quietly, one body at a time, without footage, and so it is governed as a personal inconvenience rather than what it is, the leading edge of the largest foreseeable mass casualty environment in American life.





## PART 12: PREDATORY FINANCE (166–180)





### 166. Payday Loans Trap the Poor in Debt

A payday loan is a trap with a storefront. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau puts the typical cost at about $15 for every $100 borrowed over two weeks, an annual percentage rate near 400 percent, and in some states the effective APR climbs past 600 percent. The product is marketed as a bridge to the next paycheck, but the CFPB's own research found that about 80 percent of payday loans are rolled over or followed by another loan within two weeks, because the full balance plus fees, due in one lump sum, is exactly what a borrower living paycheck to paycheck cannot produce. The median borrower ends up indebted for months and pays more in fees than the amount originally borrowed. The loan does not bridge the gap. It becomes the gap, recurring every fourteen days.



The storefronts are not distributed randomly. They concentrate in poor neighborhoods and Black and Latino communities, in states whose legislatures have declined to cap the rates, while states with a 36 percent cap watch the storefronts close and their residents somehow survive without them. The federal government already concedes the principle: the Military Lending Act caps loans to service members at 36 percent, because Congress understood that a soldier buried in 400 percent debt is a readiness problem. A waitress buried in the same debt is apparently a business model.



Conclusion:

Payday lending is the legal sale of desperation at rates that would be called usury in any honest vocabulary. It extracts its billions from the exact households this audit keeps visiting, one rollover at a time, and it persists because legislatures have decided that the lender's profit outranks the borrower's escape. The trap is not a flaw in the product. The trap is the product.





### 167. Overdraft Fees Exploit the Working Class

American banks collected more than $11 billion in overdraft and nonsufficient funds fees in a single recent year, and the CFPB's research shows who paid it: a small slice of account holders, roughly 9 percent, incur the large majority of the fees, and they are disproportionately the customers with the lowest balances and the least margin for error. The mechanics are engineered. A $6 coffee can trigger a $35 fee when transactions are processed in an order that maximizes overdrafts, a practice that has cost banks hundreds of millions in regulatory settlements and continues in new forms. The customer is not borrowing money in any meaningful sense. The bank covers a few dollars for a few days and charges a fee that, expressed as an interest rate, would make a payday lender blush.



Recent pressure has moved the industry somewhat: several large banks cut or eliminated the fees after regulatory scrutiny and competition made the practice embarrassing, and the CFPB moved to cap overdraft fees at the largest institutions. That the reforms came only after decades of extraction, and remain contested by the industry in court and in Congress, tells the story of this whole section of the audit. The fee was never about the cost of covering a transaction. It was a toll on being poor at the wrong moment in the month, collected automatically, at scale.



Conclusion:

An overdraft fee is a poverty penalty with a receipt. It takes the account holder who is already at zero and moves them to negative thirty-five, then charges them again for the consequences. When an industry's profit center is its customers' worst week, the industry is not providing a service. It is farming distress, and the harvest is measured in billions.





### 168. Credit Scores as Class Warfare

A three-digit number generated by private companies from your history of being poor now stands at the gate of nearly every necessity in American life. Landlords screen tenants by it. Employers in many states screen applicants by it. Insurers price car and home policies by credit-based scores, so a driver with a spotless record and a thin wallet can pay more than a wealthier driver with a worse one, a pattern ProPublica's analysis of insurance data documented in minority neighborhoods. The score does not measure trustworthiness. It measures the financial turbulence of your past, which is to say it measures how much money you had when life happened to you, and then it ensures life keeps happening to you at a surcharge.



The system's accuracy would not survive its own standards. A Federal Trade Commission study found that about one in five consumers had an error on at least one of their credit reports, and the burden of fixing the record sits entirely on the person the record is about, navigating dispute processes designed by the companies that made the error. Medical debt, the leading souvenir of the healthcare failures in Part 1, flowed onto credit reports for decades, converting a hospital's billing chaos into a decade of housing and employment consequences, until regulators finally began forcing some of it off the reports in recent years.



Conclusion:

The credit score began as a lending tool and metastasized into a caste system, a single number that follows the poor into every transaction and taxes them for having been taxed. It launders the judgment: no landlord or employer has to say they discriminate against poverty, because the score says it for them, in the neutral language of risk. A society that lets an unaccountable algorithm grade its citizens' worthiness for shelter has outsourced a moral decision it did not want to sign.





### 169. Rent-to-Own Scams

The rent-to-own store sells the same television twice: once as a rental and once as a bill that never ends. The model targets customers who cannot buy outright and cannot qualify for ordinary credit, offering weekly payments on furniture, appliances, and electronics. Stretched over the contract's life, those payments commonly total two to three times the item's retail price, at effective interest rates that consumer advocates have documented in the triple digits. Miss a payment and the item is repossessed, along with every dollar already paid, because the contract is written as a lease precisely so that consumer credit law, with its rate disclosures and protections, does not apply.



The legal architecture is the point. Rent-to-own operates in most states under special statutes the industry lobbied into existence, exempting the transactions from the usury and credit rules that govern every other way of buying a refrigerator over time. The customers know the math is bad. They are not stupid. They are choosing between a working stove this week at triple price and no stove, which is not a choice the affluent ever face and is therefore easy for the affluent to judge. The desperation is the market. The markup is what desperation costs.



Conclusion:

Rent-to-own is payday lending with furniture, a business that converts poverty into a permanent payment plan and repossession into a restocking strategy. When the same couch can be sold, seized, and sold again, the couch is not the product. The customer's lack of alternatives is, and the law that blesses the arrangement has chosen its side in writing.





### 170. No Federal Cap on Interest Rates

For most consumer lending in the United States, there is no national ceiling on interest. States once enforced usury laws, but a 1978 Supreme Court decision let national banks charge whatever rate their home state allowed, and states like South Dakota and Delaware promptly abolished their caps to attract the industry, which is why your credit card agreement is governed by the law of a state you may never have visited. The result is a national market in which the average credit card APR has climbed above 20 percent and storefront and online lenders in permissive states lawfully charge rates that would have been criminal a century ago.



The absence of a cap is defended as access: price credit too low, the argument goes, and the risky will get none. The country has run the comparison. The Military Lending Act's 36 percent cap for service members did not end lending to soldiers. States with rate caps have functioning credit markets. What a cap ends is the specific business model of lending designed to fail, in which the profit comes not from repayment but from the rollover, the fee, and the garnishment. Congress has repeatedly declined to extend the military's protection to the rest of the population, which means the protection's logic is conceded and its beneficiaries are chosen.



Conclusion:

Usury was a sin in every major faith tradition and a crime in most of American history, not because lenders were unpopular but because societies learned what uncapped interest does to the desperate. The current law is the experiment in forgetting, and its results are itemized across this entire part of the audit. A nation that caps nothing has not deregulated a market. It has legalized a extraction and called the extraction freedom.





### 171. Poor People Pay More for Everything

Poverty is expensive, and the surcharge is itemized everywhere. The unbanked pay fees to cash their own paychecks and buy money orders to pay their bills. Drivers in poor neighborhoods pay more for car insurance than identical drivers in wealthy ones, as ProPublica and Consumer Reports documented, because insurers price by territory and credit in ways that track race and income more faithfully than risk. Groceries cost more and offer less in neighborhoods served only by small stores, the cheapest calories carrying the highest markup per nutrient. Renters pay deposits and application fees at every move, buy furniture at rent-to-own rates per item 169, borrow at payday rates per item 166, and pay overdraft tolls per item 167, while the affluent are paid interest on money they can afford to leave sitting still.



Economists call this the poverty premium, and it functions as a second, invisible tax system, collected not by the government but by every business that prices for the desperate. Its cruelest property is its direction: it flows upward. The household with the least subsidizes, through fees and markups, the free checking, cash-back rewards, and bulk discounts of the household with the most, whose credit card points are funded by the interchange fees embedded in the prices both of them pay.



Conclusion:

The free market is supposed to reward thrift. For the poor it operates as a toll road where every necessity has a booth. When being broke costs more than being comfortable, poverty is not a condition people fail to escape through effort. It is a revenue stream, and the effort required to escape it must first outrun the surcharge for being in it.





### 172. Student Loan Interest Compounds Suffering

Item 48 indicted the student loan system. This entry is about its engine: capitalization, the process by which unpaid interest is added to principal, so that the borrower thereafter pays interest on the interest. A borrower doing everything right inside an income-driven repayment plan, paying the calculated amount every month, can watch the balance grow for years because the payment does not cover the interest. The debt they owe at thirty-five is larger than the debt they signed for at twenty-two, not because they borrowed more but because the arithmetic of the loan rewards the lender for the borrower's low wages.



The design converts time itself into a penalty. Interest accrues during schooling, during forbearance, during the servicer's processing errors and the borrower's unemployment, and each accrual is folded into the base for the next. Graduate and professional borrowers, and Black borrowers who research shows still owe nearly their full original balance two decades in, live at the sharp end of this compounding. The federal government is the lender in most cases, which means the United States Treasury books revenue from the slow financial drowning of its own graduates, and calls the arrangement an asset on its balance sheet.



Conclusion:

A loan that grows while you pay it is not a loan. It is a meter, running. Interest was meant to price risk and time for the lender. Capitalized against the young and the underpaid, it becomes a mechanism for converting an education, the one investment this country tells its children is safe, into the longest liability of their lives. The suffering is not a side effect of the math. The math is the policy.





### 173. IRS Audits the Poor More Than the Rich

For years, the most audited people in America were not tycoons with offshore accounts. They were poor parents claiming the Earned Income Tax Credit. ProPublica's investigations documented that EITC claimants were audited at rates comparable to the top 1 percent of earners, and that the most heavily audited counties in the country were poor, rural, and disproportionately Black, a map of enforcement that inverted every stated priority. The mechanism is bureaucratic rather than theatrical: EITC audits are cheap correspondence audits, conducted by mail, that count toward enforcement statistics without requiring the staff, lawyers, and years that auditing a complex high-income return demands. An IRS hollowed out by a decade of budget cuts audited what it could afford to audit, and what it could afford was the poor.



The distortion compounds at the top. As audit rates for millionaires and large corporations collapsed through the 2010s, the tax gap, the difference between what is owed and what is collected, grew to hundreds of billions of dollars a year, concentrated in exactly the complex income the IRS lacked the staff to examine. A 2023 Stanford-led study of IRS data added the racial ledger: Black taxpayers were audited at roughly three to five times the rate of other taxpayers, driven substantially by the EITC correspondence audit machine. The IRS has since acknowledged the disparity and pledged rebalancing, funded by the same appropriations that remain a perennial target for repeal.



Conclusion:

An enforcement system that audits the waitress's child credit more readily than the executive's partnership is not under-resourced by accident. It is aimed. Every dollar of the tax gap left uncollected at the top is a dollar the honest and the poor make up, and the audit map of the United States, poor counties dark with scrutiny, wealthy enclaves pale, is the social contract rendered as a heat map: enforcement for thee, latitude for me.





### 174. Corporate Tax Loopholes Bleed Public Funds

In 2017 and 2018, Amazon, one of the most profitable corporations on Earth, paid zero dollars in federal income tax, a fact documented from its own filings by the Institute on Taxation and Economic Policy. It was not an anomaly. ITEP found that 55 large, profitable American corporations paid no federal income tax in 2020 alone, and the techniques are legal and catalogued: stock option deductions, accelerated depreciation, offshore profit shifting, and credits purchased and stacked until the liability of a billion-dollar enterprise falls below the tax bill of a single nurse. The statutory corporate rate is the fiction. The effective rate for the best-advised companies is a rounding error.



The lost revenue does not vanish from the ledger. It is made up by the people whose taxes cannot be lawyered away, wage earners whose liability is withheld before they see the money, and by the public services whose starvation fills the rest of this audit. The same corporations that zero out their tax bills lobby for the infrastructure, courts, educated workforce, and patent enforcement that their businesses run on, a full-service state funded by everyone else. Recent reforms, a 15 percent corporate minimum tax on the largest companies' book income, began in 2023 to close a portion of the gap, against an industry campaign to repeal it that began before the ink dried.



Conclusion:

A tax code that bills the nurse and excuses the empire is not broken. It is written, line by line, by the people it excuses, through the lobbying and campaign finance this audit has already priced. The loophole is not a gap in the law. It is the law's purpose as purchased, and every zero on a corporate return is a charge transferred to the returns of people who cannot afford the accountants.





### 175. Credit Repair Industry Is Largely Unregulated

The credit repair industry sells hope to the people item 168 graded down, and its business record is a rap sheet. The model is standardized: charge upfront fees, which federal law prohibits for telemarketed credit repair, dispute every negative item on a report regardless of accuracy, and let the temporary removals masquerade as results until the verified debts return. In 2023 the CFPB won a judgment of roughly $2.7 billion against the companies behind CreditRepair.com and Lexington Law, the largest operation in the industry, and the court banned them from telemarketing credit repair services, a penalty the size of a small nation's budget, levied on a single scam that ran for years in plain sight.



The deeper problem is structural. Everything a legitimate credit repair company does, disputing errors and negotiating with creditors, a consumer can do free, and the FTC says so. What the industry actually sells is the complexity of the system itself, the same dispute maze the credit bureaus built, repackaged as a service at a hundred dollars a month. Regulation exists on paper, the Credit Repair Organizations Act is three decades old, but enforcement arrives after the fees are collected, one judgment per era, while new brands incorporate down the street.



Conclusion:

An industry that profits by navigating a broken system has no interest in the system being fixed, and an industry that preys on the desperate to sell them their own rights back is not a service sector. It is a toll booth on the road out of poverty, and the toll is collected monthly, in advance, from the people least able to pay for directions.





### 176. Debt Collection Harassment

Roughly one in four American adults with a credit file has a debt in collections, and the industry that pursues them operates at the edge of the law as a matter of routine. The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act prohibits threats, deception, and harassment, and the CFPB's complaint database fills annually with exactly those: calls to workplaces and relatives, threats of arrest that no collector can lawfully make, demands for debts that are inflated, already paid, time-barred, or someone else's entirely. When collectors sue, as they do in industrial volume, the overwhelming majority of consumers never respond, often because they were never properly served, and the courts enter default judgments that convert an unproven claim into wage garnishment with the full force of the state.



The debts themselves are frequently unverifiable by design. Portfolios of charged-off accounts are sold and resold for pennies, shedding documentation at every transfer, until the collector demanding payment cannot prove the amount or the owner. The consumer, unrepresented and outmatched, pays anyway, because the judgment machinery of item 106 does not care which side can afford a lawyer. Medical debt, the residue of Part 1, is among the largest categories in the machine, which means the collection industry is where America's healthcare failures go to be garnished.



Conclusion:

Debt collection in America is not the orderly settlement of accounts. It is a pressure industry whose profits scale with fear and confusion, aimed at people whose poverty is the reason the debt exists. A legal system that lends its courts and garnishment orders to claims no one has proven has not privatized justice. It has subcontracted intimidation and stamped it with a seal.





### 177. Bankruptcy Law Rigged Against Individuals

Bankruptcy is the legal system's pressure valve, and the valve opens in different directions depending on who is drowning. When Congress rewrote the law in 2005, in a bill the credit card industry had pursued for years, it imposed a means test and new paperwork burdens on individual filers, on the theory that the system's great problem was abuse by the desperate. The same code leaves corporate Chapter 11 broad and flexible, a tool sophisticated firms use strategically, shedding pensions, contracts, and liabilities while continuing operations, sometimes repeatedly. And above the code entirely sit the bailouts: in 2008 the federal government committed hundreds of billions to rescue the financial institutions whose conduct had detonated the economy, while the homeowners they had defrauded were routed into the means test.



One category of individual debt is fenced off almost absolutely. Student loans, per item 48, are nearly impossible to discharge in bankruptcy absent a showing of undue hardship so demanding that for decades almost no one attempted it, a protection Congress extended to private student loans in the same 2005 law that tightened the screws on everyone else. The hierarchy is written in statute: the corporation's debts are negotiable, the bank's losses are public, and the borrower's loan for a bachelor's degree is forever.



Conclusion:

A bankruptcy code is a society's confession of whose failure it forgives. America's forgives the boardroom reflexively and the kitchen table grudgingly, after a means test, with the largest household debt of the young exempted from mercy altogether. The law did not drift into this shape. It was lobbied there, over decades, by the creditors who collect on both sides of it.





### 178. Emergency Loans Create Lifelong Chains

The Federal Reserve's annual survey of household economics keeps returning the same number in different clothes: roughly four in ten American adults could not cover a $400 emergency expense with cash or its equivalent. That is the kindling. The fire is the cascade that follows the first desperate borrowing: the car repair goes on the credit card at 24 percent, the minimum payments crowd out the utility bill, the utility fee triggers the overdraft, the overdraft triggers the payday loan of item 166, and a $600 transmission has become a five-year sentence. KFF's research puts a face on the largest single accelerant: about 100 million American adults carry some form of healthcare debt, roughly $195 billion in aggregate, owed not for luxuries but for the crime of getting sick in the system described in Part 1.



None of the steps in the cascade is irrational. Each is the best available move for a household with no cushion, which is precisely the indictment: the economy offers its most vulnerable members a staircase in which every step down is individually sensible and the staircase only goes down. Savings would break the chain, but item 130's asset limits punish savings for the disabled, item 88's wage floor makes savings arithmetically fanciful for millions, and the emergency itself is not optional. The chain is not a series of bad decisions. It is an environment.



Conclusion:

Financial counselors call it a debt spiral, as if it were weather. It is architecture. A country where four in ten adults are one car repair from the cascade has not failed to teach resilience. It has built an economy with no floor under the ordinary accident, and then sold the falling a series of ropes, each one priced, each one attached to the next.





### 179. Bank Account Closures Without Cause

Access to a bank account is the entry ticket to modern economic life, and it is issued and revoked by a private scoring system most Americans have never heard of. ChexSystems and its competitors compile records of closed accounts, overdrafts, and suspected fraud, and banks use those records to deny new accounts, sometimes for a single old overdraft or an unpaid fee the customer never knew about. The FDIC's surveys count millions of unbanked households, about 4 percent of all households in the most recent survey, with many more underbanked, and a past banking mistake is among the documented reasons families remain outside the system for years.



Exile from banking is not an inconvenience. It is a tax schedule: check cashing fees on every paycheck, money order fees on every bill, no direct deposit, no savings vehicle, no path to the credit history that item 168 demands, and a standing invitation to the payday and rent-to-own storefronts of this section. Regulators have sanctioned banks for closure and screening practices that swept up the innocent, and some institutions have begun offering second-chance accounts, but the default remains a system in which the punishment for one bad month can be five years of financial exile, imposed without a hearing, by a database.



Conclusion:

A society that requires a bank account to participate and lets a private bureau revoke participation over a $35 fee has privatized citizenship's plumbing. The unbanked are not outside the economy. They are underneath it, paying its highest prices with the fewest protections, in a shadow system that exists because the front door was locked and never appealed.





### 180. Financial Literacy Is Systematically Denied

Americans are expected to navigate the most complex consumer financial system on Earth, credit scores, capitalized interest, adjustable rates, forfeiture clauses, the entire machinery of this part of the audit, and for generations the country made almost no effort to teach them how. Until a recent wave of state reforms, only a handful of states required high school students to take a personal finance course, and the subject remains absent or elective across much of the country even now, as roughly half the states have moved to guarantee it. The predictable result is a population signing contracts it cannot parse, priced by algorithms it has never heard of, in a marketplace whose complexity is not accidental.



That last point deserves emphasis, because the ignorance is profitable to everyone except the ignorant. Industries spend heavily to keep financial products opaque, and the same legislatures lobbied against rate caps and fee rules have historically underfunded the consumer education that might have built a constituency for them. Even the recent reforms carry the tell: financial literacy is framed as the borrower's responsibility, a curriculum fix for a structural problem, as if a teenager's semester on compound interest were a match for the legal departments of the institutions in items 166 through 179. Knowledge matters, and this audit does not mock it. But literacy was never the missing half of the equation. Power was, and the curriculum was withheld by the same hands that hold it.



Conclusion:

Denying people an education in the system that governs their economic lives is not an oversight in the syllabus. It is a load-bearing feature of the system, because an informed borrower is a worse customer for nearly every product in this section. Teach every child how interest compounds and half the storefronts in Part 12 close within a generation, which is the most concise explanation available for why it took this long to start.





## PART 13: EDUCATION SYSTEM FAILURES (181–195)





### 181. College Degrees No Longer Guarantee Jobs

The deal was explicit: borrow the money, earn the degree, and the economy will make it right. For a large share of graduates the last clause never arrives. Research from the Strada Institute and Burning Glass found that about 52 percent of college graduates are underemployed in their first job, working positions that do not require the degree they financed, and that the trap is durable: roughly 45 percent are still underemployed a decade later. A graduate who starts underemployed tends to stay there, earning closer to a high school wage while carrying a graduate's debt, the worst cell in the spreadsheet the recruiters never showed them.



The degree still pays on average, which is exactly how the system defends itself: the average conceals the distribution. Outcomes vary enormously by institution, major, and, above all, by the wealth a graduate can fall back on while searching, interning, and relocating. The graduate with family money can afford the unpaid internship and the extra year of looking. The graduate with a loan payment cannot, and takes the first job offered, and the divergence compounds for a decade. Education was sold as the great equalizer. Financed by debt and sorted by family resources, it increasingly functions as a great reproducer.



Conclusion:

A credential that half its holders cannot convert into commensurate work is not a guarantee. It is a lottery ticket sold at a fixed, ruinous price, with the odds posted nowhere. The country told its young that the degree was the one safe bet, collected the tuition up front, and left them holding the variance. That is not an opportunity structure. It is a transfer, from the hopeful to the institutions, with interest.





### 182. For-Profit Schools Exploit the Vulnerable

The for-profit college sector built its business on a simple discovery: federal student aid and GI Bill benefits can be harvested by enrolling people, whether or not they ever graduate or work. At its peak the sector enrolled roughly a tenth of American college students while accounting for a wildly disproportionate share of loan defaults, around a third to nearly half depending on the year, because its students, recruited hardest among veterans, single mothers, and low-income workers, were left with the debt and without the credential. The collapses wrote the record in headlines: Corinthian Colleges in 2015 and ITT Technical Institute in 2016, tens of thousands of students stranded mid-degree amid findings of falsified job placement rates and predatory recruiting, and billions in borrower defense discharges as the government conceded the students had been defrauded.



The targeting of veterans was structural, not incidental. A loophole in the rule requiring for-profits to get at least 10 percent of revenue from non-federal sources excluded GI Bill and Pentagon tuition money from the federal side of the ledger, making every veteran worth more to recruiters than any civilian. Congress finally closed the loophole in 2021, decades after it was mapped. The sector that remains is smaller and somewhat better policed, and the model it pioneered, federal money in, marketing out, outcomes optional, stands as the purest case study in this audit of what happens when public funds meet private extraction with the student as the conduit.



Conclusion:

These schools did not sell education. They sold enrollment, to the government, using the aspirations of the poor as the delivery mechanism. The students were never the customers. They were the product being billed, and when the scheme collapsed, they were left holding the one asset that could not be discharged, the debt, while the executives kept the decade's proceeds. A system defrauded is a scandal. A system designed to be defrauded is a policy.





### 184. Rural Schools Are Dying

Nearly one in five American public school students, more than nine million children, attends a rural school, and rural education is being slowly strangled by arithmetic. Funding formulas built on enrollment pay districts per student while their costs, a bus fleet, a physics teacher, a roof, do not shrink proportionally, so every lost family tightens the vise: cut the advanced courses, share the counselor among three towns, close the school whose closure saves money and ends the community's center of gravity. Thousands of rural schools have been consolidated away, and the savings arrive with children on buses for an hour or more each way, a commute no suburban parent would accept imposed on the children with the least political representation in the statehouse.



The shortages compound. Rural districts cannot match urban salaries, so they run chronic vacancies in math, science, and special education, rely on emergency-certified teachers, and lose their best staff to the suburbs. Broadband gaps leave rural students on the wrong side of every digital assignment, a divide the pandemic exposed in photographs of children doing homework in fast food parking lots. The graduates who leave for college mostly do not return, because the economy their education prepared them for no longer exists where they grew up, and each departure lowers the enrollment that funds the school that produced them.



Conclusion:

The death of a rural school is rarely announced as a policy. It arrives as a budget line, a consolidation vote, a route change, and by the time the building is a storage facility the town has lost its largest employer, its gathering place, and its argument that a young family might stay. A nation that lets its rural schools die of arithmetic has decided that some children's futures are a rounding error, and it will find, as the towns empty, that the arithmetic was the decision all along.





### 185. Advanced Classes Gatekept by Race & Wealth

Gifted programs and advanced classes are the fast lane of American public education, and admission to the lane is rationed by race and income. Federal civil rights data show Black students making up about 15 percent of public school enrollment but only about 8 percent of gifted enrollment, with similar gaps for Latino and low-income students. The standard screening method explains the outcome: referral, usually by a teacher or parent, a process that reliably finds the children of parents who know to ask, speak the system's language, and can advocate in the right rooms, while overlooking the equally able child whose family does not know the door exists.



The proof that the gate, not the talent, is the problem comes from a famous natural experiment. When a large Florida district switched from referrals to universal screening, testing every student, the identification of gifted Black and Hispanic students roughly tripled and low-income identification soared, without lowering standards, as researchers David Card and Laura Giuliano documented. The children had been there all along. The referral system had simply been unable to see them, which is the most polite available description of a filter that finds giftedness where it expects to find it. Districts that keep referral-based systems after this research are choosing the filter with their eyes open.



Conclusion:

Advanced education in America is allocated less by ability than by the advocacy budget of a child's parents, and then the allocation is cited as evidence of where ability lives. The gatekept classroom is where the class system of item 60 begins, in third grade, with a test some children are never offered. Universal screening costs little and finds thousands of missing minds. Its rarity is the confession.





### 187. Lack of Trade School Pathways

For two generations, American education policy has run a single advertisement: college for all. In its name, high schools dismantled their shop classes, wood, metal, auto, welding, the rooms where a different kind of intelligence was honored, and replaced them with a curriculum aimed at a bachelor's degree that, per item 181, half its earners cannot convert into matching work. The trades did not disappear. The pipeline into them did, and the country now faces the predictable shortage: construction industry surveys have found the overwhelming majority of firms reporting difficulty filling craft positions, and the existing skilled workforce is aging toward retirement faster than apprentices replace it.



The economics mock the stigma. Many trades pay middle-class wages without middle-class debt, union electricians and plumbers out-earning large shares of bachelor's graduates, and the work cannot be outsourced or automated at anything like the pace of office work. Federal support exists through the Perkins Act at a scale of roughly a billion and a half dollars a year, a rounding error beside the student loan portfolio, distributed across every high school and community college program in the nation. The message to the mechanically gifted teenager is consistent from every direction: your talent is a consolation prize. Then the country wonders why nothing gets built, fixed, or wired on schedule.



Conclusion:

A society that steers every child toward a desk and treats the trades as failure has engineered its own shortage of the people who keep the water running and the lights on. The dismantled shop class is education policy as class prejudice, and the bill arrives as unfilled jobs, delayed infrastructure, and a generation of indebted graduates who were never told the other door existed, or that it opened onto a better life than the one they borrowed for.





### 192. Tuition Increases Outpace Inflation 5x

Since the late 1970s, the price of college tuition in the United States has risen by more than 1,200 percent, roughly four to five times the overall rate of inflation over the same period, while the wages of young workers barely moved. No other essential good in American life has a price curve like it. Healthcare rivals it, which is not company any sector should want. The drivers are documented and mutually reinforcing: states cut per-student funding for public universities across decades and let tuition backfill the loss, administrative layers multiplied, and an amenities arms race for affluent students repriced the whole institution around the customers who could pay, leaving the working-class student to finance the climbing wall.



The consequence is that even the public option, the land-grant university built explicitly for the children of farmers and mechanics, now posts a sticker price that requires most families to borrow. Published in-state tuition and fees at public four-year schools average over $11,000 a year before a single book, meal, or mile of rent, and the total cost of attendance runs two to three times that. Each generation is told the increase is the cost of excellence. Each generation can also do the arithmetic of item 48: the excellence appreciates, the debt compounds, and the wages that were supposed to outrun both do neither.



Conclusion:

When the price of the ladder rises five times faster than the price of everything else, the ladder is being withdrawn, one cohort at a time, while everyone involved insists it is still there. Tuition inflation is not a natural force. It is the sum of funding decisions made by legislatures and priorities set by institutions, and its function in the economy is precise: to convert the public's universities into a toll on the ambitions of the poor, collected before the first class begins.





### 193. School Districts Funded by Property Taxes

Item 46 named the funding gap. This entry is about its legal architecture, because the gap is not an accident of geography. It is protected by one of the most consequential Supreme Court decisions most Americans have never heard of. In San Antonio Independent School District v. Rodriguez, decided in 1973, the Court held 5 to 4 that education is not a fundamental right under the U.S. Constitution and that funding schools by local property wealth, however unequal the results, does not violate the Equal Protection Clause. The plaintiffs were Mexican-American parents from a poor district taxing itself at a higher rate than the wealthy district nearby and raising a fraction of the money. The Court told them the Constitution had nothing to say about it.



The structure the decision blessed persists: roughly 45 percent of school funding is local, overwhelmingly from property taxes, so the wealth of a child's neighbors sets the budget for the child's mind, and state formulas meant to equalize routinely underfill the gap. Litigants have spent fifty years routing around Rodriguez through state constitutions, winning adequacy rulings in state after state, each victory an admission that the federal floor is no floor at all. Meanwhile the geography compounds itself: good schools raise property values, property values fund good schools, and the family's first educational decision becomes the purchase of a house in the right district, a tuition payment disguised as a mortgage.



Conclusion:

Rodriguez is the load-bearing ruling of American educational inequality: the day the Supreme Court announced that a child's constitutional claim to a fair education ends at the district line. Everything in items 46 and 184 flows downhill from it. A country may fund schools by property wealth if it chooses. It may not then call the results a meritocracy, because the merit was assigned by the assessor before the child was born.





### 194. Armed Guards Instead of Therapists

The ACLU's analysis of federal education data, published as Cops and No Counselors, counted the priorities of American schools in personnel: about 1.7 million students attend schools that employ police but no counselor, 3 million attend schools with police but no social worker, and 14 million attend schools with a police officer but no counselor, nurse, psychologist, or social worker at all. The recommended ratio of students to school counselors is 250 to 1. The national reality runs far above it, past 400 to 1 in many states, when the counselor exists at all. The security budget is found. The care budget is aspirational.



The trade is not neutral. A school with an officer and no counselor has arranged to respond to its students' worst days with arrest rather than help, which feeds the pipeline of item 47 with the efficiency of a system designed for it, while the anxiety, grief, trauma, and suicidal thinking that counselors exist to catch proceed uncaught in a generation whose mental health statistics worsen every year they are measured. After every school shooting the national debate funds the hardware again, hardening, guards, detectors, while the unglamorous intervention that addresses the despair upstream of violence, and the thousand ordinary crises that never make the news, waits for a budget cycle that never comes.



Conclusion:

A school reveals its theory of children in its staffing. Police without counselors says the theory is control: the child as a threat to be managed rather than a person to be developed. The money exists, because it is being spent, on the management. What is missing is not funding but the belief that a struggling child is a patient before he is a suspect, and until the hiring reflects that belief, the building is not a school with a security problem. It is a facility with an education program attached.





## PART 14: INFRASTRUCTURE NEGLECT (196–210)





### 196. Bridges Are Crumbling

More than 42,000 American bridges are rated in poor condition, and roughly a third of the nation's bridges need repair or replacement, according to federal highway data and the American Road and Transportation Builders Association's analyses. Americans cross the deficient structures some 167 million times a day, commuting over steel and concrete whose inspections have been generating warnings for decades. The backlog is not a surprise event. It is a maintenance debt accumulated one deferred budget at a time, by officials who understood that a bridge's deterioration is invisible until the day it is not.



The day arrived in Pittsburgh in January 2022, when the Fern Hollow Bridge collapsed into a ravine on a snowy morning, injuring ten people and dropping a city bus into the wreckage, hours before the President arrived in town to promote the infrastructure law meant to address exactly this. It arrived in Minneapolis in 2007, when the I-35W bridge fell into the Mississippi River during rush hour and killed thirteen. Between the collapses, the engineers' report cards keep issuing the same grade and the appropriations keep arriving late and short, because ribbon cuttings photograph better than rebar inspections and every legislature prefers to fund the new thing over the standing one.



Conclusion:

A bridge is a promise made in steel: cross here, the state has done the math. Forty-two thousand of them are rated poor by the state's own inspectors, which means the promise is being kept on credit. Infrastructure neglect is the audit's slowest item and its most literal: a government that will not maintain what it built will eventually bury someone under it, and call the funeral an unforeseeable event.





### 197. Lead in School Drinking Water

There is no federal law requiring most American schools to test their drinking water for lead, and the predictable result is that many do not know what is in their fountains. When the Government Accountability Office surveyed the question in 2018, it found that among the districts that had tested, more than a third detected elevated lead levels, while a large share of districts had never tested at all or could not say. The EPA's own guidance for schools is voluntary. The contaminant at issue attacks the developing brain, has no safe exposure level per item 64, and is delivered in the one building the law compels children to attend.



The districts that find lead then face the second failure: remediation money. Wealthy districts bond and replace. Poor districts, the same ones carrying items 46 and 184, issue letters, shut off fountains, and hand out bottled water while capital campaigns crawl, so the children most likely to live with lead paint and lead pipes at home are also the most likely to drink lead at school. Some states have finally imposed testing and remediation mandates with funding attached. Most have not, and in those states the official policy toward a known neurotoxin in school water is to not look, which guarantees not finding, which is recorded as safety.



Conclusion:

The compulsory schoolhouse is where the state's duty of care should be at its maximum, and on lead in water the state's position is that ignorance is compliance. A child cannot learn on a damaged brain, and a government that declines to test the water in its own schools has chosen the cheapest available relationship to that fact: distance from it.





### 201. Water Main Breaks Wasting Billions

The American Society of Civil Engineers estimates roughly 240,000 water main breaks a year in the United States, one every two minutes or so, and the drinking water lost through leaks in aging systems runs to billions of gallons a day, on the order of a sixth of all treated water never reaching a tap. The pipes doing the failing were laid generations ago, much of the network at or past its intended service life, and municipalities replace them at a pace that guarantees the backlog grows: at recent replacement rates, some systems would need centuries to renew themselves.



Each break is a small local emergency with the audit's usual distribution. Streets flood, neighborhoods lose pressure, boil-water advisories go out, and the costs land on ratepayers in systems least able to spread them, including the shrinking cities whose pipes were sized for populations that left decades ago. The treated water pouring into the ground was captured, cleaned, and pumped at public expense, which makes the loss a double payment: once for the water, once for the failure. Engineers have priced the national drinking water infrastructure need in the trillions over coming decades. The appropriations, as ever, arrive in millions and in press releases.



Conclusion:

A water system is the oldest promise a city makes, older than its schools and roads, and America is keeping it at a rate of one rupture every two minutes. The breaks are not mysteries. They are the sound of deferred maintenance reaching its term, and every gallon lost was paid for by the public twice, which is the closest thing this audit has to a motto.





### 202. Outdated Electrical Grids

The American electrical grid is a twentieth-century machine being asked to survive a twenty-first-century climate. Much of its transmission infrastructure is decades old, the Department of Energy has long noted that most transmission lines and transformers are past the midpoint of their expected lives, and weather has become the grid's principal enemy: analyses of outage data find that major power outages grew sharply more frequent in the 2010s than in the 2000s, with the overwhelming majority weather-related. The grid fails in heat waves when demand peaks, in ice storms when lines fall, in hurricanes when the water rises, and in each failure the same populations surface in the casualty reports: the elderly in uncooled apartments, patients on powered medical devices, families whose food and medicine spoil in warm refrigerators.



February 2021 wrote the case study in Texas, where a winter storm collapsed a grid that had been designed, by deliberate state choice, to operate in isolation from national interconnections and without winterization mandates. The state's official count put the dead at 246, with independent estimates higher, many from hypothermia in their own homes and carbon monoxide from desperate heating. Four years of warnings before the storm, issued after a similar freeze in 2011, had produced recommendations instead of requirements. The grid's vulnerability is not a secret kept from its operators. It is a risk they priced, accepted, and passed to the public, which paid it in the usual currency.



Conclusion:

Electricity is the load-bearing utility: water, heat, medicine, communication, and money all ride on it. A grid that fails in every predictable extreme is not experiencing bad luck. It is expressing its design standards, and those standards were set by people who will never freeze in the dark when they are tested. Modernization is slow because the costs of failure are externalized onto the dead, and the dead do not attend rate hearings.





### 204. Collapsing Sewage Systems

The Environmental Protection Agency has estimated that America's aging combined sewer systems discharge on the order of 850 billion gallons of untreated sewage a year into the nation's waters during storms, and in the places where the systems were never built at all, the sewage does not reach a river. It reaches the yard. Lowndes County, Alabama, a poor, majority-Black county, became the emblem: residents living with straight pipes running raw sewage from their homes onto the ground, because the soil will not support septic systems they cannot afford and no public sewer ever came. A 2017 study by researchers at Baylor found evidence of hookworm, a parasite of poverty thought eradicated from the American South, in Lowndes County residents. The county's response, for years, included arresting residents for the crime of having sewage they had no lawful, affordable way to dispose of.



The federal government eventually conceded the frame. In 2023 the Justice Department reached a landmark environmental justice settlement with Alabama's health department, finding that the state had discriminated against Black residents in its handling of sanitation in Lowndes County. The settlement is a beginning measured against a national map of failing wastewater systems, underfunded by the same arithmetic as items 196 and 201, in which the places with the least tax base inherit the oldest pipes and the worst water. Sewage is civilization's first technology, older than writing. Its failure in the richest country on Earth is not a technical problem awaiting a solution. It is a priority that keeps losing to everything else.



Conclusion:

Raw sewage in an American yard is not a third-world condition that wandered north. It is a domestic policy outcome, produced by funding formulas, enforced against the victims for years, and remedied only when the embarrassment became federal. A nation's infrastructure is its real constitution, written in pipe, and in Lowndes County the text read plainly: these citizens are on their own, and may be punished for it.





### 205. Natural Disaster Response Gaps

Item 70 examined FEMA's machinery. The distribution of what the machinery delivers is its own indictment. Research by sociologists, including Junia Howell's work on federal disaster aid, has found that disaster assistance in America does not merely fail to close the gap between rich and poor survivors. It widens it: in counties receiving substantial FEMA aid, white residents' wealth tends to grow in the following years while Black residents' wealth declines, because aid flows through property values, insurance, and the capacity to navigate the process, all of which are pre-sorted by the inequalities the disaster supposedly interrupted. The storm is egalitarian for an afternoon. The recovery is a sorting machine that runs for a decade.



The pattern repeats at every scale. Buyout programs acquire in wealthier areas at better prices. Small Business Administration disaster loans, a primary recovery channel, are loans, offered to people whose credit the disaster just destroyed. Renters, who own nothing the formulas recognize, receive a fraction of the attention homeowners command. And at the far end of the distribution sit Puerto Rico and the other territories, whose treatment after Hurricane Maria, a death toll in the thousands and a recovery paced in years, demonstrated that the response gap has a citizenship tier underneath the income tier.



Conclusion:

Disaster policy in America functions as an amplifier: it takes the inequality that existed the day before the storm and returns it enlarged, stamped with the seal of emergency relief. Until aid is allocated by need rather than by property and paperwork, every hurricane will do double duty, destroying the poor family's home and then financing the rich family's appreciation, and the word recovery will remain a description of the aggregate that conceals the transfer.





### 206. Schools Without Air Conditioning

A 2020 Government Accountability Office report estimated that about 41 percent of school districts needed to update or replace heating, ventilation, and air conditioning systems in at least half of their schools, representing tens of thousands of buildings. As the climate warms, the school calendar, designed around an agricultural century, now opens in August heat that earlier generations scheduled around, and districts across the country cancel days, dismiss early, and move classes during heat waves that were once rare and are now a season. The students sweat in rooms where the research says learning measurably degrades: economists studying test scores have found that hotter school years reduce achievement, with air conditioning offsetting most of the loss, and the burden falling hardest on Black and Hispanic students, whose schools are the least likely to be cooled.



The health risks ride along with the academic ones. Children are more vulnerable to heat illness than adults, student athletes practice in the same heat that has killed players on American fields, and the nurse's office of item 194, where it exists, becomes a triage station. Wealthy districts have responded the way wealthy districts do, with bond issues and modern systems. Poor districts patch window units and pray for September, which means the temperature of a child's classroom is becoming one more variable in the funding equation of item 46, set by the same property wealth, rising with the same thermometer.



Conclusion:

A country that cannot keep its classrooms below dangerous temperatures has not encountered an engineering problem. It has encountered its own reflection: the buildings are old because the funding is rationed, the heat is rising because the emissions were ungoverned, and the children absorbing both failures are, as usual, the ones with the least say in either. The thermometer is becoming a school funding formula, and it is the most regressive one yet devised.





### 208. Lack of Drinking Water in Tribal Lands

The United States holds a treaty and trust responsibility for the water of Native nations, and the plumbing tells the story of its performance. A 2019 report by the U.S. Water Alliance and DigDeep estimated that more than two million people in the United States lack running water and complete plumbing, and that Native American households are nineteen times more likely than white households to lack it. On the Navajo Nation, roughly a third of residents have no running water at home, hauling it by truck from communal taps, in a region where the federal government found no difficulty moving water for uranium mines, coal plants, and distant cities during the same decades.



The consequences are the ones this audit has catalogued in every dry system: waterborne illness, impossible hygiene during a pandemic that demanded handwashing, hours of labor, disproportionately women's labor, spent hauling the basic input of life, and health costs billed to the Indian Health Service of item 12. Congress has begun appropriating serious money, including billions for tribal sanitation in the 2021 infrastructure law, against a documented need the Indian Health Service itself has priced far higher, and the projects move at the pace of a backlog a century in the making. The legal architecture adds insult in the West, where tribes hold senior water rights on paper that were never quantified or delivered, a promise of water as theoretical as the treaties were specific.



Conclusion:

Nineteen times more likely to lack running water is not a disparity. It is a relationship, between the first nations of this continent and the government that signed treaties with them, expressed in plumbing. The water exists. The rights exist, in writing, ratified. What has never existed is the priority, and a trust responsibility honored at this pace is indistinguishable from its breach, which, in the language of this audit's closing argument, is precisely what it is.





### 210. Infrastructure Projects Displace the Poor

The interstate highway system is celebrated as America's greatest public works achievement, and its route maps were drawn through the neighborhoods with the least power to object. Historians and the Transportation Department itself have documented that the highways displaced more than a million people, overwhelmingly in Black communities: the Claiborne Expressway driven through Tremé in New Orleans, I-81 through the 15th Ward in Syracuse, I-35 as a racial boundary in Austin, whole commercial districts, the Black business corridors of a hundred cities, condemned and paved. James Baldwin named the era's governing program in 1963: urban renewal, he said, means Negro removal. The stadiums came later and smaller, billions in public subsidy for facilities whose own economists' consensus says they do not repay the public investment, rising on land cleared the same way.



The mechanism never required villains in the room. It required only a routing process that priced land and political resistance, and followed the gradient downhill, which in America always runs through the same zip codes as items 43, 65, and 71. The displaced received the era's version of fair compensation, the value of property in a neighborhood the project itself had just devalued, and the wealth destroyed, homes, businesses, social networks built over generations, does not appear in the highway program's official history of itself. Current federal programs now fund a handful of reconnection and removal projects, tearing down a few of the viaducts, a remedy whose scale, set against a million displaced, is best described as symbolic.



Conclusion:

Every era of American building has sent its bill to the same addresses. The highway through the neighborhood was not an unfortunate route. It was the route, chosen because the people in the way were the people least able to make it expensive to choose them. Infrastructure is memory in concrete: it remembers exactly who the country thought could be moved, and the descendants of the moved are still paying the mortgage on that memory, in longer commutes, divided neighborhoods, and wealth that was bulldozed before it could be inherited.





## PART 15: CIVIL RIGHTS & SYSTEMIC NEGLECT (211–220)





### 213. Reproductive Rights Stripped Across States

In June 2022 the Supreme Court decided Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization and withdrew a constitutional right Americans had held for half a century, returning abortion law to the states. Roughly a dozen states moved to ban abortion almost entirely, several more to restrict it severely, and the exceptions written into the bans, for the life of the mother, for rape, for fatal fetal conditions, have repeatedly failed the women who tried to use them, because hospitals and doctors, facing prison in the statutes, wait until a patient is sick enough to satisfy a prosecutor. The litigation and the reporting since 2022 are full of women who were denied care during miscarriages and nonviable pregnancies until they became emergencies, and of patients airlifted across state lines by hospitals unwilling to treat them at home.



The mortality ledger is already open. A Johns Hopkins study of Texas, which banned abortion in 2021 ahead of Dobbs, found that infant deaths in the state rose nearly 13 percent in the following year, driven substantially by deaths from congenital anomalies, babies carried to term because the law required it and buried because the condition was unsurvivable. Maternal care is thinning in ban states as obstetricians leave or never come, a workforce effect documented in surveys and state reporting, worsening the deserts of item 10. The right's withdrawal is also stratified in the usual direction: women with money travel. Women without it are governed.



Conclusion:

Whatever one's moral position on abortion, the legal fact is an entry in this audit: a right held for fifty years was withdrawn, and the withdrawal's costs are being paid in emergency rooms, in infant mortality tables, and in the compelled pregnancies of women too poor to leave their state. A social contract in which the terms can be revised downward for half the population, by geography, is the thing this entire audit exists to describe. The states now running the experiment are collecting the data in their citizens, and the data are not ambiguous about who bleeds.





### 215. Indigenous Women Disappear Without Justice

Murder is the third leading cause of death for Indigenous women in America, and the federal government's own research found that more than four in five Indigenous women, 84 percent, have experienced violence in their lifetimes, with a majority experiencing sexual violence. Against that background, the systems meant to count and investigate the missing have failed at the level of arithmetic. The Urban Indian Health Institute's 2018 report identified 5,712 cases of missing and murdered Indigenous women and girls in a single year, 2016, of which only 116 appeared in the Department of Justice's missing persons database. Thousands of women, uncounted by the government that holds trust responsibility for their nations, in a database that is the first step to anyone looking for them.



The jurisdictional maze does the rest. A 1978 Supreme Court decision stripped tribal courts of criminal authority over non-Indians, who commit the large majority of violence against Indigenous women, leaving prosecution to federal and state authorities whose declination rates in Indian Country cases have been documented by the GAO as staggering, roughly half of violent crime cases declined by U.S. Attorneys in studied years. Congress partially restored tribal jurisdiction in the Violence Against Women Act reauthorizations of 2013 and 2022, and passed Savanna's Act and the Not Invisible Act in 2020 to force better data and coordination. The families who light the search parties themselves, who print the posters and walk the highways, report the same experience across decades: the system does not come unless they drag it.



Conclusion:

A woman can vanish from a reservation and fall into a gap between three governments, each pointing at the next, in a country that found the jurisdiction to take the land, the children, and the minerals without any confusion about authority. The maze is not an accident of federalism. It is what a trust responsibility looks like when it is honored the way item 208's water was honored, and the missing are its measure, counted at last by their grandmothers because the state would not count them itself.





### 216. ICE Detentions of Children in Cages

In 2018, the United States government adopted a policy of separating migrant children from their parents at the southern border as a deterrent, and executed it without a system to reunite them. Court proceedings established that at least 5,500 children were separated under the policy and its predecessors, some of them infants, some of them lost in a bureaucracy that had recorded no link between the child flown to one state and the parent deported to another country. Years later, a federal task force was still working to reunify families, with roughly a thousand or more children not yet restored to their parents years after the separations, a figure that converts the phrase family separation from a policy into a duration measured in childhoods.



The detention itself became an icon for a reason. Photographs from Border Patrol processing facilities showed children held in enclosures of chain-link fencing, sleeping on concrete under foil blankets, in facilities built for short-term processing of adults and used, in documented violation of the spirit and often the letter of the Flores settlement's limits on detaining minors, for days and weeks. Independent monitors and physicians documented inadequate food, sanitation, medical care, and the deaths of children in custody. The legal architecture that permits child detention at all survives every administration, moderated or intensified by policy, which means the cages are not a scandal that ended. They are a capacity that persists, awaiting instructions.



Conclusion:

A government that separates toddlers from their parents as a messaging strategy has announced the value it assigns to both, and a government that then loses track of the children has added incompetence to cruelty and called the sum border enforcement. The trauma literature on separated children is unambiguous about the damage and its permanence. This item does not require the audit's framework to condemn it. It requires only the country's own stated values, read back to it slowly, in the presence of the photographs it took of itself.





### 217. Voting Rights Rolled Back by Voter ID Laws

The right to vote is the clause that enforces every other clause in the social contract, and it has been under legislative revision since the Supreme Court's 2013 decision in Shelby County v. Holder dismantled the Voting Rights Act's preclearance system, which for fifty years had required jurisdictions with histories of discrimination to prove their election changes were clean before making them. Within hours of the ruling, states began announcing restrictions that preclearance would once have blocked. In the decade since, Republican-led legislatures have enacted waves of new voting laws, 19 states passing 34 restrictive laws in 2021 alone by the Brennan Center's count, tightening identification requirements, cutting early voting, purging rolls, and restricting mail ballots, each measure defended as election integrity and each landing with documented unevenness on the poor, the elderly, students, and voters of color.



The mechanics are mundane, which is the point. Strict photo ID laws burden voters who do not drive, do not have a birth certificate handy, and cannot take a weekday off to visit a licensing office that their county may have closed. The Government Accountability Office's study of the 2012 election found that turnout in Kansas and Tennessee, which had enacted strict ID laws, fell by roughly 2 to 3 percentage points more than in comparison states, a margin larger than many elections are decided by. Fraud of the kind these laws target, in-person impersonation, is documented at rates so low they approach zero, a few dozen credible incidents in a billion ballots. The laws solve a problem that does not exist at a cost measured in citizens who do.



Conclusion:

A democracy that makes voting harder in the name of protecting voting has chosen its electorate, and the choice is the confession. Every other right in this audit depends on the ballot for its defense, which is why this item closes the original list: the rollback of voting rights is the lock being fitted to the door of the room where all the other grievances would be heard. Count the votes a restriction prevents, compare them to the fraud it catches, and the ratio is the policy's true text, readable by anyone willing to do the division.



## NEW ITEMS: THE AUDIT CONTINUES (218–567)





### 218. Workplace Deaths Treated as a Cost of Doing Business

The Bureau of Labor Statistics counted 5,283 fatal work injuries in 2023, which means a worker died on the job about every 99 minutes. Most of these deaths were preventable, and the fines that follow them are usually small enough for a company to treat as overhead.

Impact: Both





### 219. Child Labor Is Coming Back

The Labor Department found 5,766 minors employed in violation of federal child labor laws in fiscal 2023, an 88 percent increase since 2019, with violations more than doubling in the same period. While violations climb, state after state has rolled back child labor protections instead of strengthening them.

Impact: Both





### 220. Noncompete Clauses Chain Workers to Bad Jobs

The Federal Trade Commission estimated in 2024 that about 30 million American workers, roughly one in five, are bound by noncompete clauses, including fast food workers and security guards. The FTC tried to ban most of them, but a federal court killed the ban and the agency abandoned it, leaving the chains in place.

Impact: Money





### 221. No Federal Paid Sick Leave

The United States has no federal law requiring employers to give workers a single paid sick day. The Family and Medical Leave Act guarantees only unpaid leave, so millions of workers choose between a paycheck and spreading illness at work, at school, and on the bus.

Impact: Health





### 222. Farmworkers Excluded From the Labor Laws Everyone Else Gets

The National Labor Relations Act of 1935 explicitly excludes agricultural workers from the federal right to organize, and the Fair Labor Standards Act still denies most farmworkers overtime pay. The people who harvest the nation's food were written out of its basic labor protections on purpose, and Congress has never written them back in.

Impact: Both





### 223. Domestic Workers Written Out of Labor Law

The National Labor Relations Act also excludes domestic service workers, the nannies, house cleaners, and home aides who work inside other people's homes. Most of them are women, many are immigrants, and federal law still treats their workplace as if it were not a workplace at all.

Impact: Money





### 224. No Federal Right to a Meal Break

Federal law does not require employers to give adult workers a meal break or a rest break, no matter how long the shift runs. In states without their own break laws, a worker can legally be kept on the line for ten or twelve hours with no guaranteed chance to eat.

Impact: Health





### 225. Truck Drivers Exempt From Overtime

Section 13(b)(1) of the Fair Labor Standards Act, the Motor Carrier Exemption, denies overtime pay to most interstate truck drivers, no matter how many hours past forty they work. Drivers also routinely sit unpaid for hours at loading docks, waiting on freight that is not ready, because the law lets carriers treat that time as free.

Impact: Money





### 226. Salaried Workers Cheated Out of Overtime

The salary threshold below which workers are guaranteed overtime pay sat at $35,568 a year, barely above poverty wages for a family. The Labor Department raised it in 2024, but a federal judge threw the raise out nationwide, sending the line right back down and leaving millions of low-paid salaried workers working free hours.

Impact: Money





### 227. Outdoor Workers Cook With No Federal Heat Standard

There is still no finalized federal standard that requires employers to give outdoor and indoor workers water, shade, and rest breaks in dangerous heat. OSHA proposed a heat rule in 2024, decades after heat deaths became predictable, and farmworkers and roofers keep dying every summer while the rule sits unfinished.

Impact: Health





### 228. Forced Arbitration Strips Workers of Their Day in Court

The Economic Policy Institute estimated that more than 60 million American workers are covered by mandatory arbitration agreements, often signed as a condition of getting hired. When the boss steals wages or harasses them, these workers cannot go to court or join with coworkers, and the hearing happens in private, in front of an arbitrator the company helps choose.

Impact: Money





### 229. Warehouse Workers Get Hurt So Companies Can Ship Faster

Analyses of Amazon's own injury data filed with OSHA have repeatedly found that its warehouse workers suffer serious injuries at roughly twice the rate of the rest of the warehousing industry. The packages arrive faster because the pace inside is set by algorithm, and human bodies pay the difference.

Impact: Health





### 230. Meatpacking Lines Move Too Fast for Human Bodies

Federal rules cap most poultry lines at 140 birds per minute, and waivers let some plants run at up to 175 birds per minute, a speed set for machinery and not for the hands, shoulders, and wrists of the workers doing the cutting. Repetitive motion injuries and amputations are the predictable output of a line that never slows down.

Impact: Health





### 231. Temp Workers Get Hurt and Nobody Is Responsible

OSHA had to launch a special Temporary Worker Initiative because staffing agency workers kept getting injured in their first days on jobs they were never trained for. The host company says the agency is responsible, the agency says the host is, and the injured worker is left holding the medical bills.

Impact: Health





### 232. Home Health Aides Paid Poverty Wages to Care for Everyone's Parents

Home health and personal care aides do the intimate work of bathing, feeding, and lifting America's aging parents, and the Bureau of Labor Statistics puts their median pay at only about $34,000 a year. The country trusts them with its most vulnerable people and pays them like they are disposable.

Impact: Money





### 233. Childcare Workers Paid Less Than the People Who Watch Our Cars

Childcare workers, the people entrusted with the nation's toddlers during their most important years of brain development, earn a median of only about $15 an hour according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Many qualify for the same public assistance programs as the families they serve, because caring for children is treated as unskilled labor.

Impact: Money





### 234. Plant Closings Leave Towns With 60 Days or Nothing

The WARN Act only requires 60 days of warning before a mass layoff, and only from employers with 100 or more workers closing a site with 50 or more jobs lost. It requires no severance at all, and smaller shutdowns require nothing, so a town's main employer can lock the doors on a Friday and owe nobody an explanation.

Impact: Money





### 235. Misclassified Workers Lose Every Protection at Once

When a company calls an employee an independent contractor, that one label strips away minimum wage, overtime, unemployment insurance, workers' comp, and the employer's share of Social Security taxes all at the same time. Misclassification is not a paperwork error. It is a business model, and the worker absorbs every risk the company sheds.

Impact: Money





### 236. Legal Aid Turns Away Half the People Who Qualify

The Legal Services Corporation reports that its funded legal aid offices are forced to turn away 49 percent of the eligible people who ask for help because there is not enough money to take their cases. Its 2022 Justice Gap study found that low-income Americans received no legal help, or not enough of it, for 92 percent of their substantial civil legal problems.

Impact: Both





### 237. Public Defenders Buried Under Impossible Caseloads

A 2023 national public defense workload study backed by the American Bar Association found that competent defense of a single felony takes far more hours than the old caseload standards assumed. Defenders across the country still carry two or three times a defensible load, which means the constitutional right to counsel exists on paper and collapses in practice.

Impact: Both





### 238. Courts Fund Themselves by Fining the Poor

The Justice Department's 2015 investigation of Ferguson, Missouri documented a city that ran its municipal budget on fines and fees extracted from its own poorest residents through its court. Ferguson was not an outlier. It was the one that got investigated, and hundreds of towns still balance their books the same way.

Impact: Money





### 239. Innocent People Plead Guilty Because Trial Is Punished

The National Registry of Exonerations has documented hundreds of people who were later proven innocent but had pled guilty anyway, many because prosecutors offered a short sentence for a plea and threatened decades in prison if they lost at trial. When the price of insisting on your innocence is your whole life, a guilty plea stops meaning guilt.

Impact: Both





### 240. Prison Phone Calls Tax the Families of the Incarcerated

For years, a 15-minute phone call from a local jail could cost a family more than ten dollars, with the jail itself taking a cut of the profit. Congress finally passed the Martha Wright-Reed Act in 2022 and the FCC capped rates in 2024, though the FCC delayed enforcement of those caps in 2025 and moved to raise them again, and all of it came only after decades of companies and sheriffs splitting billions taken from the poorest families in the system.

Impact: Money





### 241. Probation Fees Keep People Trapped in the System

Many states charge the people on probation and parole monthly supervision fees, plus surcharges for drug tests, classes, and electronic monitoring. When a person who cannot pay falls behind, the debt itself can become a violation that extends their supervision or sends them back to jail, so poverty becomes the crime that never ends.

Impact: Money





### 242. Eyewitness Mistakes Put Innocent People in Prison

Eyewitness misidentification contributed to roughly two thirds of the wrongful convictions later overturned by DNA evidence, according to the Innocence Project. Human memory is fallible, police lineups are often suggestive, and yet a confident stranger pointing across a courtroom is still treated as some of the strongest evidence the law allows.

Impact: Both



### 243. Qualified Immunity Lets Officials Break the Law Without Paying for It

Under the judge-made doctrine of qualified immunity, a police officer or official who violates a person's constitutional rights cannot be sued unless a past court case already declared the exact same conduct illegal in nearly identical facts. Rights that exist in the Constitution evaporate in civil court because no earlier victim managed to win the same case first.

Impact: Both





### 244. Solitary Confinement Is Torture by Another Name

A 2023 report by Solitary Watch and the Unlock the Box Campaign counted about 122,840 people locked in solitary confinement for 22 or more hours a day in American prisons and jails on a given day. The United Nations treats more than 15 days of it as torture, and thousands of Americans spend years inside it.

Impact: Health





### 245. Wrongful Convictions Have Stolen More Than 35,000 Years of Human Life

The National Registry of Exonerations has documented more than 3,800 exonerations since 1989, and the people freed had collectively lost more than 35,000 years behind bars for crimes they did not commit. Those are only the cases somebody eventually proved. The registry itself calls its count a floor, not a total.

Impact: Both





### 246. Rape Kits Sit Untested While Rapists Walk Free

Investigations in city after city have found stockpiles of untested rape kits sitting in police storage rooms for years or decades, with national estimates of the backlog running into the hundreds of thousands. Every kit is a survivor who did everything right and a suspect whose DNA the government never bothered to run.

Impact: Both





### 247. People Die in Jail Waiting for a Trial They Never Got

The Bureau of Justice Statistics counted 1,200 deaths in local jails in 2019, the highest total in two decades of record keeping, with suicide as the leading single cause. Most of the people in those jails had not been convicted of the charge that put them there. They died legally innocent.

Impact: Health





### 248. Most People in Jail Have Not Been Convicted of Anything

The Prison Policy Initiative reports that roughly two thirds of the people held in America's local jails are pretrial, meaning they are locked up for being accused and too poor to buy their way out. The Constitution promises a presumption of innocence. The jail delivers the punishment first and the verdict whenever it gets around to it.

Impact: Both





### 249. Police Can Lie to Children in the Interrogation Room

In most states it is still legal for police to lie to a child during interrogation, falsely claiming they have DNA evidence or that a friend already confessed. Illinois became the first state to ban deceptive interrogation of minors in 2021, which means that for most of the country's history, and in most of the country still, tricking a scared teenager into a false confession breaks no rule at all.

Impact: Both





### 250. False Confessions Send Innocent People to Prison

The Innocence Project reports that false confessions contributed to about 29 percent of the wrongful convictions later overturned by DNA evidence. People confess to crimes they did not commit when interrogations go on for hours, when they are young or exhausted or mentally ill, and when the police are allowed to lie about the evidence. The system then treats the confession as proof.

Impact: Both





### 251. Fired Officers Win Their Jobs Back in Secret Arbitration

A Washington Post investigation of the nation's largest police departments found that about a quarter of the officers fired for misconduct were reinstated through appeals and arbitration, often with back pay. Chiefs who try to remove abusive officers get overruled in closed proceedings the public never sees, and the officer goes back on the street.

Impact: Both





### 252. Mental Health Crises Get Answered With Guns

The Treatment Advocacy Center has found that people with untreated serious mental illness are about 16 times more likely to be killed during a police encounter than other people approached by law enforcement. Families call 911 begging for help for a loved one in crisis, and the only responders the system sends carry weapons instead of training.

Impact: Health





### 253. The Government Compensates the Wrongfully Convicted in Pennies, or Not at All

There is no federal right to compensation after a wrongful conviction, and a patchwork of state laws decides what a stolen decade is worth, with some states paying nothing at all. People walk out of prison exonerated and are handed less support than the state gives people who actually committed the crime and got paroled.

Impact: Money





### 254. A Criminal Record Is a Life Sentence That Never Ends

Roughly 70 million Americans have a criminal record, and that record follows them into every job application, apartment application, and licensing office for the rest of their lives. The sentence ends. The punishment does not, because the record keeps firing them and evicting them long after any judge's work is done.

Impact: Money





### 255. Licensing Boards Lock People With Records Out of Honest Work

State and federal laws carry more than 44,000 collateral consequences that bar or burden people with criminal records, including licensing restrictions that lock them out of work as barbers, nurses, contractors, and hundreds of other licensed trades. The government tells people leaving prison to get a job, then makes it illegal for them to hold the license the job requires.

Impact: Money





### 256. Immigration Detention Is a Business Model

The large majority of people in ICE detention are held in facilities run by private prison companies or private contractors paid by the day and by the bed. When a company profits from every filled bunk, empty beds look like lost revenue, and the pressure to detain more people is built into the contract.

Impact: Money





### 257. Citizen Children Live in Fear of Losing Their Parents

The Pew Research Center has estimated that about 4.6 million United States citizen children live with at least one unauthorized immigrant parent. These children are Americans with American passports and American rights, and they go to school every day knowing the government could take their mother or father by dinner.

Impact: Both





### 258. Immigration Court Backlog Buries Cases for Years

The immigration court backlog tracked by the Transactional Records Access Clearinghouse has climbed past three million pending cases. People wait years for a hearing that decides whether they can stay in the only home they know, working and raising families in a legal limbo the government created and cannot process.

Impact: Both





### 259. Guest Workers Are Tied to One Boss by Law

Workers who come legally on H-2A and H-2B guest worker visas are bound to the single employer named on their visa. If that employer steals their wages, houses them in filth, or threatens them, quitting means losing their legal status and their ride home, so the law hands the boss a workforce that cannot walk away.

Impact: Money





### 260. The Desert Is Part of the Border Policy

More than 8,000 migrant deaths have been recorded along the southern border since 1998, and researchers agree the true number is far higher because many remains are never found. A border strategy built on pushing people into deadly terrain does not stop migration. It just makes sure some of the people who try it die of thirst where nobody can see.

Impact: Health





### 261. The Government Deports Its Own Citizens by Mistake

The Government Accountability Office found that from fiscal 2015 through early 2020, ICE arrested 674 potential United States citizens, detained 121, and removed 70 from the country. GAO also found ICE did not systematically track these cases, so even the government's own watchdog cannot say how many Americans its agents have exiled.

Impact: Both





### 262. Veterans Get Deported After Serving the Country That Deported Them

A 2019 GAO report found that ICE did not consistently follow its own policies for veterans in removal proceedings and did not even keep reliable data on how many veterans it deported. Men and women who wore the flag on their shoulder have been exiled to countries they left as children, separated from the VA care they earned.

Impact: Both





### 263. Foster Kids Age Out Into Homelessness

About 20,000 young people age out of foster care every year when the system that raised them decides they are adults, ready or not. Research from Chapin Hall has found that roughly a third of them experience homelessness by their mid twenties, because the state was their parent and the state discharged them to the street.

Impact: Both





### 264. Foster Children Are Raised by a Revolving Door

Children in foster care are routinely moved through placement after placement, changing homes, schools, and caregivers with every move. Some children endure ten or more placements before they age out, which means the system designed to give them stability instead teaches them that every home is temporary and every adult leaves.

Impact: Health





### 265. Poverty Gets Investigated as Child Neglect

Federal child welfare data show that neglect accounts for about three quarters of confirmed child maltreatment cases, far more than physical or sexual abuse. Most neglect findings trace back to poverty itself, to empty refrigerators, unstable housing, and missed appointments, so the system investigates being poor and calls it parenting failure.

Impact: Both





### 266. Grandparents Raising Grandchildren Get Less Help Than Strangers

Millions of grandparents and relatives step in to raise children whose parents cannot, and in most states a relative who takes a child informally receives a fraction of the support paid to a licensed stranger foster parent, or none at all. The family member who keeps a child out of the system is punished financially for doing the government's job for free.

Impact: Money





### 267. Child Support Debt Follows Poor Fathers Into the Grave

When poor parents fall behind on child support, states pile on interest and penalties, suspend driver's licenses, and sometimes jail them for a debt they cannot pay. Much of the money owed by the poorest parents is owed not to their children but to the government itself, as repayment for public assistance the family already received.

Impact: Money

# The Pentagon Papers: An Audit of the American Dream — Audit 3

By Mary-Victoria Crockett

This document is part of a series that presents the complete audit from "Not Yours To Take: An Audit Of The American Dream," rewritten and fact-checked, items 1 through 500. Audit 1 holds Parts 1 through 9 (items 1 to 130). Audit 2 holds Parts 10 through 15 and new items 218 to 267. This document continues the new items from 268.

## 268. Nursing Homes Run on Skeleton Crews

Federal data and staffing studies have repeatedly found that most nursing homes operate below the staffing levels researchers link to safe care, and the federal government's first minimum staffing rule, issued in 2024, was struck down in court in 2025 before it could take hold. The residents pay for the shortage in falls, bedsores, missed medications, and hours spent waiting for help that is covering too many people at once.

Impact: Both

## 269. Private Equity Buys Nursing Homes and Mortality Follows

A widely cited study of Medicare patients found that private equity ownership of nursing homes was associated with a roughly 10 percent increase in short-term mortality, along with declines in staffing and mobility. The business model buys the facility, loads it with debt, cuts the labor, and sells the real estate, and the residents, who cannot comparison shop from a hospital bed, absorb the difference in their bodies.

Impact: Both

## 270. Elder Abuse Is Everywhere and Almost Never Reported

The National Council on Aging estimates that about 1 in 10 Americans over 60 has experienced some form of elder abuse, and a landmark New York study found that for every case known to authorities, roughly 24 more were never reported. The abusers are most often family members and caregivers, the victims are often dependent on them, and the system built to catch it, Adult Protective Services, runs on caseloads that guarantee most of it stays invisible.

Impact: Both

## 271. Home Care Workers Are Paid Like Their Work Does Not Matter

The home health and personal care aides who bathe, feed, and lift America's elderly and disabled parents earn a median wage of roughly $16 an hour, with annual earnings so low that many qualify for the public assistance programs their clients also depend on. The country is projected to need millions more of these workers as it ages, and it is recruiting them with poverty wages and no benefits, then expressing surprise at the shortage.

Impact: Both

## 272. Long-Term Care Costs Families Into Bankruptcy

Genworth's annual Cost of Care survey put a private room in a nursing home at roughly $128,000 a year in 2024, and assisted living at $70,800, sums no ordinary retirement survives for long. Medicare does not cover long-term custodial care, so families spend down a lifetime of savings to the poverty line, at which point Medicaid takes over, which means the system's design requires the middle class to become poor before it will help them stay cared for.

Impact: Both

## 273. Guardianship Can Erase a Person Legally

Under guardianship, a court can strip an adult of the right to decide where to live, how to spend money, whom to see, and what medical care to accept, and hand those rights to a stranger. Government Accountability Office reports and investigative series have documented professional guardians draining estates through fees while their wards' families fight for visitation, and in many states no one can even say how many people are under guardianship, because the courts do not count.

Impact: Both

## 274. Seniors Are the Prime Target of Fraud and the System Shrugs

The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center reported that Americans over 60 lost about $3.4 billion to fraud in 2023, the highest of any age group, through tech support scams, romance scams, and government impersonation calls that spoof official numbers. Banks flag some transfers and miss most, platforms profit from the scam ads that seed the calls, and the victims, ashamed and often cognitively vulnerable, underreport exactly as item 270's victims do.

Impact: Both

## 275. Unpaid Family Caregivers Hold Up the Entire System

AARP valued the unpaid care provided by America's family caregivers at roughly $1 trillion a year in 2024, labor performed mostly by daughters, wives, and husbands who cut their own hours, drain their own savings, and damage their own health to keep parents and spouses out of the facilities in items 268 and 269. If family caregivers charged what their work is worth, the long-term care system would collapse in a week, and the government's contribution to their support is, for most of them, a pamphlet.

Impact: Both

## 276. Childcare Costs More Than College

The Economic Policy Institute has found that infant care costs more than in-state public college tuition in most states, and national surveys put the average price of center-based infant care around $15,000 a year, with two children in care exceeding the rent in much of the country. Parents are asked to pay the highest care costs of their lives at the exact moment their earnings are lowest and their sleep is shortest, and the bill arrives whether or not item 77's unpaid leave has already emptied the account.

Impact: Money

## 277. Half the Country Lives in a Childcare Desert

The Center for American Progress found that about 51 percent of Americans live in a childcare desert, a neighborhood where there are more than three young children for every licensed childcare slot, or no providers at all. The deserts cover rural counties and poor urban neighborhoods alike, and they convert directly into parents, overwhelmingly mothers, leaving jobs, cutting hours, and declining promotions, a tax on the economy that never appears in the childcare debate.

Impact: Money

## 278. Childcare Workers Earn Poverty Wages to Raise Everyone Else's Children

The people entrusted with America's toddlers earn a median wage of $15.41 an hour, less than parking lot attendants in many cities, and a large share rely on public assistance for their own families. Turnover runs so high that a toddler may cycle through several teachers a year, and the centers cannot raise pay because the parents in item 276 are already at their limit, a three-way vise in which the worker, the parent, and the child are squeezed by the same missing public investment.

Impact: Both

## 279. Childcare Subsidies Reach Only a Fraction of Eligible Children

Federal childcare assistance, the main program meant to make care affordable for low-income working families, serves only about one in six children who are eligible for it under federal rules, according to analyses of HHS data. The rest join waiting lists that in some states run to tens of thousands of names, and a parent offered a job without care available is not really being offered a job, which makes the subsidy's shortfall a quiet veto on the employment the same government demands in item 29.

Impact: Both

## 280. Head Start Turns Away Most of the Children It Exists For

Head Start, the federal preschool program for low-income children whose benefits have been documented for decades, has funding to serve only about a third of eligible three- to five-year-olds, and Early Head Start reaches only about one in ten eligible infants and toddlers. The waiting lists are not evidence that the program failed. They are evidence that it was never funded to the size of its own eligibility rules, a promise printed at full size and financed at a third.

Impact: Both

## 281. America Proved It Could Cut Child Poverty and Chose to Stop

In 2021, the expanded Child Tax Credit, paid monthly and made fully available to the poorest families, drove the child poverty rate to 5.2 percent, the lowest ever recorded, cutting it nearly in half in a single year. Congress let the expansion expire, and by 2022 the child poverty rate had more than doubled back to 12.4 percent, the largest one-year increase on record. The experiment was run, the results were published, and the policy was discontinued anyway, which makes child poverty in America a chosen condition with a measured cure.

Impact: Both

## 282. Maternal Deaths in the Richest Country Are a Scandal With Data

The United States has the highest maternal mortality rate in the wealthy world, at 17.9 deaths per 100,000 live births in 2024, down from 22.3 in 2022 but still several times the rates of peer nations, and Black women die at more than three times the rate of white women. The CDC's own reviews have concluded that more than 80 percent of pregnancy-related deaths are preventable, a word that converts every one of them from a tragedy into an item in this audit.

Impact: Health

## 283. One in Five Workers Has No Paid Sick Day at All

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reports that roughly one in five American workers, concentrated in low-wage service jobs, has no access to a single paid sick day. They choose between wages and contagion, and they choose wages, which is why the person coughing behind the counter is a policy outcome. The United States remains one of the only wealthy nations with no federal paid sick leave standard, and the illness spreads from the worker to the customer to the customer's family on a schedule set by the payroll department.

Impact: Both

## 284. Camp Lejeune's Poisoned Families Waited Half a Century

From the 1950s through the 1980s, the drinking water at Marine Corps Base Camp Lejeune in North Carolina was contaminated with industrial solvents at levels orders of magnitude above safety limits, and Marines and their families drank it, bathed in it, and buried children lost to cancers and birth defects the government spent decades declining to connect. Congress finally opened a path to compensation in the Camp Lejeune Justice Act of 2022, and the claims process has since buried the same families in backlog and litigation, so that justice at fifty years' distance arrives, for many, addressed to an estate.

Impact: Both

## 285. Bad Paper Discharges Cut Wounded Veterans Off From Care

Tens of thousands of veterans hold less-than-honorable discharges issued for misconduct that was itself a symptom of service-connected trauma, PTSD, brain injury, or military sexual trauma, and those discharges can bar them from VA healthcare and benefits entirely. The Government Accountability Office found that most service members separated for misconduct in studied years had been diagnosed with a mental health condition or brain injury shortly before separation, which means the military punished the wound and then the VA cited the punishment to deny treatment for it.

Impact: Both

## 286. The VA's Caregiver Program Cut the Caregivers

The VA's Program of Comprehensive Assistance for Family Caregivers pays stipends to the spouses and parents who provide full-time care to severely wounded veterans, and the VA spent years attempting to reassess and remove families from the program under tightened standards, cutting off households that had organized their entire lives around the care. The VA paused the removals after public outcry, suspending the reassessments and extending protections for longtime participants, but the episode taught every caregiver family the lesson of this audit: even the benefit designed for the people holding the system together is administered as a cost to be trimmed.

Impact: Both

## 287. Veterans' Nursing Homes and Clinics Decay Off Camera

The VA operates nursing homes, domiciliary programs, and hundreds of clinics in facilities whose maintenance backlogs have drawn repeated inspector general findings, from infection control failures to staffing shortages mirroring the civilian systems in items 268 and 271. The veterans inside are the same population promised lifelong care in item 122, and the gap between the promise's language and the buildings' condition is measurable in inspection reports anyone may read and few in power appear to.

Impact: Health

## 288. Divorce and Custody Courts Charge Families for Their Own Destruction

Family court is where Americans meet the justice system most often, and it operates on the pay-to-play terms of item 106: custody evaluations that cost thousands, parenting classes and supervised visitation billed by the hour, and guardians ad litem whose fees fall on the parents least able to pay. A parent too poor to fund the process can lose time with their children to a parent who can, not on the merits of anyone's parenting but on the arithmetic of the invoice, and the children absorb the ruling.

Impact: Both

## 289. The Foster Care Pipeline Begins With a Hotline Anyone Can Call

Child protective hotlines accept anonymous reports, and the system investigates the families of roughly one in three American children before they turn eighteen, according to research published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, with the rate for Black children above half. Most investigations close without findings, but the knock on the door, the interviews at school, and the file that now exists follow poor families through every later contact with the state, a surveillance baseline no wealthy family would tolerate.

Impact: Both

## 290. Schools Bill Poor Families for the Truancy Poverty Causes

When children miss school because the bus did not come, the shift ran late, the asthma flared, or the family was evicted, many districts respond with truancy fines and court referrals that convert a symptom of poverty into a legal debt. Parents have been jailed over unpaid truancy fines in documented cases, and the child whose attendance the court claims to protect watches the state punish the household for the conditions the state itself could have relieved with a bus pass and a nurse.

Impact: Both

## 291. Free School Buses Are Not Guaranteed

In many districts, students who live within an arbitrary radius of school, often two miles, are entitled to no bus at all, and in some places districts charge families fees for transportation or have cut routes entirely under budget pressure. The result is six-year-olds walking highway shoulders, parents choosing between a shift and a school run, and attendance gaps that item 290 then prosecutes, a closed loop in which the district creates the absence and the court fines it.

Impact: Both

## 292. Assisted Living Costs a Fortune and Answers to Almost No One

Assisted living facilities charge a median of $70,800 a year, operate almost entirely on private payment, and, unlike nursing homes, face no comprehensive federal regulatory or staffing standards, leaving oversight to a patchwork of state rules of wildly varying strength. Families discover the gaps at the worst moment: the facility that marketed memory care can evict a resident whose dementia worsens, because the contract, unlike the brochure, never promised to keep her.

Impact: Both

## 293. Junk Fees Are a Second Price Tag on Everything

The CFPB found that credit card companies alone charged more than $14 billion in late fees in a single recent year, and that is one line item in an economy of drip pricing: resort fees, service fees, convenience fees, and processing fees that appear at the final screen, after the customer has invested the time to buy. The fees are designed to be unshoppable, because a price you learn at the end cannot be compared at the beginning, and regulators who move against them are fought as enemies of business by the businesses collecting them.

Impact: Money

## 294. Forced Arbitration Stole the Courtroom

Buried in the terms of nearly every bank account, credit card, job application, and phone contract is a clause waiving the right to sue and to join a class action, routing every dispute into private arbitration chosen and paid for by the company. The CFPB's congressionally mandated study found that consumers recover almost nothing in arbitration while class actions returned billions to millions of people, and the Supreme Court has enforced the clauses even when they make pursuing a claim mathematically irrational, which is precisely their function.

Impact: Both

## 295. Robocalls Ring Billions of Times a Month

Americans receive an estimated four to five billion robocalls a month, a substantial share of them scams impersonating the IRS, the bank, the sheriff, and the grandchild in trouble, and the calls land hardest on the elderly targets of item 274. The technology to authenticate calls exists and carriers were ordered to deploy it, yet the calls keep coming from spoofed numbers and overseas gateways, because every actor in the chain earns something from the traffic except the person whose phone rings.

Impact: Money

## 296. Data Brokers Sell Your Location to Anyone Buying

An industry of data brokers trades in the movements of nearly every American phone, assembled from apps that quietly sold their users' location streams, and the Federal Trade Commission has had to bar specific brokers from selling visits to abortion clinics, churches, and military bases after documenting that such data was on the open market. The person tracked has no relationship with the broker, no notice, and no opt-out that functions, in a country that calls privacy a value and regulates it as a suggestion.

Impact: Both

## 297. Subscriptions Are Easy to Start and Engineered to Never End

The FTC documented the pattern so thoroughly it wrote a click-to-cancel rule in 2024 requiring companies to make quitting as easy as joining, and an appeals court struck the rule down in 2025, leaving the maze intact: the gym that requires a certified letter, the service that hides cancellation behind a retention specialist, the free trial that converts in silence. Every dark pattern is a small theft of money and time, multiplied by millions of accounts whose owners simply give up and keep paying.

Impact: Money

## 298. Airlines Owe You Almost Nothing When They Ruin Your Trip

When a European flight is delayed or canceled by the airline's own failures, passengers are owed cash compensation by law, up to 600 euros. When an American flight collapses the same way, passengers are owed, until recent refund rules took effect, essentially a voucher and an apology, and compensation for the hotel, the missed wedding, and the lost day remains zero. The difference is not the weather over the Atlantic. It is which government decided its citizens' time has a price.

Impact: Money

## 299. One Company Controls the Concert and the Ticket

In 2024 the Department of Justice sued Live Nation and Ticketmaster, alleging an illegal monopoly over live entertainment built from exclusive venue contracts, artist management, and ticketing fees stacked on fees. Fans experience the monopoly as prices that bear no relation to the seat, dynamic pricing that auctions a teenager's birthday against an algorithm, and service charges for the privilege of being charged. The government that let the companies merge in 2010 returned fourteen years later to describe the merger's results as a crime scene.

Impact: Money

## 300. American Roads Kill Like a War and Are Treated Like Weather

Roughly 41,000 people died on American roads in 2023, and pedestrian deaths have climbed to their highest levels in forty years, a toll far above peer nations that drive the same cars at similar speeds. The difference is design: wide, fast arterial roads through the places people walk, speed limits set by the fastest drivers, and a safety establishment that for decades blamed the dead for being in the street. Other wealthy countries decided traffic death was a solvable engineering problem and cut their rates dramatically. America decided it was the price of driving.

Impact: Both

## 301. A Thousand Trains Derail a Year and the Safety Bill Waits

Federal Railroad Administration data record roughly a thousand or more train derailments a year in the United States, most minor, some catastrophic, as at East Palestine, Ohio in 2023, where a derailed train's burning chemicals forced a town's evacuation and left residents testing their soil and doubting their water years later. The railroads had spent the preceding decades cutting crews and inspection staff under a model Wall Street rewarded, and the comprehensive rail safety bill introduced after East Palestine has stalled in Congress while the industry reports record profits.

Impact: Both

## 302. Highways Get the Money and Transit Gets the Bake Sale

Federal transportation funding has historically split roughly 80 percent to highways and 20 percent to public transit, and the transit systems that working people, the elderly, and the disabled depend on run on aging fleets with service that evaporates outside rush hour and outside big cities. In most of America there is no bus to miss, which makes the car of item 171 a mandatory purchase and its breakdown an employment emergency, a transportation policy that functions as a wealth test administered at the dealership.

Impact: Money

## 303. Five Thousand People a Year Die in Truck Crashes

More than 5,000 people die annually in crashes involving large trucks in the United States, the overwhelming majority of them occupants of the smaller vehicle, while the industry lobbies against speed limiters, longer rest protections, and side underride guards that safety regulators have studied for decades. Drivers are paid by the mile, which pays them to be tired, and the electronic logs that finally documented their hours were fought by the industry as an intrusion, as if the public's interest in an awake driver were a regulatory whim.

Impact: Both

## 304. The FAA Let Boeing Grade Its Own Homework

The Federal Aviation Administration's practice of delegating safety certification to manufacturers put Boeing employees in the position of certifying Boeing's own work, an arrangement exposed after two 737 MAX crashes killed 346 people and internal messages showed employees mocking the regulators they were deputized to replace. A door plug blowing out of an Alaska Airlines flight in 2024 confirmed the culture outlived the hearings. Delegation was sold as efficiency, and it functioned as capture with a org chart.

Impact: Both

## 305. One in Five Cars on the Road Has an Unrepaired Recall

Safety researchers estimate that roughly one in five vehicles on American roads, more than 50 million cars, carries an open safety recall that has never been repaired, from airbags that can explode to brakes that can fail. Owners miss notices when cars change hands, used car dealers in most states may legally sell recalled cars without fixing them, and the defect that prompted a federal recall rides along with the family that bought the car in good faith at a price that assumed it was safe.

Impact: Both

## 306. Food Poisoning Sickens 48 Million Americans a Year

The CDC estimates that foodborne illness strikes about 48 million Americans annually, sending 128,000 to the hospital and killing about 3,000, in the richest food system on Earth. The inspection regime is split between agencies by whether the food contains meat, staffed below its own targets, and weighted toward paperwork audits of plants whose violations surface, as they reliably do, in the outbreak reports that follow the funerals.

Impact: Health

## 307. The Meat Inspector Shortage Is a Policy, Not an Accident

The USDA has run chronic vacancy rates among the food safety inspectors who are supposed to stand on slaughter lines, while the industry has won approval for faster line speeds and, in some plants, for company employees to perform inspection tasks the law once reserved for federal staff. The arrangement asks the producer to grade its own product at higher speed with fewer referees, and the results arrive as the recalls and outbreaks of item 306, priced in the same currency.

Impact: Health

## 308. One Contaminated Plant Starved the Nation's Babies

When FDA inspectors found dangerous bacteria and a whistleblower's ignored warnings at Abbott's Sturgis, Michigan infant formula plant in 2022, the shutdown exposed that a single facility supplied an enormous share of America's formula, and that federal WIC contracts had concentrated the market into a few producers by design. Shelves emptied nationwide, parents drove across state lines and diluted formula against every warning, and the government that had engineered the fragility airlifted formula from Europe like a country under siege.

Impact: Both

## 309. Farm Subsidies Grow the Ingredients of Disease

Federal farm support flows overwhelmingly to commodity crops, corn, soy, and wheat, that become sweeteners, oils, and animal feed, while the fruits and vegetables the government's own dietary guidelines tell Americans to fill half their plates with receive a small fraction of the support. The subsidy structure is then cited as a mystery in the obesity statistics, as if the price of a calorie of corn syrup and the price of a calorie of spinach arrived at their ratio by weather. The government pays for the inputs of the diet and then pays again, through item 1's system, for the diet's results.

Impact: Both

## 310. Overdose Kills More Than a Hundred Thousand a Year

The CDC counted 105,007 drug overdose deaths in the United States in 2023, the majority involving opioids, a toll exceeding the population of many American cities, accumulated one funeral at a time in a country that has known the cause for a decade. Fentanyl made the supply lethal at the level of a few grains, counterfeit pills carried it to teenagers who thought they were buying something else, and the death rate among Black and Native Americans rose fastest, in the communities with the least treatment access in item 312.

Impact: Health

## 311. The Sacklers Bought a Shield and the Supreme Court Took It Back

Purdue Pharma's owners, the Sackler family, withdrew billions from the company as OxyContin's toll mounted, then offered a fraction of it in bankruptcy in exchange for legal immunity from every future opioid claim, a shield no jury had granted them. In 2024 the Supreme Court struck the deal down, ruling that bankruptcy law does not let the wealthy purchase absolution for claims they never faced, and the renegotiated settlement that followed put more money on the table and the family's name on museum walls came down anyway, a decade and hundreds of thousands of deaths after the marketing began.

Impact: Both

## 312. Treatment for Addiction Exists and Reaches One in Five

Medications for opioid use disorder, methadone and buprenorphine, cut mortality roughly in half in study after study, and federal data show only about one in five Americans with opioid use disorder receives them. Methadone remains locked inside specialized clinics patients must visit daily in many cases, buprenorphine prescribers cluster in affluent areas and often refuse insurance, and the waiting lists of item 121's logic reappear: a person who asks for help today is offered an appointment in three weeks, in a disease whose timeline is measured in days.

Impact: Health

## 313. The Overdose Antidote Costs $45 and Permission

Naloxone reverses an opioid overdose in minutes, is safe enough that the FDA approved it for over-the-counter sale in 2023, and still reaches the moment of need unreliably, priced around $45 a kit at retail, stocked unevenly in the schools, libraries, and bars where overdoses happen, and restricted in effect by local resistance to the syringe service programs that distribute it most efficiently. A society that accepts item 310's death toll while rationing a harmless antidote has made a decision, and the decision is legible in the morgue statistics of the counties that made the opposite one.

Impact: Health

## 314. Seventy-Six Billion Pills Were Shipped Into the Epidemic

The DEA's own database, pried loose by litigation and published by the Washington Post, showed that drug companies shipped 76 billion oxycodone and hydrocodone pills into the United States between 2006 and 2012, as overdose deaths climbed in plain sight and distributors filled orders for pharmacies dispensing more pills than their counties had people. Every actor in the chain had the data. The manufacturers, distributors, pharmacies, and the DEA itself watched the map light up county by county, and the shipments continued, because each pill was revenue and the dying were somebody else's department.

Impact: Both

## 315. Buy Now, Pay Later Debt Is Invisible Until It Isn't

Buy now, pay later services split purchases into installments marketed as fee-free, and the CFPB's research found their heaviest users are the financially fragile, stacking multiple plans across providers until the installments quietly exceed the paycheck. Because the loans historically were not reported to credit bureaus, neither lenders nor the borrowers' other creditors can see the total, and a consumer can be drowning in a debt that appears on no report, item 168's number serene while the bank account empties.

Impact: Money

## 316. Vehicles Grew Until They Became Weapons

American vehicles have grown taller and heavier for decades, and the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety's research found that vehicles with high, blunt front ends are significantly more likely to kill the pedestrians they strike, because the impact lands on the chest and head rather than the legs. Federal safety ratings long measured only the survival of people inside the vehicle, grading the tank, not the threat, so the market optimized for occupants while item 300's pedestrian toll climbed, a externality measured in other people's children.

Impact: Health

## 317. A Deli Meat Plant's Violations Were Filed, Not Fixed

The 2024 listeria outbreak traced to Boar's Head's Jarratt, Virginia plant killed ten people and sickened dozens, and when USDA inspection records became public they showed years of documented violations at the plant, mold, insects, and residue, noted by inspectors whose findings accumulated in files while production continued. The plant closed only after the dead were counted. The gap between inspection and enforcement is the whole story of items 306 and 307 in one facility: the government knew, in writing, in advance.

Impact: Health

## 318. Polling Places Closed by the Thousands

A report by the Leadership Conference Education Fund counted 1,688 polling place closures between 2012 and 2018 in states and counties once covered by the Voting Rights Act's preclearance system, closures that began within months of the Supreme Court freeing those jurisdictions from federal review. The remaining polling places absorb the displaced voters into longer lines, and study after study finds Black voters wait significantly longer to vote than white voters, a tax measured in hours, levied on one side of the electorate.

Impact: Both

## 319. Voter Purges Delete the Eligible Along With the Dead

States purge their voter rolls in the name of list maintenance, and the matching software they use has repeatedly proven sloppy enough to remove eligible citizens by the tens of thousands, often for a mismatched middle initial or a hyphen. Kansas's Crosscheck program, once used by dozens of states, was abandoned after researchers showed its matches were wrong far more often than right and its data security was a sieve. The purged voter typically learns of it at the polling place, where the remedy offered is a provisional ballot that may never be counted.

Impact: Both

## 320. Gerrymandering Lets Politicians Choose Their Voters

In Wisconsin's 2018 elections, Democratic candidates won the statewide vote for the state Assembly while Republican candidates took nearly two thirds of the seats, an outcome manufactured by district maps drawn after the 2010 census with surgical precision. The Supreme Court ruled in 2019 that federal courts may not review partisan gerrymandering at all, leaving the practice to the state courts and legislatures, which is to say, in most states, to the beneficiaries. The mapmakers do not fear the voters. They selected them.

Impact: Money

## 321. Felony Disenfranchisement Silences 4 Million Citizens

The Sentencing Project estimates that about 4 million Americans are barred from voting because of a felony conviction, including millions who have completed their sentences entirely and live, work, and pay taxes in communities whose governments they may not choose. Florida's voters amended their constitution in 2018 to restore rights to roughly 1.4 million people, and the legislature answered by requiring payment of all fines and fees first, in a state that often cannot tell a returning citizen how much he owes, converting the amendment into a toll booth with no cashier.

Impact: Both

## 322. Dark Money Buys Elections in Secret

Since the Supreme Court's Citizens United decision in 2010 opened the door to unlimited outside spending, more than a billion dollars of dark money, spent by groups that do not disclose their donors, has flowed through single federal election cycles. The voter sees the advertisement. The voter may not see that it was paid for by an industry regulated by the candidate it supports, routed through a nonprofit designed to launder the attribution. A democracy cannot audit influence it is forbidden to see, which is the point of the plumbing.

Impact: Money

## 323. Election Workers Are Being Driven Out by Threats

Local election officials, the county clerks and poll workers who run American democracy for near-volunteer pay, have faced a sustained campaign of threats, harassment, and conspiracy accusations since 2020, and surveys by the Brennan Center have found large shares of them planning to leave before the next presidential election. Their replacements, in some counties, arrive as believers that the elections they now administer were stolen. The machinery of voting is not computers. It is these people, and the country is watching them walk away.

Impact: Both

## 324. The IRS Answers One Call in Ten

At the depths of its underfunding, the Internal Revenue Service answered roughly one in ten phone calls from taxpayers seeking help, according to the National Taxpayer Advocate, and its enforcement staff shrank by about a third across the 2010s while the number of returns grew. The same Congress that demands item 173's audits of the poor starved the agency that might audit anyone else, then campaigned against the agency's dysfunction as if it had blown in with the weather. Recent rebuilding funds were targeted for repeal before the phone lines were even fixed.

Impact: Money

## 325. The Tax Prep Industry Lobbies to Keep Filing Expensive

For decades the United States has refused to do what dozens of countries do, calculate the typical wage earner's tax and let them confirm it, because companies like Intuit and H&R Block lobby to protect the market for paid preparation, spending millions to kill return-free filing and to keep the IRS's free options obscure. ProPublica documented how the industry steered eligible filers from the free program into paid products for years. When the IRS finally piloted its own Direct File tool in 2024, the industry and its allies moved to kill it, and in 2025 it was discontinued.

Impact: Money

## 326. The Tax Gap Is a Half-Trillion-Dollar Honor System

The IRS projects that Americans underpay their taxes by roughly $688 billion a year, the gross tax gap for 2021, concentrated not in the wage income the IRS can see, which is reported nearly perfectly, but in the business, partnership, and offshore income it cannot. Wage earners are taxed by withholding before they touch their money. The sophisticated are taxed by self-report, audited rarely per item 324, and the difference between the two systems is the largest single line in the federal deficit that no one campaigns on.

Impact: Money

## 327. Property Tax Assessments Quietly Overcharge the Poor

Research on property tax assessments across the country has found a consistent pattern: homes in poor and Black neighborhoods are systematically over-assessed relative to their sale prices, while expensive homes are under-assessed, so the families least able to pay carry effective tax rates measurably higher than their wealthier neighbors for the same public services. The appeals process that corrects the error is used overwhelmingly by those who can hire the firms that advertise for the work, leaving the overcharge in place for everyone who cannot.

Impact: Money

## 328. The Estate Tax Now Exempts Fortunes and Calls It Family Farms

The federal estate tax in 2024 applied only to estates above about $13.6 million per person, roughly $27 million per couple, a threshold that exempts all but about one in a thousand estates, the largest transfers of inherited wealth in the republic's history passing untaxed or lightly taxed through trusts, stepped-up basis, and valuation games. The tax was sold for repeal as protection for family farms and small businesses, categories its current threshold already exempts many times over. What it protects is dynasty, and dynasty is what it was revised to protect.

Impact: Money

## 329. Taxpayers Build the Stadiums and Billionaires Keep the Teams

Public money for professional sports stadiums has run into the tens of billions of dollars over recent decades, including a federal subsidy delivered through tax-exempt bonds, for facilities owned or controlled by franchise owners whose teams are worth billions. Economists who study the deals, across the political spectrum, reach a rare consensus: the promised jobs and growth do not materialize at anything like the projected scale, because spending at the stadium mostly replaces spending elsewhere in the same city. The public builds the building, assumes the risk, and buys its own tickets back at market price.

Impact: Money

## 330. Foxconn Promised Wisconsin a Miracle and Delivered a Fraction

Wisconsin offered Foxconn a package of incentives worth roughly $3 billion or more, among the largest in American history, in exchange for a $10 billion factory and 13,000 jobs that were announced with presidential fanfare in 2017. The plant shrank repeatedly, the jobs never approached the promise, and the deal was renegotiated in 2021 down to about $80 million in incentives for a commitment of 1,454 jobs, after the state had already spent heavily on land and infrastructure for the original mirage. The ribbon was cut. The miracle was not.

Impact: Money

## 331. Amazon Made 238 Cities Bid Against Themselves

When Amazon announced a competition for its second headquarters in 2017, 238 cities and regions submitted bids offering billions in tax breaks, free land, and infrastructure, disclosing their desperation in writing to one of the richest companies on Earth. Amazon selected the two locations its executives had favored from the start, beside the capital and in New York, whose $3 billion package then collapsed under public revolt. The auction's real product was intelligence: Amazon collected, free, a continent's worth of data on what governments would surrender.

Impact: Money

## 332. Opportunity Zones Delivered Luxury Towers and Call It Renewal

The Opportunity Zone program, created in 2017, offers capital gains tax breaks for investment in designated low-income areas, and the money flowed predictably toward the areas least in need of it within the zones: downtown parcels, luxury apartments, hotels, and self-storage facilities, while the Government Accountability Office found the government does not collect the data needed to know whether poor residents benefited at all. A program can be judged by its reporting requirements. This one's designers chose not to know, which is itself a finding.

Impact: Money

## 333. Farm Subsidies Flow Uphill to the Largest Operations

The Environmental Working Group's subsidy database, compiled from the government's own records, shows that farm subsidy payments concentrate heavily at the top, with the largest tenth of recipients collecting the large majority of commodity payments across recent decades, including payments to operations and owners of substantial wealth. The programs were built in the Depression to save the family farm. They now function as an entitlement scaled to acreage, which is to say scaled to how little saving the recipient requires.

Impact: Money

## 334. Fossil Fuel Subsidies Pay Both Ends of the Fire

The United States supports fossil fuels with tens of billions of dollars a year in direct tax breaks and subsidies, a figure the International Monetary Fund expands into the hundreds of billions when the unpriced damage of pollution and climate change is counted as the subsidy it economically is. The same government then spends, per items 70, 136, and 205, on the floods, fires, and heat the subsidized product causes. No private insurer would sell both the matches and the fire policy. The federal government does, and reports the arrangement as energy policy.

Impact: Both

## 335. The Public Funds the Research and the Public Pays the Price

A study published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences found that National Institutes of Health funding contributed to the research underlying every one of the 210 new drugs approved in the United States from 2010 to 2016. The public therefore stands at both ends of the pharmaceutical transaction: it finances the science through taxes, accepts the risk of the failures, and then pays the highest prices in the world for the successes, priced by companies whose foundational asset was a grant. Item 1's insulin is not an exception to this system. It is the system, working.

Impact: Both

## 336. The F-35 Will Cost Two Trillion Dollars

The Government Accountability Office projects the F-35 fighter program's total lifetime cost, acquisition plus decades of sustainment, at more than $2 trillion, for an aircraft whose mission-capable rates have chronically fallen short of the services' own targets. Costs rose even as performance lagged, in a program spread across so many congressional districts that its political engineering is more reliable than its logistics chain. The F-35 is not merely a weapons program. It is a demonstration that a project can become too distributed to cancel, whatever it does in the air.

Impact: Money

## 337. The Navy Bought Ships It Retired Before Their Time

The Littoral Combat Ship program spent well over $28 billion building fast, lightly armed ships whose missions kept changing, whose mechanical failures became a genre of news, and several of which the Navy moved to retire after less than a decade of service, some after barely five years. Sailors called them the Little Crappy Ships in the fleet's own gallows humor. The contractors were paid in full, the admirals rotated onward, and the hulls went to mothballs or foreign sale at a loss the taxpayer absorbed in silence.

Impact: Money

## 338. The Pentagon Cannot Pass an Audit

The Department of Defense has failed every department-wide financial audit since auditors first attempted one in 2018, seven consecutive failures through 2024, unable to account fully for its roughly $4.1 trillion in assets. No other federal agency of its size could operate this way, and no private company could survive it. The Pentagon's own comptrollers promise a clean opinion years in the future, a deadline that recedes on schedule. In any other context, an institution that cannot say where the money went would be investigated. This one is given more money, annually, by acclamation.

Impact: Money

## 339. A Pentagon Contractor Charged Markups in the Thousands of Percent

Defense Department inspector general reviews of the contractor TransDigm found excess profits on 46 of 47 parts examined, components sold to the Pentagon at markups that reached into the thousands of percent, because the contracting system let a monopoly supplier of spare parts name its price to a buyer forbidden by urgency from shopping elsewhere. TransDigm refunded some money after exposure and kept the model, as have others, in a spare parts economy where the government's own auditors keep publishing the same report with different part numbers.

Impact: Money

## 340. The Pentagon Admits It Has Bases It Does Not Need and May Not Close Them

The Defense Department's own analyses have estimated that roughly a fifth of its domestic infrastructure is excess capacity, bases and facilities it does not need, while Congress forbids a new round of base closures because every installation is a jobs program in somebody's district. The services thus pay to heat, guard, and maintain empty capacity indefinitely, a subsidy to geography billed to readiness, while the same budget debates cut the childcare, housing, and maintenance accounts of the troops stationed at the bases that remain.

Impact: Money

## 341. Recruiters Hunt Where the Options Are Fewest

Military recruiting draws disproportionately from poor and working-class communities, not because patriotism is rationed by income but because the offer, steady pay, healthcare, housing, and college money, is worth most where the civilian economy offers least, and recruiters are stationed and quota'd accordingly. Researchers call it the poverty draft, and it is not a conscription anyone voted on: it is the free market of item 88's wages and item 48's tuition making the enlistment office the best-funded opportunity in town. The all-volunteer force is voluntary in the way the payday loan is a choice.

Impact: Both

## 342. Congress Buys Weapons the Military Did Not Ask For

Year after year, Congress adds billions to the defense budget for weapons and equipment the services did not request, famously including tanks the Army's own leadership testified it did not need, because the factories sit in the right districts. The services then pay to store, maintain, and eventually dispose of the surplus, while their actual requests, ship repair, barracks, munitions, wait in line behind the unrequested. Patriotism is the stated motive and geography is the documented one, and the difference between the two is billed to the same account as item 338's missing trillions.

Impact: Money

## 343. More Than 120,000 People Sit in Solitary Tonight

A 2023 count by Solitary Watch and Unlock the Box estimated that more than 122,000 people in American prisons and jails were being held in solitary confinement on a given day, locked alone for 22 or more hours, in the practice item 110 documented for children and the adult system practices at industrial scale. The United Nations' Mandela Rules call solitary beyond 15 days a form of torture. American facilities measure stays in months and years, and the psychiatric wreckage is discharged, untreated, directly to the street.

Impact: Both

## 344. Release Is the Beginning of the Sentence

The Bureau of Justice Statistics found that 44 percent of people released from state prison were arrested again within the first year, and in its long-term study 83 percent were arrested at least once within nine years. Those numbers are usually read as the character of the released. They can also be read as the design of release: a person exits with a record that bars employment and housing, supervision conditions that criminalize ordinary stumbles, and no identification, bank account, or address, into the labor market of item 106's second tier.

Impact: Both

## 345. A Conviction Carries 44,000 Legal Penalties

The National Inventory of Collateral Consequences of Conviction catalogues more than 44,000 laws and regulations that restrict people with criminal records: occupational licenses they may not hold, housing they may not rent, benefits they may not receive, loans they may not take, in many cases for life and regardless of the offense's age or relevance. The sentence pronounced in court is the smallest part of the punishment. The rest is administered by databases and application forms, forever, with no judge and no end date.

Impact: Both

## 346. Prison Phone Calls Tax the Families of the Incarcerated

For decades, a handful of telecom companies held monopoly contracts to connect incarcerated people with their families at rates that reached a dollar a minute or more, with the facilities taking commissions on the calls, a revenue stream extracted from the poorest families in the system for the sound of a parent's voice. Congress finally authorized real rate regulation in 2022, the FCC slashed the caps in 2024, and several states simply made the calls free, having noticed that contact with family is among the few things proven to work against item 344's numbers.

Impact: Both

## 347. Supervision Violations Refill the Prisons

The Council of State Governments Justice Center found that violations of probation and parole supervision account for roughly 45 percent of admissions to state prisons, and a large share of those are technical violations: a missed appointment, a failed drug test, an unapproved address, conduct that is not a crime for anyone not under supervision. The system thus imprisons people not for new offenses but for failing the administrative obstacle course set for them, at a cost per prisoner that would fund the housing and treatment the violations usually signal a need for.

Impact: Both

## 348. Compassionate Release Is Granted at a Trickle

Federal law allows the release of prisoners who are terminally ill or incapacitated, and for decades the Bureau of Prisons, the gatekeeper of its own process, approved only a small fraction of the applications its wardens forwarded, while the inspector general documented prisoners dying while their paperwork circulated. Courts received direct authority to grant release in 2018 and the numbers improved from nearly none to merely few, against a prison population aging into the diseases item 350 counts.

Impact: Both

## 349. People Die in Local Jails at Record Rates Without Ever Being Convicted

The Bureau of Justice Statistics counted 1,200 deaths in local jails in 2019, the highest number since counting began, and most of the dead were being held pretrial, legally innocent, many arrested on the minor charges of item 109. Suicide and untreated withdrawal lead the causes, in facilities that book people in medical crisis and staff their clinics to the standard of item 113. A jail stay of 48 hours, survivable by definition in the system's imagination, is a mortal event for a measurable number of Americans every week.

Impact: Both

## 350. Prisons Are Becoming Nursing Homes Without the Nurses

Prisoners over 55 are the fastest-growing age group in American corrections, their numbers having multiplied several times over since the 1990s as the long sentences of that era come due, and incarcerating an elderly person costs two to three times what a younger prisoner costs, mostly in medical care the facilities are constitutionally required and operationally unable to provide. The result is a geriatric ward system run by corrections departments: dementia patients in segregation, hospice in cells, and parole boards weighing the dangerousness of men who cannot dress themselves.

Impact: Both

## 351. Tribal Courts Are Forbidden to Fully Judge Crimes on Their Own Land

Federal law caps the sentencing authority of tribal courts, limiting them under the Indian Civil Rights Act to short sentences, raised only to a maximum of three years per offense under the Tribal Law and Order Act of 2010 and only for tribes meeting federal conditions. A tribal nation may therefore watch a violent offender its own court convicts return in a fraction of the time a state court would impose across the road, in the jurisdictional architecture item 215 described, where sovereignty is recognized in speeches and capped in statutes.

Impact: Both

## 352. The United States Took 90 Million Acres of Native Land by Paperwork

Under the Dawes Act of 1887, the federal government broke tribal land into individual allotments and declared the remainder surplus, open to non-Native settlement, and by 1934 tribal landholdings had fallen from about 138 million acres to about 48 million, a transfer of some 90 million acres accomplished not by cavalry but by forms. The fractionated ownership the allotment system left behind, parcels with hundreds of co-owners, still paralyzes land use on reservations today, poverty engineered at the level of the deed.

Impact: Both

## 353. The Government's Own Count Found Nearly a Thousand Children Dead at Its Boarding Schools

The Interior Department's investigation of the federal Indian boarding school system, completed in 2024, identified more than 400 boarding schools operated or supported by the United States and documented at least 973 deaths of Native children in them, with marked and unmarked burial sites at dozens of schools. The children were taken, renamed, punished for their languages, and in hundreds of cases never returned, and the government that ran the system kept such poor records that the investigators state plainly the true number of deaths is higher.

Impact: Both

## 354. Native Voters Face Barriers No Other Citizens Face

North Dakota's voter ID law required a residential street address of a kind many homes on reservations do not have, a requirement federal courts examined for years while Native voters organized workarounds at their own expense. The pattern generalizes: polling places hours from reservation communities, mail service too unreliable for mail voting, and registration systems built for addresses the reservation was never given. Item 217's rollback lands on Native voters with the weight of item 352's history underneath it.

Impact: Both

## 355. Sacred Land Is Transferred to Mining Companies Over Tribal Objection

At Oak Flat in Arizona, a site sacred to the Western Apache since time immemorial, the federal government arranged by a rider attached to a 2014 defense bill to transfer the land to a foreign-owned copper mining venture whose planned mine will swallow the site into a crater, over the sustained religious objection of the tribes. The transfer was accomplished without a vote on its own merits, in the fine print of a must-pass bill, which is how the taking of Native land has usually been accomplished since the paperwork method of item 352 was perfected.

Impact: Both

## 356. Territorial Medicaid Is Capped Because Congress Can

Medicaid in the fifty states is an open-ended entitlement: federal matching funds flow as need flows. In Puerto Rico and the other territories, Congress caps the federal contribution at a fixed allotment and sets the matching rate by statute below what the territory's poverty would earn under the states' formula, so the poorest jurisdictions in America run their health programs for the poor on rationed federal money. When the allotment runs out, the care does, a sentence that cannot be written about any state in the union.

Impact: Both

## 357. Puerto Rico's Debt Is Governed by an Unelected Board

In 2016 Congress imposed an unelected fiscal oversight board on Puerto Rico, empowered to override the island's elected government on budgets, in response to a debt crisis built partly from bonds Congress's own tax code had made artificially attractive and the island was legally barred from restructuring in bankruptcy court. The board's austerity budgets cut pensions, schools, and the university while the island's residents, American citizens all, could not vote for a single member of the body governing them or for the president who appointed it.

Impact: Both

## 358. More Than 3.5 Million Americans Cannot Vote for Their Own President

Roughly 3.6 million people live in Puerto Rico, Guam, the U.S. Virgin Islands, American Samoa, and the Northern Mariana Islands, citizens in four of the five territories and U.S. nationals in American Samoa, serve in the military at high rates, hold U.S. passports, and cannot cast a single ballot in the presidential election or send a voting member to the Congress that taxes, drafts, and governs them. Their nonvoting delegates may speak in the House and vote in committee. The arrangement has a name in the civics textbooks of an earlier century, and the textbooks called it colonialism.

Impact: Both

## 359. The Jones Act Taxes Island Americans for Their Geography

A 1920 law requires that goods shipped between American ports travel on American-built, American-crewed ships, among the most expensive fleets in the world, and its costs land on the captive markets of Puerto Rico, Hawaii, Alaska, and Guam, where nearly everything arrives by sea. Island consumers pay the premium in every grocery aisle while being told the arrangement is a national security necessity, a claim that has survived a century without ever being itemized against the grocery bills of the citizens financing it.

Impact: Money

## 360. Pacific Islander Migrants Were Denied Medicaid for 24 Years by Drafting Error's Cousin

Citizens of the Marshall Islands, Micronesia, and Palau may live and work in the United States under Compacts of Free Association, an arrangement connected to America's military use of their homelands, including the nuclear testing grounds of the Marshall Islands. The 1996 welfare law stripped them of Medicaid eligibility anyway, and Congress did not restore it until 2020, leaving a generation of migrants, many with radiation-linked illnesses, uninsured in the country that irradiated their atolls and employs their children.

Impact: Health

## 361. The Public Health Workforce Was Hollowed Out Before It Was Needed

Public health in America is delivered by state and local health departments that spent the decades before the pandemic shedding staff, with workforce studies estimating the nation entered the 2020s needing roughly 80,000 additional public health workers to deliver basic services. The pandemic then stress-tested the hollow system on live television: case investigations unmade, data arriving by fax, guidance unfunded. The workforce has been partially rebuilt with emergency money that expires, returning the system to the posture in which the emergency found it.

Impact: Health

## 362. Disease Data in America Still Moves by Fax Machine

During the pandemic, hospitals and laboratories reported case data to health departments by phone, spreadsheet, and, in documented accounts across the country, by fax, into systems that could not talk to each other or to the CDC in real time. The richest medical economy on Earth runs its outbreak surveillance on infrastructure older than its nurses. Modernization funding has begun and remains a fraction of what the hospital industry spends on billing software, which is a precise statement of what the data system is for.

Impact: Health

## 363. More Than 150 Rural Hospitals Have Closed and the Map Keeps Shrinking

The Cecil G. Sheps Center's count of rural hospital closures has passed 150 since 2005, with many more converting to emergency-only facilities that cannot admit a patient overnight, leaving counties where the nearest obstetric unit, ICU, or trauma surgeon is an hour or more away. The closures follow the arithmetic of item 8's non-expansion map with grim fidelity, and each one removes the county's largest employer along with its emergency room, so the ambulance of item 364 drives farther, past the building that used to be the hospital.

Impact: Both

## 364. Millions of Americans Live in Ambulance Deserts

Researchers at the Maine Rural Health Research Center estimated that about 4.5 million people live in ambulance deserts, places more than 25 minutes from the nearest ambulance station, concentrated in rural counties where volunteer services are aging out and closing for lack of funding and recruits. The golden hour of trauma care does not negotiate with geography. A heart attack or a farm accident in an ambulance desert is a different medical event than the same crisis in a city, with the same insurance card in the wallet.

Impact: Health

## 365. Measles Came Back to a Country That Had Eliminated It

The United States declared measles eliminated in 2000, and in 2025 it recorded its worst measles year in three decades, with well over a thousand cases and the first American measles deaths in a decade, nearly all among the unvaccinated, as childhood vaccination rates slid below the thresholds herd immunity requires. The resurgence was not caused by a shortage of vaccine, which is cheap and has existed since 1963. It was caused by the collapse of trust that items throughout this audit document being earned, institution by institution, and then spent.

Impact: Health

## 366. The 988 Lifeline Answers Calls the System Behind It Cannot

The 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, launched in 2022, answers hundreds of thousands of calls, texts, and chats a month, and routes them to local crisis centers whose funding depends on state decisions that vary from robust to nearly nothing. A caller in crisis may reach a counselor in another state with no knowledge of local help, and the help itself, the mobile crisis team, the open bed, the follow-up appointment, is the unfunded part. The country built the front door of crisis care and left the rooms behind it to local improvisation.

Impact: Health

## 367. A Hundred Thousand Americans Are Waiting for an Organ

Federal data count more than 100,000 people on the national transplant waiting list, most of them waiting for a kidney, and roughly 17 people die each day still waiting, in a system where transplantable organs are buried and cremated daily because the deceased's wishes were never registered or never honored. The allocation network itself spent decades under criticism for geographic inequities that made a patient's survival depend on which list their hospital joined, the map again, drawn this time in organs.

Impact: Health

# The Pentagon Papers: An Audit of the American Dream — Audit 4

By Mary-Victoria Crockett

This document is part of a series that presents the complete audit from "Not Yours To Take: An Audit Of The American Dream," rewritten and fact-checked, items 1 through 500. Audit 1 holds Parts 1 through 9 (items 1 to 130). Audit 2 holds Parts 10 through 15 and new items 218 to 267. Audit 3 holds new items 268 to 367. This document continues the new items from 368.

## 368. The Government Buys the Location Data It Would Need a Warrant to Seize

The Supreme Court held in 2018 that the government generally needs a warrant to obtain a person's cell phone location history. Federal agencies responded by buying location data from the brokers of item 296: a 2023 report from the Office of the Director of National Intelligence acknowledged that intelligence agencies purchase commercially available information that is sensitive and can be used to track individuals, and immigration and border agencies' purchases of location databases have been documented in their own contracts. The Fourth Amendment, it turns out, has a checkout lane.

Impact: Both

## 369. Facial Recognition Keeps Arresting the Wrong Black Americans

Every publicly documented case of a wrongful arrest caused by a false facial recognition match in the United States has involved a Black person, including Robert Williams, arrested in his Detroit driveway in 2020 in front of his children, and Porcha Woodruff, arrested in 2023 while eight months pregnant. Police departments deployed the technology for years without accuracy standards, disclosure rules, or any obligation to tell the accused that a machine had picked their face, and the error rate's distribution is the technology's confession about the data it was raised on.

Impact: Both

## 370. License Plate Readers Record Every Drive You Take

Networks of automated license plate readers, led by private vendors like Flock Safety, photograph billions of license plates a month in American neighborhoods, building searchable records of where ordinary people drive, worship, protest, and seek care, retained for weeks or years depending on local policy most drivers have never seen. Police query the databases without warrants in most jurisdictions, the vendors' contracts sprawl across thousands of towns, and a record created by a camera on a pole belongs, in practice, to everyone except the person whose movements it describes.

Impact: Both

## 371. The Government Monitors Protesters and Calls It Awareness

Federal agencies have repeatedly been documented building files on lawful dissent: the Department of Homeland Security compiled intelligence reports on journalists and protesters during the 2020 Portland demonstrations, immigration authorities have contracted for social media monitoring of the people they police, and fusion centers have tracked movements from anti-war groups to pipeline opponents. The First Amendment promises assembly. The surveillance state prices it, in a currency of dossiers that follow a citizen long after the march disperses.

Impact: Both

## 372. Section 702 Lets the FBI Search Americans Without a Warrant

Section 702 of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act authorizes surveillance aimed at foreigners abroad, and the FBI has used it to run warrantless searches for Americans' communications in the resulting databases, hundreds of thousands of times in some years, in documented violation of its own querying rules that a federal court had to drag into the open. Congress reauthorized the authority in 2024 without the warrant requirement civil liberties groups across the spectrum demanded. The program's defenders call the American searches incidental. The Americans searched call them searches.

Impact: Both

## 373. Schools Surveil Students on the Devices School Requires

Monitoring software installed on school-issued laptops watches students around the clock in many districts, scanning their writing and messages for flagged words and routing alerts to administrators and, in documented cases, to police. Civil liberties researchers have found the systems out LGBTQ+ students to unsupportive homes, discipline students for searches made in private, and convert the one computer a poor family has into the school's listening post inside the home, a condition of education no wealthy family would sign.

Impact: Both

## 374. Predictive Policing Launders Yesterday's Arrests Into Tomorrow's Patrols

Predictive policing software directs patrols to neighborhoods the algorithm flags as high risk, trained on historical arrest data that records where police have always policed, so the software's predictions ratify the past and call it the future. Residents of flagged blocks receive more stops, more arrests for the minor offenses of item 109, and more data confirming the model, in a feedback loop several cities have abandoned after audits and others continue to buy. The bias is not in the code. It is in the archive, and the code's job is to make the archive look like math.

Impact: Both

## 375. America Has No Real Privacy Law and the Breaches Show It

The United States has no comprehensive federal privacy law, leaving a patchwork of state statutes and sector rules while the European Union has enforced one framework for years. The Identity Theft Resource Center counted a record 3,205 data compromises in the United States in 2023, exposing the records of hundreds of millions of people, whose remedy is typically a year of free credit monitoring from the company that lost the data and a lifetime of watching item 168's number for damage they did not cause.

Impact: Both

## 376. Forever Chemicals Are in the Tap Water of Tens of Millions

PFAS, a class of industrial chemicals that do not break down in the environment or the body, contaminate drinking water across the country: a U.S. Geological Survey study detected them in about 45 percent of tap water samples nationwide, and the EPA issued the first national drinking water limits for several PFAS only in 2024, decades after the manufacturers' own research documented the risks. The chemicals are linked to cancers, immune damage, and developmental harm, and the cleanup bills are arriving, as usual, at the water utilities of the communities that were poisoned, not the firms that profited.

Impact: Both

## 377. Superfund Sites Wait Decades for a Cleanup That May Never Finish

More than 1,300 toxic sites sit on the Superfund National Priorities List, and cleanup at a typical site is measured in decades: investigation, litigation over who pays, remedy selection, construction, and monitoring, while the neighborhoods around the fence lines, disproportionately poor and nonwhite like every map in this audit, live with the plume. The dedicated tax on the chemical and petroleum industries that once funded orphan cleanups was allowed to lapse for a generation, shifting costs to general revenue and slowing the queue to the pace the exposed have learned to expect.

Impact: Both

## 378. Coal Ash Ponds Leak Into Groundwater Across the Country

For every coal plant there is an ash pond or landfill holding the toxic residue of combustion, laced with arsenic, lead, and mercury, and when the EPA finally required groundwater monitoring, an analysis by environmental groups of the industry's own disclosed data found contamination above health standards at more than 90 percent of the plants examined. The ash sits in unlined pits in floodplains, in the same river towns as items 43 and 65, and its cleanup obligations are negotiated plant by plant while the groundwater does what groundwater does.

Impact: Both

## 379. Lead Paint Still Poisons Children in Millions of Homes

The Department of Housing and Urban Development estimates that roughly 29 million American homes contain lead-based paint, with millions presenting hazards significant enough to poison the children living in them, decades after the paint was banned in 1978. Enforcement runs through disclosure forms at sale and lease that disclose nothing testable, and the burden of discovering the hazard falls on the toddler's blood test, after the exposure the law was written to prevent. Flint was water. This is the same metal, in the walls, at national scale.

Impact: Health

## 380. Farmworkers Absorb the Pesticides the System Counts as Safe

The EPA has estimated that farmworkers suffer thousands of acute pesticide poisonings a year, with the true number widely believed to be far higher because the workforce is immigrant, dispersed, and afraid to report, and the federal worker protection standard permits reentry into sprayed fields on timelines written with industry at the table. The people who harvest the nation's food accept a chemical exposure at work that would close any office building in America, and their illnesses are recorded, when recorded at all, in a surveillance system designed by the states least inclined to count.

Impact: Health

## 381. Fracking Wastewater Shook Oklahoma Until the State Admitted Why

When Oklahoma's earthquake rate exploded in the 2010s, surpassing California's, state and federal scientists traced the swarms to the deep injection of oil and gas wastewater, and a magnitude 5.8 quake near Pawnee in 2016 damaged buildings across the region before regulators ordered injection cuts that finally bent the rate down. For years beforehand, the state's official posture was uncertainty, maintained while homeowners, who cannot buy standard earthquake insurance for a risk the industry created, absorbed cracked foundations and the state's geological survey was discouraged from saying what it knew.

Impact: Both

## 382. Almost None of Your Plastic Is Recycled and Never Was

The United States recycles only about 5 to 6 percent of its plastic waste, a rate that has fallen as plastic production has grown, while the chasing-arrows symbol stamped on containers implies a system that does not exist for most resins. Internal industry documents surfaced in recent years show the plastics industry understood for decades that recycling could not economically absorb its output even as it promoted recycling to defeat bans and deposits. The rest is landfilled, incinerated, or exported, and the consumer sorting the bin is the unpaid labor of a public relations strategy.

Impact: Both

## 383. Congress Promised to Fund 40 Percent of Special Education and Pays About 13

When Congress passed the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act in 1975, it pledged to pay 40 percent of the excess cost of educating students with disabilities, and in the half century since, the federal contribution has hovered near 13 percent, reaching the promised level in no year. Local districts must provide the services regardless, by law, so the shortfall is extracted from the same budgets as items 46 and 184, and the families of disabled children learn to hire lawyers to obtain what the statute guarantees, converting a civil right into a litigation budget.

Impact: Both

## 384. American Schools Are Resegregating by Policy and by Map

Researchers at UCLA's Civil Rights Project have documented for years that the segregation of Black and Latino students in American public schools has been intensifying for three decades, as court supervision was lifted and attendance boundaries, school choice systems, and housing patterns did the work the old statutes once did. Large shares of Black and Latino students now attend schools where nearly every classmate is poor and nonwhite, schools that then present, in items 46 and 185, the predictable resource profile. The integration era is treated as history. Its reversal is treated as weather.

Impact: Both

## 385. Chronic Absenteeism Doubled and Stayed Doubled

Before the pandemic, about 15 percent of American students were chronically absent, missing a tenth or more of the school year. By 2022-23 the figure had roughly doubled, to around 28 to 30 percent nationally, and it has remained far above the old baseline since, cutting across districts rich and poor. The causes are the audit's familiar cast: illness without item 283's sick days, housing instability, broken transportation of item 291, and a generation's frayed attachment to institutions that closed on them. The response, in too many places, is item 290's court date.

Impact: Both

## 386. More Than a Million Students Are Homeless

Federal education data identify roughly 1.37 million public school students as homeless in the 2022-23 school year, and about 1.55 million in 2023-24, doubled up in other families' homes, in motels, in shelters, in cars, a count that excludes the children too young for school and the families who never disclose. The law guarantees these students transportation and immediate enrollment, rights delivered unevenly by districts that cannot always find them, and their grades and graduation rates carry the arithmetic of a childhood spent moving. A locker becomes a fixed address. That is the housing policy underneath the education statistics.

Impact: Both

## 387. University Endowments Compound Tax-Free While Students Borrow

The largest American university endowments hold sums that rival national budgets, Harvard's exceeding $50 billion, compounding largely free of tax under the charitable exemption, subject only since 2017 to a small excise tax on the very largest. The same institutions raise tuition at item 192's pace and employ the adjunct labor of the education economy at wages that qualify some instructors for assistance. The endowment is defended as intergenerational equity. The generations currently enrolled, borrowing at item 172's rates, are invited to admire it.

Impact: Money

## 388. An Eviction Filing Alone Blacklists a Family for Years

Eviction Lab has counted roughly 3.6 million eviction filings in a typical pre-pandemic year, and tenant screening companies treat the filing itself, not its outcome, as the mark: cases that were dismissed, settled, or won by the tenant still surface in screening reports and still produce denials, because the databases do not distinguish accusation from judgment. A family can thus be exiled from mainstream housing by a landlord's unproven claim, with the record sold to every future landlord by the same industry that runs item 168's scores.

Impact: Both

## 389. Manufactured Homeowners Own the House and Rent the Ground It Sits On

About 22 million Americans live in manufactured homes, the nation's largest source of unsubsidized affordable housing, and a growing share of the land beneath those homes is owned by institutional investors and private equity funds that have discovered lot rent as a revenue stream. The homeowner cannot move a house that costs more to relocate than it is worth, so the rent rises are take-it-or-leave-your-home offers, and the equity a family spent decades building drains into a land lease their grandparents never imagined could be financialized.

Impact: Both

## 390. Zoning Laws Ban the Apartments Working People Need

Analyses of American cities find that the large majority of residential land, around three quarters in many major cities, is zoned exclusively for single-family detached houses, making it illegal to build the apartments, duplexes, and small multifamily buildings that working people can afford, in the neighborhoods with the jobs and schools. The bans are defended as neighborhood character, and they function as a wall with a price of admission set at the cost of a detached house, which is why item 19's waiting lists and item 25's encampments grow in the shadow of empty zoning capacity.

Impact: Both

## 391. Home Insurance Is Abandoning the States That Need It Most

Major insurers have stopped writing new homeowners policies or withdrawn entirely from Florida, California, and parts of the Gulf and Midwest, as climate-driven losses climb, and the state insurer of last resort in Florida swelled past 1.4 million policies in 2023, then fell back below a million by late 2024 as policies were moved back to private carriers, holding the riskiest homes at prices that still doubled for many families. A house that cannot be insured cannot be mortgaged, and a house that cannot be mortgaged cannot be sold, so the insurance map is quietly redrawing the housing map of entire coastlines, item 138's resettlement arriving as a premium notice.

Impact: Both

## 392. Your Home Is Worth Less Because an Appraiser Saw Your Family Photos

Federal housing research has documented appraisal bias in the industry's own data: homes in Black neighborhoods are substantially more likely to be appraised below their contract price than homes in white neighborhoods, and case after case has surfaced of appraisals jumping by six figures when the owners removed evidence that a Black family lived there and had a white colleague stand in. The appraisal is the gate through which every refinance and sale must pass, and the gate, the data say, reads race as a comp.

Impact: Both

## 393. The Ambulance That Arrives Is the Bill That Bankrupts

The No Surprises Act of item 5 banned most surprise medical bills and deliberately left out ground ambulances, because the ambulance industry and its municipal patchwork were too complicated to regulate in one law. Health policy researchers have found that roughly half of ground ambulance rides carried the risk of an out-of-network surprise bill, for a service the patient summons in the worst minutes of their life, cannot decline, cannot price, and often did not choose, dispatched by a 911 system to whichever company holds the territory.

Impact: Both

## 394. Prior Authorization Means Your Doctor Asks a Clerk for Permission

Before an insurer pays for the treatment a physician ordered, its prior authorization department must agree, and in the American Medical Association's recurring surveys, more than nine in ten physicians report that the process delays care, with a substantial share reporting that it has led to serious harm, hospitalization, or a patient abandoning treatment entirely. The reviewers who deny the requests are employed by the company that profits from the denial, a conflict item 3 measured in denial rates. This item measures it in the weeks between diagnosis and permission.

Impact: Health

## 395. The Middlemen of the Pharmacy Counter Take a Cut of Every Pill

Pharmacy benefit managers, the intermediaries who decide which drugs your plan covers and what you pay, were found in the Federal Trade Commission's interim reports, the first issued in 2024 and the second in January 2025, to steer patients toward their own affiliated pharmacies and to mark up the prices of lifesaving drugs, in some documented cases by thousands of percent, at pharmacies they owned. The rebates they negotiate are secret, the spreads they keep are secret, and the patient's copay is calculated from a list price almost no one pays, a hall of mirrors with a cash register.

Impact: Both

## 396. Pharmacies Are Closing Where They Are Needed Most

Thousands of pharmacies have closed across the United States in recent years, from independent drugstores squeezed by item 395's reimbursement rates to the bankruptcy of national chains, and the closures concentrate in rural counties and poor urban neighborhoods, leaving pharmacy deserts where the nearest pharmacist is a county away. The pharmacist is the most accessible clinician in America, the one who notices the interaction and answers without an appointment. The system is letting that clinician vanish from exactly the places with no other.

Impact: Health

## 397. Medicare Covers the Body Except the Parts That Hear, See, and Chew

Item 9 indicted the dental exclusion. The pattern is wider: traditional Medicare also excludes routine vision care and hearing aids, devices that cost an average of several thousand dollars a pair before the FDA's 2022 over-the-counter rule began to bend the price, in a population where untreated hearing loss is linked in study after study to dementia, falls, and isolation. The program for the elderly declines to cover the senses the elderly are losing, and the resulting loneliness is treated, expensively and late, as depression.

Impact: Health

## 398. Hospital Mergers Raise Prices and Call It Efficiency

When hospitals merge, the prices they charge rise, a finding replicated so consistently across decades of research that the Federal Trade Commission now challenges mergers on the strength of it, with studies finding increases of 6 percent to more than 20 percent in concentrated markets and no corresponding improvement in quality. Two thirds of American hospital markets are now highly concentrated by the government's own measures. The patient in item 3's denial letter and item 5's surprise bill is negotiating, alone, against a regional monopoly that the antitrust authorities waved through.

Impact: Both

## 399. Hospitals Pay Agencies Billions for the Nurses They Would Not Retain

During the pandemic, hospitals that had held staff nurses' wages flat for years paid staffing agencies crisis rates that tripled their contract labor costs, enriching intermediaries while the nurses doing the work received a fraction of the billed rate and the staff positions beside them stayed unfilled. The American Hospital Association's own reporting documented contract labor expense rising several-fold between 2019 and 2022. The money for nurses existed. It was routed, at a markup, around employing them, and when the crisis ebbed the agencies' rates fell and the staff wages mostly did not rise to meet them.

Impact: Both

## 400. Half of American Counties Have No Psychiatrist

Research on the mental health workforce has found that more than half of U.S. counties have no practicing psychiatrist at all, and the shortage deepens across psychologists, counselors, and inpatient beds, so that the emergency room and the county jail of item 349 have become the mental health system of record for much of the country. Item 4 documented insurers failing to cover mental health at parity. This is the other half of the failure: in most of the map, there is nothing to cover, at any price, within any network.

Impact: Health

## 401. Just-in-Time Scheduling Makes Workers' Lives Unplannable

Research by the Shift Project at Harvard and UC Berkeley has found that the large majority of hourly service workers receive less than two weeks' notice of their schedules, with shifts canceled, added, and rotated in ways that make childcare, second jobs, classes, and sleep unschedulable, while their hours and therefore their incomes swing week to week. A small number of cities and states have passed fair workweek laws requiring notice and predictability pay. In most of the country, the algorithm that staffs the store treats the worker's life as a variable to be optimized last.

Impact: Both

## 402. The Overtime Threshold Was Set So Low It Exempts the Poor

Federal law guarantees overtime pay to salaried workers below a salary threshold, and that threshold sat at $23,660 a year, barely above the poverty line, for fifteen years, exempting assistant managers and salaried staff earning poverty wages from a cent of overtime no matter how many hours they worked. A 2024 rule raising the threshold to roughly $58,656 was struck down by a federal judge before it fully took effect, restoring the lower line, and with it the legal fiction that a person earning $35,000 a year is an executive whose hours need no counting.

Impact: Money

## 403. Farmworkers Were Written Out of Labor Law in 1938 and Left There

The landmark labor statutes of the New Deal excluded agricultural workers, a compromise struck with Southern legislators whose economies ran on Black field labor, and the exclusion largely stands: farmworkers have no federal right to organize under the National Labor Relations Act and no federal right to overtime pay, rights every other workforce takes as the floor. A handful of states, California, Washington, and New York among them, have repaired pieces of the exclusion at the state level. The harvest workforce of item 380 works under the law's oldest exception, which was never about agriculture.

Impact: Both

## 404. Domestic Workers Were Written Out Beside Them

The same New Deal compromise excluded domestic workers, the nannies, housekeepers, and home care workers who are now overwhelmingly women, immigrants, and women of color, from the core labor statutes, and while minimum wage coverage was extended to many of them in 1974, they remain outside the National Labor Relations Act's organizing rights and outside occupational safety law's reach in private homes. Roughly two million people do this work. The home is their workplace, and the law treats the workplace as a private castle into which labor rights do not follow.

Impact: Both

## 405. Employers Spend Hundreds of Millions a Year to Keep Unions Out

The Economic Policy Institute has estimated that employers spend roughly $340 million a year on union avoidance consultants, an industry that scripts captive audience meetings, trains supervisors to detect organizing, and times the terminations that item 86's weak remedies make affordable. Workers who win elections anyway face a second wall: a large share of newly unionized workplaces never reach a first contract, because delay costs the employer less than the contract would. The right to organize exists in the statute. The industry exists to make exercising it a career risk.

Impact: Money

## 406. The Warehouse Injury Rate Is a Business Model

Analyses of federal injury data by the Strategic Organizing Center have repeatedly found that Amazon warehouse workers suffer serious injuries at roughly twice the rate of the warehousing industry as a whole, a gap the company disputes in methodology while its own internal research, reported in the press, linked injury rates to the pace its systems demand. The model is item 86's silence applied to logistics: rates set by algorithm, discipline automated, and the injured worker replaced from a labor pool the injury rate itself helps keep desperate.

Impact: Health

## 407. Half the Private Workforce Has No Retirement Plan at Work

Research by AARP and others finds that roughly half of private-sector workers, some 57 million people, lack access to a retirement savings plan through their employer, and workers without one are dramatically less likely to save at all, because the payroll deduction is the entire technology of middle-class saving. The result arrives in item 130's territory at age 70: Social Security, designed as one leg of a stool, standing alone. A growing number of states have built automatic IRA programs to fill the gap. The federal response remains a tax code that subsidizes, lavishly, the plans of those who already have them.

Impact: Money

## 408. Courts Fund Themselves by Fining the People Before Them

The Justice Department's investigation of Ferguson, Missouri documented a municipal court operated as a revenue engine, issuing warrants and fines to fund city government from the pockets of its poorest residents, and subsequent research has found hundreds of jurisdictions where fines and fees supply a tenth or more of the municipal budget. A court that must fine to fund itself cannot be neutral about guilt, and a defendant who cannot pay discovers item 109's cascade wearing judicial robes. Justice is supposed to be the one public service that cannot be run for profit. In much of America it is run for margin.

Impact: Both

## 409. Justice Speaks One Language and the Court Provides No Interpreter

Federal civil rights law requires courts to provide language access, and audits, lawsuits, and the Justice Department's own enforcement letters keep finding courts where litigants in eviction, custody, and even criminal proceedings face interpreters who are unqualified, unavailable, or billed to the person who cannot understand the proceedings without one. A hearing you cannot comprehend is not a hearing. It is a ceremony performed over your life in a language the state chose, with your signature taken as consent.

Impact: Both

## 410. Immigration Court Is a Courtroom Without a Lawyer for the Accused

Immigration court is civil in name, so the right to appointed counsel does not apply, and the government appears by trained prosecutor while the respondent, who may be a child, appears alone in the large majority of cases. Transactional Records Access Clearinghouse data put the immigration court backlog above 3.6 million cases at its peak, and it still stood above 3 million cases in mid 2026, with waits measured in years, and represented asylum seekers win their cases at multiples of the rate of the unrepresented. The outcome of the most consequential proceeding in a family's life is predicted less by the facts than by the presence of a lawyer they cannot afford, item 106 with a border.

Impact: Both

## 411. Notarios Sell Legal Help They Are Not Allowed to Give

In Spanish, notario suggests a licensed legal official, and in immigrant communities across the United States, storefront notarios and immigration consultants exploit the word, charging thousands for applications they are unqualified and unauthorized to file, botching deadlines, filing claims that trigger deportation, and vanishing before the consequences land. The Federal Trade Commission and state attorneys general have pursued the fraud for decades, but the market persists because item 410 leaves a vacuum: people who cannot afford lawyers and cannot navigate the system alone will buy help from whoever is selling it.

Impact: Both

## 412. A Felony Record Bars You From Judging Anyone Else

Most states bar people with felony convictions from serving on juries, in many states for life, excluding tens of millions of Americans from the one civic duty that puts ordinary citizens inside the justice system, and skewing juries in the communities where convictions are most common, per items 108 and 345. The system thus distrusts the judgment of the very people who know it best, while asking their neighbors to judge them. A jury of one's peers, in a county where a third of the peers are disqualified, is a phrase under strain.

Impact: Both

## 413. Immigration Detention Is a Private Industry With a Bed Count

Analyses by the ACLU and other monitors have found that roughly 90 percent of people in ICE detention are held in facilities operated by private companies or private contractors, paid per bed per day, in a system whose population is set not by courts but by congressional appropriations and enforcement discretion. The detainees are civil, not criminal, and include asylum seekers whose cases item 410 counts in the millions. The companies' revenue rises with the census, which makes immigration detention item 111's incentive structure with a different flag on the building.

Impact: Both

## 414. The Work Authorization System Flags the Authorized

E-Verify, the federal system employers use to confirm work authorization, issues tentative nonconfirmations that fall disproportionately on naturalized citizens and work-authorized immigrants because of database errors, name mismatches, and outdated records, and workers have lost jobs in the days it takes to correct a record the government itself corrupted. Proposals to make the system mandatory nationwide would scale the error rate with it, converting database hygiene into an employment right, administered by the agencies of item 324.

Impact: Both

## 415. DACA Recipients Renew Their Lives Every Two Years

About 530,000 people held active protection under Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals at the end of 2024. The count has since fallen below 500,000. The status exists by executive memorandum, has been in litigation since 2017, and must be renewed, with a fee, every two years, each renewal a fresh encounter with the possibility that the program ends before the paperwork clears. Its recipients were brought to the United States as children, work, pay taxes, and serve in hospitals and classrooms, and their legal existence is a subscription the government may cancel, a precarity no legislature has had the votes or the will to end in over a decade.

Impact: Both

## 416. Asylum Seekers May Not Work While the Government Takes Years

By statute, an asylum applicant must wait at least 150 days after filing before even applying for work authorization, and processing delays stretch the real wait far longer, during the years item 410's backlog consumes, leaving families legally present, legally forbidden to support themselves, and dependent on charity and the informal economy that item 83's protections do not reach. The same political debate that demands asylum seekers not burden the public fisc forbids them, by law, from relieving it.

Impact: Both

## 417. Citizenship Itself Costs $760 and a Year of Waiting

Applying for naturalization costs $760 in federal fees, before any help with the forms, a price that prices out the dishwasher and the home care worker who have lived lawfully in the United States for decades, and fee waivers exist on paper that processing makes difficult to use. Hundreds of thousands of applications sit pending at any time. The civics test at the end is famous. The toll at the beginning is the policy: the country charges admission to the citizenship it urges immigrants to earn, and the charge is calibrated to be just payable by those who least need the urging.

Impact: Money

## 418. Small Water Systems Break the Rules Because They Cannot Afford Them

The Environmental Protection Agency's own data show that the large majority of drinking water systems with health-based violations of the Safe Drinking Water Act serve small communities, systems with a handful of staff, no chemist, and a rate base too poor to finance the treatment the law requires. The violations are not mysteries of chemistry. They are the arithmetic of item 201 applied to water quality: a town of 800 people cannot amortize a treatment plant, so the standard exists on paper and the contaminant exists in the water.

Impact: Health

## 419. Water Bills Are Rising Faster Than the Incomes That Pay Them

Municipal water and sewer bills have climbed steeply across the country, in many cities rising by half or more over a decade, driven by the deferred infrastructure bills of items 196 and 201 coming due at the ratepayer's kitchen table. Federal water assistance, briefly created during the pandemic, expired, leaving shutoff as the collection tool for a necessity with no substitute. A family can economize on almost anything else in this audit. It cannot economize on water, which is why utilities can price it like a monopoly and call the shutoff notice a billing practice.

Impact: Money

## 420. The Flood Insurance Program Is Twenty Billion Dollars in Debt

The National Flood Insurance Program owes the U.S. Treasury more than $20 billion, even after Congress canceled $16 billion of its debt in 2017, because for decades it charged premiums below the risk it insured in the floodplains of item 138, subsidizing construction in places the water has repeatedly reclaimed. The program now pays hundreds of millions a year in interest alone on its own insolvency, a public insurer kept alive to underwrite private risk, in a climate whose invoice grows by item 75's arithmetic.

Impact: Money

## 421. The Flood Maps Are Wrong and the Claims Prove It

A large share of federal flood insurance claims, a quarter to a third by various analyses, come from properties outside the zones FEMA's maps designate as high risk, because the maps are old, drawn from old rainfall data, and blind to the development that has paved the watershed since. Families buy homes assured by a map that the water has not read, decline insurance they are told they do not need, and meet the flood uninsured, in the gap between the government's cartography and the century's weather.

Impact: Both

## 422. Veterans' Community Care Became a Privatization Spigot

The VA's community care program, expanded by the MISSION Act of 2018 to let veterans see private doctors when VA waits run long, has grown into one of the largest spending lines in the department, tens of billions a year flowing to private networks and their administrators, with inspector general and GAO findings documenting duplicate payments, poor coordination, and veterans lost between the two systems. The choice offered was VA or private. The result, too often, is a referral into the same fragmented market as item 3, wearing a flag.

Impact: Both

## 423. A Quarter of Service Members Struggle to Feed Their Families

Defense Department surveys and RAND research have found that roughly one in four active-duty service members experienced food insecurity in a recent year, with junior enlisted families, paid on the scale of item 88's economy and stationed in high-cost areas, driving to food pantries in uniform's civilian clothes. The military has responded with allowances and screenings, and the number remains a standing embarrassment that no recruiting commercial mentions: the force that item 341 recruited from poverty kept a measurable share of its families in it.

## 424. Privatized Military Housing Let Families Live With Mold and Sewage

A 2018 Reuters investigation and subsequent Senate hearings documented the privatized housing empire on American bases, where a handful of companies collected billions in contracts while military families lived with mold, lead, vermin, and sewage, and work orders vanished into the landlord's ledger. Congress added tenant protections after the hearings, and GAO reviews continue to find the services cannot verify the companies' performance data. The families could not withhold rent, move off base without losing their housing allowance, or sue easily. They could only testify, which they did, holding photographs.

Impact: Both

## 425. Your Neighbors' Credit Card Points Are Paid for by the Poor

Research by economists at the Federal Reserve Bank of Boston documented that the credit card rewards system transfers money upward: merchants raise prices to cover card processing fees, cash customers, who are disproportionately poor and unbanked per item 179, pay the higher prices without receiving rewards, and the points, miles, and cash back flow to the affluent cardholders who need them least. The annual transfer was estimated at about $149 paid out by each cash-using household, while each card-using household received about $1,133, a private welfare system running in reverse, invisible at the register.

Impact: Money

## 426. Timeshares Are Sold in Hours and Owned Forever

The timeshare industry sells vacation weeks in presentations engineered to last hours and exhaust resistance, and the Federal Trade Commission and consumer advocates have documented the aftermath for decades: maintenance fees that rise annually forever, resale markets where the week's value approaches zero, and an exit industry of second-order scams charging desperate owners thousands to escape the first trap. The contract is the rare consumer product designed so that the buyer's regret is a permanent revenue feature, inheritable by the buyer's children, who did not attend the presentation.

Impact: Money

## 427. Student Loan Servicers Collected Billions and Failed at Arithmetic

The companies paid to administer federal student loans have repeatedly been caught steering borrowers into costlier plans, misapplying payments, and losing records, most expensively Navient, which paid a $1.85 billion settlement to state attorneys general in 2022 over allegations of predatory steering. The borrower of item 48 cannot choose a competent servicer, cannot fire an incompetent one, and cannot discharge the consequences in item 177's court.

## 428. Textbooks Are Priced Like Luxury Goods for a Captive Market

The College Board's standard student budget allocates more than $1,000 a year for books and supplies, in a market where a handful of publishers sell new editions on cycles designed to kill the used market and increasingly bundle the homework itself into access codes that expire, making the book unresalable and the code nontransferable. Students respond rationally: surveys consistently find large shares skipping required texts, sharing codes, or taking the grade hit, which converts a pricing strategy into an educational outcome, measured in the currency of item 181.

Impact: Money

## 429. The Security Deposit Is a Loan the Landlord May Simply Keep

For most renters, the security deposit is the largest single sum they hand a stranger all year, and in much of the country its return depends on the landlord's honesty, because many states impose weak penalties, short timelines the tenant must enforce in the courts of item 106, and no requirement to prove deductions with receipts. Surveys and legal aid caseloads document the pattern: deposits diminished or kept on pretexts, by landlords who know the tenant has moved, is exhausted, and cannot fund a small claims fight over money the landlord is already holding.

## 430. Your HOA Can Foreclose on Your Home Over a Few Hundred Dollars

In many states, a homeowners association may place a lien and foreclose on a home over unpaid assessments and fees that began as a few hundred dollars and compounded through the association's own attorney charges, a power investigative reporting has documented being used against elderly owners, deployed soldiers, and families who never received the notices. Impact: Both

## 431. The Government Fined a Game Company $275 Million for Harvesting Children

In 2022, Epic Games agreed to pay $275 million, the largest penalty ever under the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act, for collecting the personal data of children under 13 without parental consent in Fortnite, on top of a separate $245 million for design tricks that steered players into purchases. Impact: Money

## 432. Broadband in America Costs More for Less Because Competition Is Optional

Federal and independent analyses have long found that tens of millions of American households have at most one provider offering true broadband speeds at their address, and that Americans pay more for slower service than consumers in most peer nations, where regulators imposed competition on the lines. The maps that declared the country served were drawn from industry filings that counted a census block as covered if a single house could buy service, a cartography item 421's flood maps would recognize, and the monopoly's price is collected monthly from every homework assignment and telehealth visit in the territory.

## 433. Dollar Stores Are the Fastest-Growing Grocer in the Food Desert

Dollar store chains have become the fastest-growing food retailers in America, opening thousands of stores concentrated in the poor urban and rural areas item 171 mapped, stocking shelf-stable processed food and almost no fresh produce, meat, or dairy, and undercutting the last independent grocer whose departure they then complete. ## 434. Meatpacking Plants Record About Two Amputations a Week

Analyses of the Occupational Safety and Health Administration's severe injury reports have found that workers in American meat and poultry plants suffer amputations at a pace of roughly two per week, year after year, alongside lacerations, chemical burns, and repetitive strain injuries at rates among the highest in manufacturing. The plants run at the line speeds of item 307, staffed by the workforce of item 380, and the injuries are priced into the same cheap protein as item 43's lagoons, the invoice arriving in different envelopes at the same addresses.

## 435. Drunk Driving Still Kills More Than Thirteen Thousand People a Year

The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration counted 13,524 alcohol-impaired driving deaths in 2022, about a third of all traffic fatalities, a toll that has resisted four decades of campaigns, enforcement, and the rideshare era. ## 436. Four Airlines Control Four Fifths of American Skies

Decades of approved mergers have left four carriers controlling roughly 80 percent of the domestic airline market, and the results are the lived experience of item 298: markets abandoned, fees harmonized upward, and service failures with no competitor to defect to at the regional airport. The government that blocked a century of railroad and oil consolidation waved through the airline mergers one by one, each time accepting promises of efficiency that the route maps then refuted, until the map itself became the monopoly's portrait.

## 437. A Million Abandoned Wells Leak on the Public's Land and Ledger

Federal estimates count well over a million abandoned oil and gas wells across the United States, with the documented orphan wells, those with no solvent owner, numbering about 142,000 on state and federal lists, plus an estimated 250,000 to 740,000 more that remain undocumented, leaking methane and brine into soil and water while the bonds posted by their operators cover a fraction of plugging costs. ## 438. Coal Companies Promised to Clean Up and Declared Bankruptcy Instead

For decades, coal companies were permitted to self-bond their mine reclamation obligations, promising their own balance sheets as the guarantee that mined land would be restored, and when the industry's giants passed through bankruptcy in the 2010s, the Government Accountability Office documented billions in reclamation liability at risk of landing on states and taxpayers. ## 439. Manufactured Homes Are Tornado Magnets and the Parks Have No Shelters

The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration's research has long shown that residents of manufactured homes die in tornadoes at rates wildly disproportionate to their share of the housing stock, because the homes are lighter, anchored less deeply, and concentrated in the tornado belts of the South and Midwest, and most manufactured home parks provide no storm shelter at all. The warning system works. Radios announce the county and the hour. What the residents lack is anywhere to go, which is not a meteorology problem.

## 440. Silence About Harassment Was a Contract Term Until Congress Objected

For decades, employment and settlement contracts across American industries included nondisclosure and nondisparagement clauses that barred victims of sexual harassment and assault from describing what happened to them, protecting serial offenders whose employers settled, silenced, and in documented cases passed them to new workplaces unwarned. Congress finally voided predispute NDAs for sexual harassment and assault claims in the Speak Out Act of 2022. Every other species of workplace misconduct remains negotiable in the dark, and the clauses' long career is the measure of what the law once considered a legitimate purchase: a victim's voice, at settlement prices.

Impact: Both

## 441. Private Probation Companies Bill the Poor for Their Own Supervision

In hundreds of courts across the South, documented by Human Rights Watch and subsequent litigation, judges sentence people who cannot pay fines to private probation, for-profit companies that supervise them for monthly fees added to the debt they could not pay in the first place, extending supervision month after month as the fees compound, with jail as the enforcement for nonpayment. The arrangement is item 408's revenue court with a middleman: the poorer the defendant, the longer the supervision, the larger the company's take, a business model whose growth market is insolvency.

Impact: Both

## 442. Hundreds of Thousands of Rape Kits Sat in Storage Untested

Audits and inventories conducted city by city over the past decade have found untested rape kits in police storage numbering in the hundreds of thousands nationally, each one a survivor who submitted to an invasive examination on the promise that the evidence mattered, and each one a suspect left unidentified while the kit aged on a shelf. # The Pentagon Papers: An Audit of the American Dream — Audit 5

By Mary-Victoria Crockett

This document is part of a series that presents the complete audit from "Not Yours To Take: An Audit Of The American Dream," rewritten and fact-checked, items 1 through 500. ## 443. The Government Loses a Quarter Trillion a Year to Improper Payments

The Government Accountability Office estimated that federal agencies made about $247 billion in improper payments in fiscal 2022 alone, payments that were incorrect, inadequately documented, or sent to the wrong people, a figure that recurs annually at a scale that would fund entire cabinet departments. Most improper payments are error rather than fraud, which is worse in one way: the system is not being robbed so much as it is being run at a standard of accuracy no taxpayer would accept from a bank, by the same government that audits item 173's waitresses to the dollar.

Impact: Money

## 444. Federal Buildings Sit Empty While the Rent Is Paid

Government Accountability Office reviews of federal office space have found agency headquarters buildings operating at a fraction of their capacity, around a quarter utilized on average in the buildings studied even before the pandemic emptied more of them, while the government pays full maintenance and security on the emptiness and disposes of surplus property at a pace measured in decades. The same budget that cannot find item 361's public health workers maintains millions of square feet of vacant monument to itself.

Impact: Money

## 445. Children May Legally Work the Fields at Twelve

Federal labor law sets the minimum age for most work at 14 and for hazardous work at 18, but in agriculture a child of 12 may legally work outside school hours with a parent's consent, and hazardous farm work is permitted at 16, exemptions written for the family farm of a century ago and used today by commercial operations. The children of item 380's workforce thus grow up inside the exemption, breathing the same fields, under a statute that decided a century ago that their childhoods were negotiable in a way suburban childhoods were not.

Impact: Both

## 446. One Worker in Fourteen Has Wages Garnished

Payroll industry data have shown that roughly one in fourteen American workers has wages garnished, most commonly for child support, consumer debt, and student loans, a deduction taken before the worker touches the check and processed by the employer as an unpaid collection agent for the creditor. Federal law caps garnishment at 25 percent of disposable earnings for ordinary debts, a ceiling that assumes the remaining 75 percent covers rent in the markets of item 390. The garnished worker is item 178's cascade, arrived: the emergency of five years ago, collected at payroll speed, indefinitely.

Impact: Money

## 447. The Eyewitness Is Wrong More Often Than the Courtroom Believes

The Innocence Project's accounting of DNA exonerations finds that mistaken eyewitness identification contributed to about 69 percent of the wrongful convictions DNA later overturned, making the confident stranger on the stand the single most productive source of proven false convictions in the system. Memory science has known the mechanisms for decades, suggestion, cross-racial identification error, confidence inflated by feedback, and most jurisdictions have adopted only fragments of the reforms the science supports. The jury hears certainty. The archive records error.

Impact: Both

## 448. Innocent People Plead Guilty Because the Alternative Is Worse

More than nine in ten criminal cases in America end in plea bargains, and the National Registry of Exonerations counts hundreds of people later proven innocent who pleaded guilty anyway, because the sentence offered for the plea was a fraction of the sentence threatened after trial, the differential prosecutors call negotiation and defendants experience as arithmetic. A system that convicts almost entirely without trial has replaced the burden of proof with a price list, and the innocent, who know exactly how juries can err per item 447, pay the listed price like everyone else.

Impact: Both

## 449. Prisoners Are Counted Where They Are Caged, Not Where They Live

The Census Bureau counts incarcerated people as residents of the prison's location rather than their home communities, a practice called prison gerrymandering that transfers political representation from the urban neighborhoods the prisoners come from to the rural districts that host the prisons, inflating those districts' power in state legislatures with the bodies of people who may not vote at all per item 321. A growing list of states has moved to count prisoners at home. In the rest, the map is drawn partly in cellblocks.

Impact: Both

## 450. Long COVID Pushed Millions Out of the Workforce

Federal surveys have found millions of American adults living with long COVID symptoms that limit their daily activities, and economic researchers estimated at the pandemic's height that the condition was keeping on the order of two to four million people out of work entirely, with more working reduced hours through brain fog, exhaustion, and cardiac symptoms the disability system of item 123 was not built to evaluate. A mass disabling event arrived in a country whose disability benefits require proving, to a skeptical reviewer, exactly the kind of fluctuating invisible illness long COVID is.

Impact: Both

## 451. Dementia Care Costs More Than the Defense of Some Nations

The Alzheimer's Association estimated the cost of care for Americans living with Alzheimer's and other dementias at roughly $360 billion in 2024, a figure that excludes the unpaid care of item 275, valued in the hundreds of billions more, and the total climbs every year as the population ages into the disease. There is no cure, the new drugs are priced like the drugs of item 335 and debated like them, and the financial plan offered to most families is item 272's: spend everything, then Medicaid, in a facility from item 268.

Impact: Both

## 452. The World Runs on Plasma From America's Poor

The United States supplies roughly 70 percent of the world's source plasma for medicines, collected at centers that pay donors, who may give twice a week, and that concentrate, as researchers and journalists have mapped, in poor neighborhoods and along the southern border, where Mexican nationals cross to sell their plasma as an income strategy. The products are exported worldwide at pharmaceutical prices. The donors receive a prepaid card and juice. No other wealthy nation permits the frequency or the commerce at this scale, because no other wealthy nation has this many people for whom the money is worth the needle.

Impact: Both

## 453. Thousands of High-Hazard Dams Are Aging in Place

The average American dam is more than 60 years old, and state dam safety officials count more than 2,500 dams in the high-hazard category, those whose failure would kill people downstream, that are rated in poor or unsatisfactory condition, with a national rehabilitation bill estimated in the hundreds of billions. The failures arrive as item 70's disasters with a different fluid: Oroville's spillway crisis in 2017 evacuated 188,000 people in an afternoon. The dams were built by a country that funded infrastructure as a creed. They are maintained by one that funds it as a leftover.

Impact: Both

## 454. The Military Poisoned Its Own Bases and Surrounding Towns

The Department of Defense has identified more than 700 installations with known or suspected releases of PFAS, the forever chemicals of item 376, from the firefighting foam used in training for decades, contaminating drinking water for base families and the civilian communities sharing the aquifers, towns whose residents never enlisted in anything. Cleanup obligations stretch across decades and billions, litigated site by site, while the department that cannot pass item 338's audit also cannot yet say what its foam did to the water of how many towns.

## 455. Schools Restrain and Seclude Disabled Children Tens of Thousands of Times a Year

Federal civil rights data have counted more than 100,000 students subjected to physical restraint or seclusion in a single school year, and the overwhelming majority, around four in five, are students with disabilities, who make up a small fraction of enrollment. The practices, being pinned, being locked alone in a room, are documented to injure and occasionally kill, and they concentrate in the underfunded special education system of item 383, where an aide shortage and a behavior plan meet a child whose disability the building cannot staff for.

Impact: Both

## 456. American Babies Die at Rates the Wealthy World Left Behind

The United States records an infant mortality rate of roughly 5.6 deaths per 1,000 live births, far above the rates of peer nations that cluster near 3 to 4, and the rate for Black infants runs more than twice the rate for white infants, a gap that persists across income and education. The causes are the audit in miniature: preterm birth without item 10's preventive care, unsafe sleep in crowded housing, maternal health from item 282 arriving too late. The first year of life is the first audit, and the richest country fails it at a rate its peers would call a crisis.

Impact: Health

## 457. The Doctor's Fee Was Only the First Fee

Hospitals increasingly charge a facility fee on top of the professional fee for the same visit, so a ten-minute appointment in a clinic the hospital acquired generates two bills, one for the doctor and one for the room, a charge that did not exist when the clinic was independent and that buys the patient nothing but the hospital's ownership of the building. State legislatures have begun banning the practice for routine visits, against the industry's lobbying, which describes the second bill as the cost of quality, charged to the person sitting in the room being charged for.

Impact: Money

## 458. Thieves Skim the Grocery Money of the Poorest Shoppers

Criminals attach skimming devices to the card readers of grocery stores and copy the data of SNAP benefit cards, which lack the chip security of modern credit cards, draining a family's monthly food budget in minutes in another state. States and journalists have documented losses in the millions of dollars, and federal authority to replace stolen benefits, granted temporarily in 2022, expired in December 2024, leaving the poorest shoppers in America holding the loss from a fraud the payment industry fixed for everyone else a decade ago.

Impact: Money

## 459. Members of Congress Trade Stocks and Fine Themselves $200

The STOCK Act of 2012 required members of Congress to disclose their stock trades promptly, and the investigative project Conflicted Congress documented dozens of members violating the disclosure rules, with the standard penalty a $200 fine, often waived, while the trades themselves touched industries the members regulated. Bills to ban congressional stock trading altogether have been introduced by both parties for years and have never been allowed a vote that mattered. The referees own shares in the teams, and the league office charges them two hundred dollars when they forget to mention it.

Impact: Money

## 460. America Has Lost Close to 3,500 Newspapers Since 2005

Northwestern University's Medill State of Local News reports count close to 3,500 newspapers closed in the United States since 2005, with closures continuing at a pace of about two and a half papers a week, leaving counties with no source of local reporting at all and vast regions where the school board, the sheriff, and the water authority of item 418 operate unwatched. Every other item in this audit depends on someone noticing. The noticing infrastructure is being liquidated, its buildings sold to the chains and funds that closed the papers.

Impact: Both

## 461. The 911 System Is Understaffed at the Moment of the Call

Emergency dispatch centers across the country report chronic staffing shortages, with centers operating well below authorized strength and callers in some cities waiting minutes for an answer or reaching hold queues during surges, in a system built of local funding fragments like item 408's courts. The dispatcher is the first link in every emergency chain this audit follows, the ambulance of item 393, the officer of item 114, the fire crew of item 73, and the link is a person working mandatory overtime in a room the county cannot fill.

Impact: Both

## 462. Guestworkers Arrive in Debt and May Not Leave the Boss

The H-2A agricultural guestworker program has grown to roughly 385,000 certified positions a year, with about 315,000 visas actually issued in fiscal 2024, binding each worker to a single employer, so that complaining about the housing, the pay theft of item 79, or the pesticides of item 380 risks the job, the visa, and the debt many workers incurred to recruiters in their home countries to obtain the job at all. The Labor Department's enforcement actions in the program read as a catalog of the predictable: wages stolen, housing unfit, workers injured and discarded. A visa that functions as a leash manufactures the compliance it then cites as consent.

Impact: Both

## 463. Debt Lawsuits Are the Civil Courts' Main Business and the Defendant Rarely Comes

Research by the Pew Charitable Trusts found that debt collection claims are the most common type of civil case in state courts, that fewer than one in ten defendants has a lawyer, and that the large majority of cases end in default judgment for the plaintiff, often a debt buyer from item 176 suing on records it cannot fully document. The civil justice system, in volume terms, is a machine for converting unexamined claims into garnishments of item 446, with a judge's signature as the only moving part.

Impact: Money

## 464. Apartment Hunting Charges the Poor by the Application

Renters in tight markets pay application fees of $30 to $75 or more per unit, nonrefundable, often for screenings run by the companies of item 388 that reject them for records they cannot see, and a family may pay the fee a dozen times in a single search, spending the deposit money on the privilege of being evaluated. Some cities have capped or pooled the fees. In most of the market, the search itself is a revenue stream extracted from the people with the least housing and the most applications, item 171's poverty premium collected at the leasing office.

Impact: Money

## 465. Investors Outbid Families for the Starter Homes

At the market's peak in the early 2020s, investors were purchasing roughly a quarter of the single-family homes sold in the United States, by the tallies of housing data firms, concentrating in the affordable starter homes of the Sun Belt and paying cash that a family's mortgage cannot match at the closing table. The purchases convert owner-occupied neighborhoods into rental stock owned by entities the tenants will never meet, and the wealth building that homeownership performed for a century of families is rerouted to shareholders, one three-bedroom at a time.

Impact: Both

## 466. The Supreme Court Unprotected Half the Nation's Wetlands in One Ruling

In Sackett v. EPA in 2023, the Supreme Court narrowed the Clean Water Act's protection to wetlands with a continuous surface connection to permanent waters, a test that agency scientists and independent researchers estimated could strip federal protection from roughly half or more of the nation's wetlands, the ecosystems that filter the water of item 376 and hold the floods of item 421. The ruling was delivered as statutory interpretation. Its consequences arrive as hydrology, in the basements and aquifers of the states now invited to protect what the federal government no longer will.

Impact: Both

## 467. Hundreds of Medicines Are in Shortage, Including the Generic Ones Nobody Profits From

Drug shortage trackers at the University of Utah and the American Society of Health-System Pharmacists counted more than 300 active shortages at the 2024 peak, a record, concentrated in cheap generic injectables, antibiotics, and chemotherapy agents whose prices are too low to justify redundant manufacturing. Hospitals ration, substitute, and delay treatment while the market's verdict stands: a drug can be essential, on the WHO's list and the oncologist's protocol, and unprofitable enough that no one guarantees its supply. Item 13's patents block the expensive drugs. This is the other failure, the cheap ones abandoned.

## 468. The Algorithm Sets Your Wage and Won't Show Its Work

Researchers studying gig platforms have documented algorithmic wage discrimination, in which workers are paid different amounts for the same work based on the platform's model of what each individual will accept, computed from their behavior, their desperation, and their history of saying yes. The pay is personalized, the formula is secret, and the worker cannot negotiate with a function. Item 83 took away the benefits. This takes away the wage itself as a public fact, replacing the posted rate, a century of labor practice, with a private price for each person's compliance.

## 469. The Federal Contractor Minimum Wage Was Raised and Revoked by Signature

In 2021 an executive order raised the minimum wage for workers on federal contracts to $15 an hour, covering janitors, cafeteria workers, and laborers on the government's own jobs, and in 2025 a new executive order revoked it, returning hundreds of thousands of workers to whatever the market and item 88 provide. The episode is a complete diagram of the American wage floor for the unorganized: it exists at the pleasure of the pen, rises by signature, falls by signature, and the worker's rent is due under both administrations.

Impact: Money

## 470. Freedom of Information Requests Die of Old Age

The Freedom of Information Act promises federal records within twenty business days, and the reality, documented by the National Security Archive's audits, is backlogs in the hundreds of thousands of requests, waits of years as routine, and some requests pending for decades, answered when the requester, the events, or the officials involved are safely historical. Exemptions swallow the rest. A transparency law administered at geological speed is not a transparency law. It is an archive with a suggestion box, and every item in this audit that begins "the government's own data show" was extracted through it or around it.

## 471. Ankle Monitors Bill the Monitored by the Day

Courts and supervision agencies across the country charge the people they monitor daily fees for electronic ankle monitors, commonly five to fifteen dollars a day, imposed on pretrial defendants who are legally innocent and on parolees whose poverty is the premise of their supervision, with nonpayment punishable by the jail the monitor was supposed to replace. The device reports location to the state. The invoice reports something else: that in this system even liberty, conditionally granted, is rented back to its holder by the day.

## 472. Medical Credit Cards Charge Deferred Interest on the Sick

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's research on medical financing products found that patients who signed up for medical credit cards and payment plans at the hospital or dentist's office paid roughly a billion dollars in deferred interest over a recent three-year period, charges triggered when a promotional period expired and interest was applied retroactively to the original balance, on procedures the patient had already undergone and could not return. The financing is offered at the point of maximum vulnerability, by staff in scrubs, and the terms are item 170's absence of a cap wearing a healthcare badge.

## 473. Hospitals Must Post Their Prices and Mostly Don't

Since 2021, federal rules have required hospitals to publish their actual negotiated prices in usable form, and compliance studies in the years since have repeatedly found large shares of hospitals incomplete, noncompliant, or publishing data so unwieldy as to be decorative, while federal penalties were for years too small and too rarely imposed to discipline anyone. The secrecy is the business model of item 398's monopolies: a market in which the buyer cannot know the price is not a market, and the industry's resistance to the price tag is the audit's simplest confession.

## 474. Poor Neighborhoods Have Fewer Trees and Hotter Streets

American Forests' Tree Equity Score, built from canopy, income, race, and heat data, documents that neighborhoods of color and low-income neighborhoods in American cities have systematically less tree canopy than wealthy white ones, and that the difference is measured in degrees: blocks without trees run measurably hotter in the heat waves of item 139, with the health consequences arriving at the same addresses as items 71 and 206. The canopy is infrastructure, planted or withheld by a century of municipal budgets, and its map is the redlining map, photosynthesizing.

## 475. Sewage Sludge Spread PFAS on America's Farms

For decades, cities disposed of treated sewage sludge by selling or giving it to farmers as fertilizer, and the sludge carried the PFAS of items 376 and 454 from industry and households into farm soil and groundwater, a contamination pathway that surfaced when Maine, after finding farms and wells poisoned and a dairy industry in crisis, banned the practice in 2022, the first state to do so. The farmers followed the guidance of the agencies that promoted sludge. The liability is now being negotiated between the poisoned and the promoters, and the sludge still spreads in most states.

## 476. Foster Children Are Medicated at Rates No One Would Accept for Their Own

Government Accountability Office studies of foster care found that children in state custody were prescribed psychotropic medications at rates far higher than other children on Medicaid, including combinations of multiple powerful drugs and, in the data of several states, prescriptions for infants, with oversight of consent and monitoring documented as inadequate. A foster child has no parent at the pharmacy counter. The state is the parent, and the state's supervision of the drugs it authorizes for its own children would end careers if the children were anyone else's.

## 477. School Starts Before the Teenage Brain Wakes Up

The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that middle and high schools start no earlier than 8:30 in the morning, based on the established biology of adolescent sleep, and the large majority of American high schools start earlier, many before 8:00, because bus schedules, sports, and adult convenience are arranged that way. Districts that have moved start times later report the predicted results: more sleep, better attendance, fewer teen car crashes. The science is settled and the bell schedule is not, a small daily item that shows how completely the system's logistics outrank the child's biology.

Impact: Health

## 478. Colleges Close With Days of Notice and Keep the Credits Hostage

Hundreds of American colleges have closed or merged in the past decade, with the pace quickening to a drumbeat of closures announced mid-semester, sometimes with weeks or days before the doors locked, leaving students with partial degrees, credits that transfer imperfectly or not at all, and loans item 48 will collect regardless. The regulators who accredited the institutions and the lenders who financed the students discover the insolvency when the students do. A degree plan is a four-year contract. Only one side is held to it.

## 479. A $50 Tax Debt Can Take a House

Investigations of tax lien sales, most famously the Washington Post's 2013 series on the District of Columbia, documented elderly homeowners losing houses worth hundreds of thousands of dollars over property tax debts that began as small as a few dozen dollars, after the liens were sold to investors whose fees and interest multiplied the debt beyond any possibility of payment by the people least equipped to navigate redemption. ## 480. The Airline's Most Valuable Asset Is Not the Airplanes

When United Airlines needed financing in 2020, it pledged its MileagePlus loyalty program as collateral, valued in the transaction at roughly $22 billion, a figure that exceeded the market value of the airline itself at the time, and analysts have reached the same conclusion across the industry: the carriers' points programs, which mint currency sold to banks at a markup and redeemed at a discount the issuer controls, are worth more than the flying. The planes lose money. The scrip makes it. The passenger of item 298 is not the customer. The passenger is the mint.

## 481. Your Television Watches You Back

The Federal Trade Commission settled with Vizio in 2017 over its smart televisions' collection of the viewing habits of 11 million households, second by second, sold to data brokers without meaningful consent, and the practice it exposed, automatic content recognition built into the modern television, remains standard across the industry, governed by settings menus designed to be endured rather than read. The screen in the living room reports to the brokers of item 296. The family watching it believes it is the audience. It is the broadcast.

## 482. Geofence Warrants Asked Google Who Was Anywhere Near a Crime

For years, police served geofence warrants on Google demanding the identities of every device within a radius of a scene, a dragnet that swept up bystanders whom investigators then had to clear, in cases documented to have falsely implicated people whose only offense was proximity. ## 483. Public Transit Owes a Hundred Billion in Repairs It Cannot Pay

The Federal Transit Administration has estimated the national backlog of transit repairs, the state of good repair deficit, at more than $100 billion, trains, buses, signals, and stations aging past their design lives in the systems item 302 funds last. ## 484. There Are Not Enough Air Traffic Controllers and the Schedule Hides It

## 485. When the Fire Comes, the Utility Turns Off the Breathing Machines

## 486. Military Sexual Assault Is Measured in Tens of Thousands a Year

## 487. The Languages Are Dying With the Elders Who Carry Them

## 488. American Samoans Are Nationals of a Country That Won't Call Them Citizens

People born in American Samoa are United States nationals but not United States citizens, the only American soil where birth does not confer citizenship, a status preserved by a century-old doctrine from the Insular Cases and left standing when the Supreme Court declined to review the question in 2022. ## 489. The Pass-Through Deduction Sends Its Billions to the Top

The 2017 tax law's 20 percent deduction for pass-through business income was sold as relief for small business, and analyses by the Joint Committee on Taxation and independent tax experts have found that the majority of its benefit flows to the highest-income business owners, the partners, landlords, and investors whose income needs no relief, at a cost of tens of billions a year. ## 490. Crop Insurance Subsidies Pay Out Rain or Shine, Mostly Uphill

## 492. Registration Is the Voter's Homework in America Alone Among Democracies

The United States is nearly alone among established democracies in placing the burden of registration on the individual citizen, and the predictable result is that tens of millions of eligible Americans, by Pew's recurring estimates roughly a quarter of the eligible population in recent decades, are not registered at all, concentrated among the young, the poor, and the frequently moved. ## 493. Predatory Truck Leases Leave Drivers Owing the Company Store

Studies of eviction courts by the ACLU and housing researchers consistently find the same asymmetry: landlords appear with attorneys in the overwhelming majority of cases, around 90 percent in studied courts, while tenants have counsel in roughly one case in ten, in proceedings that move in minutes and end in the loss of the home. ## 495. Exonerated Americans Lost More Than 31,000 Years to Wrongful Convictions

## 496. The EpiPen Went From $100 to $600 Because It Could

Mylan acquired the EpiPen, a device delivering a dollar's worth of epinephrine invented decades ago with public research in its lineage, and raised the price of a two-pack from about $100 in 2007 to more than $600 by 2016, because the patients who carry it cannot decline to breathe. Congressional hearings, a Justice Department settlement over Medicaid rebates, and state investigations followed, and the episode became the cleanest parable of item 335: the monopoly was the product, the allergy was the market, and the price was set at the value of a child's life, discounted for what insurance could be made to bear.

## 497. The Air Ambulance Bill Arrived Before the Patient Woke Up

Before Congress included air ambulances in the No Surprises Act, the Government Accountability Office found median prices running roughly $36,000 for a helicopter transport and about $41,000 for a fixed-wing transport, with patients routinely balance-billed for tens of thousands after insurers paid their share, for a flight dispatched by someone else, to a hospital chosen by protocol, in a market where two companies came to dominate the sky. Item 393's ground ambulances remain outside the law. The distinction between the two fleets is not medical. It is the order in which the lobbies were defeated.

## 498. Radon Seeps Into One Home in Fifteen and Nobody Tests

The Environmental Protection Agency estimates that radon, an invisible radioactive gas released from soil, is the second leading cause of lung cancer in the United States, responsible for about 21,000 deaths a year, and that roughly one in fifteen American homes has levels above the agency's action threshold. There is no federal requirement to test at sale in most of the country, no standard in most rental law, and the mitigation, a fan and a pipe, costs less than the inspections homebuyers routinely order for cosmetic defects. The deadliest environmental exposure in American housing is the one the closing process does not mention.

Impact: Health

## 499. The Law Capped an Oil Spill's Damages at $75 Million

The Oil Pollution Act of 1990 capped an offshore operator's liability for economic damages from a spill at $75 million, a figure set before the Deepwater Horizon blew out in 2010 and produced damages counted in the tens of billions, paid by BP only after it waived the cap under political duress. The cap remains the statutory starting point for the next spill, an insurance policy written by the industry against its own catastrophe, in which the fishing towns, hotels, and wetlands of item 65's coastline hold the deductible.

Impact: Both

## 500. America Spends the Most on Healthcare and Dies the Youngest Among Rich Nations

The United States spends roughly twice as much per person on healthcare as the average wealthy nation, about 17 to 18 percent of its entire economy, and records a life expectancy of about 78.5 years against peer averages above 82, a gap that has widened for four decades and that no other item in this audit fully explains because all of them explain it together. Five hundred entries in, the arithmetic is the argument: the money is spent. It is spent on the machinery documented in Part 1 and item 395 and item 398, and the years of life it was supposed to buy are the audit's missing remainder.

Impact: Health

## 501. Pregnancy Coverage Used to Expire Sixty Days After the Baby

For decades, Medicaid's pregnancy coverage, which finances roughly four in ten American births, ended sixty days after delivery, in a country whose own CDC data show that a large share of pregnancy-related deaths occur between six weeks and one year postpartum, from cardiomyopathy, mental health crises, and the chronic conditions item 282's reviews call preventable. States have only in the last few years, under a federal option created in 2021, extended the coverage to a full year, nearly all of them, an admission that the sixty-day cliff was never a medical judgment.

Impact: Health

## 502. The School Librarian Is an Endangered Position

Analyses of federal education data find that the number of school librarians in the United States has fallen by roughly a fifth or more since 2000, with the losses concentrated in poor districts, even as the information environment children must navigate grew teeth of the kind item 91 describes. The librarian is the professional who teaches a student to evaluate a source, find a book that fits, and use a library that item 460's closures are also eroding. The position is cut as a luxury in the budgets of item 46, in the century when it became a necessity.

Impact: Both

## 503. A Third of College Students Are Food Insecure

Surveys by Temple University's Hope Center, the largest recurring study of students' basic needs, have repeatedly found roughly a third or more of college students food insecure, with substantial shares housing insecure and a measurable fraction homeless, in the population item 48 is financing at interest. The campus food pantry has become standard infrastructure, a charitable patch on a pricing structure that charges full tuition to students skipping meals, and quietly redefines what the degree costs: not the sticker price, but the sticker price plus hunger.

Impact: Both

## 504. Paying Rent Builds the Landlord's Credit, Not the Tenant's

For most renters, the largest monthly payment of their lives has historically counted for nothing in item 168's score, because rent payments were not reported to the credit bureaus, while a single missed credit card payment is immortal. A tenant can pay two thousand dollars on time for a decade and present a thinner file than a cardholder with a fraction of the obligation, a gap only recently and partially addressed by opt-in reporting programs. The score measures participation in the credit economy, and then the housing economy prices the score, and the renter funds both from outside.

Impact: Money

## 505. Reverse Mortgages Took the Homes of Widows the Product Was Sold to Protect

Investigations of reverse mortgages, including a USA Today analysis of federal data, documented thousands of foreclosures that fell on surviving spouses who were not listed on the loans, often elderly widows who learned after a husband's death that the product sold as a way to age in place required the home to be sold or the balance paid within months. Regulators tightened the rules after the reporting. The foreclosures already completed were not undone, and the widows' equity, the family's item 210 asset, transferred on schedule.

Impact: Both

## 506. The Foreclosure Machine Signed What It Never Read

The 2012 National Mortgage Settlement, a $25 billion agreement between the government and the five largest mortgage servicers, resolved documented, systematic fraud in the foreclosure process: robo-signed affidavits, forged documents, and families foreclosed by institutions that could not prove they owned the loans, during the crisis that item 177's bailouts answered for the banks. The settlement's size made headlines. Divided among the millions of foreclosed households of the era, it priced the fraud at a few thousand dollars a home, and the executives of the process kept the decade.

Impact: Both

## 507. Car Title Loans Take the Car From One Borrower in Five

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's study of car title loans found that about one in five borrowers ends up losing the vehicle to repossession, the predictable output of a product that lends a few hundred dollars against the title of the family's only transportation at triple-digit interest, due in a single balloon payment the CFPB found most borrowers cannot make, since about 80 percent of title loans are rolled over or quickly reborrowed. The loan is underwritten by the repossession. The car was the collateral and the point, item 166 with keys.

Impact: Money

## 508. The Right to Repair Your Own Tractor Is a Legislative Fight

Farmers who buy modern tractors have sued and legislated for the right to repair machines that manufacturers lock behind proprietary software, and the Federal Trade Commission's 2021 Nixing the Fix report documented repair restrictions across tractors, phones, and medical equipment as a systematic practice. Colorado became the first state to guarantee farm equipment repair access in 2023, against the manufacturers' lobbying. The question underneath is older than software: whether buying a thing means owning it, a question the sellers of items 297, 426, and 480 have also answered in the negative.

Impact: Money

## 509. The Prison Tablet Is Free and Everything on It Costs

Corrections departments and their vendors now distribute tablet computers to incarcerated people, marketed as free technology, and then charge by the message, the song, the movie, and the minute for video calls, at rates documented by the Prison Policy Initiative and state reporting that extract millions from the families of item 346. Books that were once donated are replaced by licensed content with a meter. The tablet is genuinely useful, which is the mechanism: every humane improvement to prison life now arrives as a storefront, and the register is staffed by the same industry as item 111.

Impact: Money

## 510. Rural Roads Kill at One and a Half Times the Rate of City Streets

Federal traffic data show that the fatality rate per mile driven on rural roads runs about one and a half times the urban rate, although only about a fifth of Americans live in rural areas, a product of higher speeds, older road design, longer distances, and the ambulance deserts of item 364 waiting at the end of the crash. The rural driver pays item 302's transportation tax in risk rather than money: the road to the consolidated school of item 184, the closed hospital of item 363, and the job in the county seat is the most dangerous daily commute in the country.

Impact: Both

## 511. The School Bus Has No Driver

Surveys of school districts in recent years have repeatedly found the large majority reporting school bus driver shortages, with routes cut, doubled, or consolidated, start times shifted, and students waiting at stops for buses that arrive late or not at all, feeding item 385's absences and item 291's walks. The job requires a commercial license, a background check, and a split shift at wages competing with the delivery apps, and districts are bidding for drivers against the entire logistics economy with the budgets of item 46. The yellow bus is the largest transit system in America, and it is short-handed at the wheel.

Impact: Both

## 512. The National Parks Carry a $23 Billion Maintenance Backlog

The National Park Service's deferred maintenance backlog reached roughly $23 billion in recent federal estimates, trails eroding, water systems failing, and historic structures decaying in the crown jewels of the public estate, in the years when visitation set records and the parks' admirers in Congress posed in them. The backlog is item 196's bridge logic in prettier country: maintenance is the least photogenic line in the budget, so the estate is consumed as an inheritance and bequeathed as a liability, with entrance fees raised on the visitors to patch what appropriations would not.

Impact: Money

## 513. The Census Undercounted the Same People It Always Undercounts

The Census Bureau's own post-enumeration survey of the 2020 census found statistically significant undercounts of the Black population, about 3.3 percent, the Hispanic population, about 5 percent, and Native Americans on reservations, alongside an overcount of the white population, in the count that apportions Congress and distributes hundreds of billions in federal funding for a decade. The errors were not random weather. They were the predictable output of an underfunded count, conducted in a pandemic, under political pressure, of exactly the households items 386 and 492 describe the state as worst at seeing.

Impact: Both

## 514. Public Service Loan Forgiveness Denied 99 Percent of Its First Applicants

When the first cohort of borrowers completed ten years of public service under the Public Service Loan Forgiveness program, the Education Department's own figures showed roughly 99 percent of applications denied, most on technicalities of loan type, repayment plan, and paperwork that the program's servicers had misinformed borrowers about for years, per the Government Accountability Office and item 427's record. Teachers, nurses, and soldiers who had organized their finances around a statutory promise learned the promise had an asterisk administered by its vendor. Congress and the Department spent the following years repairing, case by case, what the fine print had done wholesale.

Impact: Both

## 515. Blue Water Sailors Waited Forty-Four Years for Agent Orange Benefits

Sailors who served on ships off Vietnam, the Blue Water Navy, were excluded from the Agent Orange presumptions granted to troops on the ground, on the theory that the herbicide's dioxin had not reached them through the water their ships distilled and drank, and they litigated and lobbied for decades until Congress finally extended the presumption in the Blue Water Navy Vietnam Veterans Act of 2019. Many of the claimants were dead by the vote. Item 125's burn pit generation studied this precedent and understood it exactly: the government's first answer is no, and its second answer arrives at the funeral pace of the exposed.

Impact: Both

## 516. The Supreme Court Extended the Union Exclusion to the Entire Public Sector

In Janus v. AFSCME in 2018, the Supreme Court held that public sector workers who decline to join their union cannot be required to pay fair share fees for the representation the union must legally provide them, extending item 78's free-rider architecture from the right-to-work states to every public workplace in the nation by constitutional ruling. The decision's logic guarantees the union's duty and dissolves its funding, and its effects compound through the schools of item 50 and the agencies of item 324, whose workers' weakening was not a side effect of the doctrine but its stated purpose by the litigants who financed it.

Impact: Money

## 517. Executing a Prisoner Costs More Than Imprisoning Him for Life

Study after study, in state after state, has found that a death penalty case costs taxpayers far more than life imprisonment, commonly estimated at several times the cost, with California's system alone assessed by researchers at billions spent since 1978 for a small number of executions. The premium buys the constitutionally required process, the appeals, the experts, the segregated death row housing for decades. Whatever position a citizen takes on the punishment itself, the fiscal record is item 338's cousin: the state pays the highest price in its entire justice system for the outcome least often completed.

Impact: Money

# The Pentagon Papers: An Audit of the American Dream — Audit 6



By Mary-Victoria Crockett



This document is part of a series that presents the complete audit from "Not Yours To Take: An Audit Of The American Dream," rewritten and fact-checked, items 1 through 500. Audit 1 holds Parts 1 through 9 (items 1 to 130). Audit 2 holds Parts 10 through 15 and new items 218 to 267. Audit 3 holds new items 268 to 367. Audit 4 holds new items 368 to 442. Audit 5 holds new items 443 to 517. This document holds the final new items, 518 to 567, and closes the series with the author’s final word, carried from the original audit.





## 518. Unemployment Insurance Reaches Only a Fraction of the Unemployed

In ordinary times, only about a quarter to a third of unemployed American workers actually receive unemployment benefits, the product of state rules that exclude part-time workers, misclassified workers from item 83, people who quit for cause the state does not recognize, and earnings thresholds set in another century. When crisis hits, the systems themselves fail, running in some states on decades-old computer code while claims jam for months. The program is called insurance. It behaves like a lottery with a claims department.

Impact: Money





## 519. Mass Layoffs Require Notice, and the Notice Law Is Full of Doors

The WARN Act promises workers sixty days' notice of a mass layoff, and its thresholds and exceptions, employers under 100 workers exempt, faltering companies excused, unforeseeable circumstances undefined, mean that a large share of American layoffs lawfully arrive with no notice at all, the shift simply not on next week's schedule of item 401. Studies of the Act's coverage find it reaches only a minority of displaced workers. The notice was the consolation for the job. For most workers, the consolation is also theoretical.

Impact: Money





## 520. Crime Victims' Compensation Denies the Victims It Exists For

Every state runs a crime victim compensation fund, and an Associated Press investigation of the programs found that Black victims and families are denied compensation at significantly higher rates than white applicants, on grounds that include alleged contributory conduct, late reporting to police, and outstanding warrants, rules that screen out the communities where violence concentrates. The funds pay for funerals and counseling in theory. In practice the application is an interrogation, and the check, like item 470's records, arrives on the state's schedule or not at all.

Impact: Both





## 521. Junk Science Testified in American Courtrooms for a Century

The National Academy of Sciences' landmark 2009 report on forensic science concluded that many techniques courts had admitted for decades, bite mark comparison, hair microscopy, some arson indicators, and aspects of fingerprint and toolmark analysis, lacked adequate scientific foundation, and the exonerations of item 495 include people convicted by exactly these methods, presented by credentialed experts to juries told they were hearing science. The courtroom's gatekeeping failed for a hundred years in the direction of the prosecution, which is the direction item 447's eyewitnesses also err.

Impact: Both





## 522. Drug-Resistant Infections Kill More Than 50,000 Americans a Year in the Antibiotic Era

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's threat assessment, updated in 2026 using 2023 data, counted more than 9.7 million antimicrobial-resistant infections and more than 50,000 deaths a year in the United States, in a country whose antibiotic pipeline is broken by economics: a new antibiotic must be sold sparingly to remain effective, which makes it unprofitable to develop, so the companies that made item 335's fortune leave the field to small firms that keep going bankrupt on success. The miracle drugs of the twentieth century are expiring of market failure, and the surgeries and chemotherapies built on them expire with them.

Impact: Health





## 523. More Than Half of American Suicides Are by Firearm

Federal mortality data show that firearms account for more than half of the roughly 49,000 suicide deaths in the United States each year, and the mechanism is not mysterious: a firearm attempt is fatal about nine times in ten, while the methods most people survive allow the crisis to pass, as most crises do. Veterans, item 126's population, die by firearm at higher rates still. Other wealthy nations have despair, addiction, and mental illness. Their suicide rates are lower in large part because the most lethal instrument is not in the nightstand, a variable the policy debate treats as untouchable and the mortality tables treat as decisive.

Impact: Health





## 524. A Dead Zone the Size of a State Forms in the Gulf Every Summer

Each summer, fertilizer runoff from the farms of items 43 and 309 rides the Mississippi River into the Gulf of Mexico and feeds an algal bloom whose decay consumes the water's oxygen, creating a hypoxic dead zone that federal scientists measured at roughly 6,700 square miles in 2024, about the size of New Jersey, in which shrimp, crabs, and fish cannot live. The zone has a federal task force, a goal set decades ago, and a deadline that has been postponed as routinely as item 136's targets. The river collects the whole watershed's decisions and delivers them to the fishermen.

Impact: Both





## 525. The Country Loses a Billion Tons of Topsoil a Year

Agricultural researchers estimate that American farmland loses topsoil to erosion at a pace exceeding a billion tons a year, soil that took centuries to form washing into the rivers of item 524 and blowing off fields managed for this year's yield at the expense of the next generation's. The Dust Bowl is taught as history. The erosion rate is taught nowhere, and the farm programs of items 333 and 490 pay for production practices with barely a condition attached about the asset being liquidated underneath them, the only capital in this audit that cannot be rebuilt at any price.

Impact: Money





## 526. Pregnant Workers Could Be Fired for Needing a Chair Until 2023

For forty-five years after the Pregnancy Discrimination Act, American law forbade firing a worker for being pregnant while permitting employers to refuse her the small accommodations, a stool, a water bottle, lighter lifting, that her condition required, and women lost jobs and pregnancies at the seam between the two rules. The Pregnant Workers Fairness Act finally required reasonable accommodation when it took effect in 2023, which means that within the living memory of today's workforce, the law of the richest country on Earth treated a pregnant cashier's chair as an employer's prerogative.

Impact: Both





## 527. Online Charter Schools Lose Students a Year of Learning

Stanford's CREDO research center, the standard evaluator of charter school performance, found that students in full-time online charter schools learned so much less than comparable public school students that the gap equaled losing about 180 days of learning in math in a single school year, and 72 days in reading, results replicated across CREDO's studies even as enrollment in virtual charters grew. The model collects full per-pupil funding for an education delivered at a fraction of the cost, and its report card is written in the only currency that matters, the children's learning, in red.

Impact: Both





## 528. One Student in Ten Is Learning English in a System Short of Teachers for Them

Federal education data count English learners at roughly one in ten American public school students, a population that has grown for decades, while schools report persistent shortages of teachers certified to teach them and districts satisfy the legal obligation to serve them with whoever is available. The civil rights law is clear that language barriers may not function as exclusion from education. The staffing tables are equally clear about the distance between the obligation and the building, a distance measured, as in item 383, in the futures of the children assigned to absorb it.

Impact: Both





## 529. Heirs' Property Quietly Transfers Black Land to Anyone But the Heirs

When a landowner dies without a will, the land passes to all heirs as fractional co-owners, and over generations a single parcel can accumulate hundreds of owners, no one of whom can mortgage, improve, or in many states even protect it, while a single distant heir or an outside speculator who buys one share can force a sale of the whole at auction prices. Researchers have identified heirs' property as a primary mechanism of Black land loss in the South, locking up billions of dollars of land value, and the partition sales have historically paid the family in fractions of what the land was worth to keep.

Impact: Both





## 530. The Pawnshop Is the Bank of Last Resort and Charges Like It

Millions of American households rely on pawnshops, where a loan against a wedding ring, a tool set, or a television carries interest and fees that commonly reach triple-digit annual rates and ends, for the borrowers who cannot redeem in time, in forfeiture of the goods, which was always an acceptable outcome for the lender. The pawnshop is honest about its terms in a way item 166 is not, and its persistence at scale, in the richest financial system in history, is the audit's small glass window into how many people the system of items 168 and 179 has left outside the door.

Impact: Money





## 531. One Hacked Vendor Froze the Nation's Pharmacies and Hospitals

In February 2024, a ransomware attack on Change Healthcare, a single claims-processing company inside UnitedHealth Group, paralyzed billing for a large share of American healthcare for weeks, with pharmacies unable to process prescriptions, hospitals unable to verify coverage or get paid, and the personal data of more than 192 million people exposed in what became the largest healthcare data breach in American history. The system of Part 1 had quietly centralized its plumbing into one pipe, defended it at one company's standard, and billed the public for the flood.

Impact: Both





## 532. The Intercity Bus Is Disappearing From the Towns That Need It

Intercity bus service, the cheapest way to travel between American towns and the only public transportation in much of the rural map, has contracted for decades, and the system's largest carrier has sold off its downtown stations to developers, leaving curbside stops without shelter, information, or a ticket window in the communities where the riders are poorest. The rider of item 302's transit desert who must reach a specialist two counties away, a court date, or a family funeral faces the distance in a car they may not have, on the roads of item 510, because the bus that served their grandparents was a line on a balance sheet.

Impact: Money





## 533. Abandoned Mines Undermine the Towns Above Them

Beneath the coalfields of Appalachia and the mining country of the West lie hundreds of thousands of abandoned mine features, tunnels, shafts, and waste piles mapped imperfectly by the federal Abandoned Mine Land program, whose inventory of unreclaimed hazards runs past ten billion dollars. Houses crack as old workings subside, water runs orange from portals a century abandoned, and the companies that dug the voids are dissolved into the same bankruptcy courts as item 438, leaving the surface owners, who never signed a royalty check, holding the deed to a collapsing asset.

Impact: Both





## 534. Women Veterans Are the Fastest-Growing Veterans and the VA Was Built Without Them

About two million women have served in the American military, and women are the fastest-growing population of veterans, yet Government Accountability Office reviews have repeatedly found VA facilities short of women's health providers, lacking the privacy and equipment for basic gynecological care, and fielding harassment complaints from women veterans at the hospitals meant to serve them. Item 121's backlog and item 285's discharges fall on this population inside a system whose intake forms, clinics, and culture were designed when the veteran was assumed to be a man, an assumption the recruiting of item 341 outgrew decades ago.

Impact: Health





## 535. Museums Still Hold the Remains of More Than a Hundred Thousand Native Ancestors

ProPublica's Repatriation Project, compiling the federal government's own data in 2023, found that museums, universities, and agencies still held the remains of more than 110,000 Native American ancestors, three decades after the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act ordered their return, with many institutions having classified remains as culturally unidentifiable, a category that functioned as a shelf. The universities that teach anthropology kept the ancestors of the tribes whose children item 353 counts in storage drawers. The law said return them. The drawers said otherwise, for thirty years.

Impact: Both





## 536. Puerto Rico's Grid Fails Island-Wide and the Bills Rise Anyway

Puerto Rico's electricity system, privatized in its transmission and distribution under a contract with LUMA Energy after Hurricane Maria destroyed the old grid, has continued to suffer island-wide blackouts, including one that darkened nearly the entire island on New Year's Eve of 2024, while residents pay some of the highest electricity rates under the American flag for the least reliable service under it. The island's citizens, item 358's nonvoters, cannot vote out the government that signed the contract or the Congress that oversees the arrangement, which is the arrangement.

Impact: Both





## 537. The Carried Interest Loophole Outlives Every President Who Promises to Close It

Private equity and hedge fund managers have their compensation taxed at the lower capital gains rate through the carried interest loophole, on the theory that managing other people's money is an investment in itself, a preference that presidents of both parties have campaigned against for two decades and that survives every Congress intact, at a cost in revenue measured in billions per decade. The loophole is small as these things go. Its immortality is the exhibit: a provision with no constituency but its beneficiaries and no defense but their lobbyists, undefeated, in a tax code item 174's nurses file under.

Impact: Money





## 538. Corporations Spend a Trillion-Dollar Decade Buying Their Own Stock

S&P 500 companies have spent sums approaching a trillion dollars a year at the peak on share buybacks, $923 billion in 2022 alone, a practice that was treated as illegal market manipulation until the SEC legalized it in 1982 and that now absorbs cash that could fund the wages of item 401 or the maintenance of item 512. Buybacks raise share prices and executive compensation tied to them, and their modern scale dates precisely from the 2017 tax law's corporate cuts, which were sold as investment in workers and arrived, in the main, as this.

Impact: Money





## 539. Election Day Is a Working Tuesday and the Law Guarantees No Time to Vote

The United States votes on a Tuesday in November because an 1845 law scheduled around farmers' travel to county seats by horse, and most democracies that have examined the question vote on weekends or holidays, while federal law guarantees American workers no paid time off to vote at all, leaving a patchwork of state rules that many hourly workers cannot use without losing the shift of item 401. The franchise of item 217 is thus administered inside a workday designed before electricity, and the line at the polling place at 6 p.m. is the schedule's honest self-portrait.

Impact: Both





## 540. The Watchdogs Can Be Fired, and Were

Inspectors general, the internal watchdogs Congress installed in every major agency after Watergate, serve at the pleasure of the president they audit, and in January 2025 more than a dozen of them were dismissed in a single weekend, following earlier cycles of removals and long vacancies that the Government Accountability Office has documented across administrations. An audit culture that depends on the audited party's permission to exist produces audits like item 338's, of the Pentagon, by accountants the Pentagon's commander may remove. The watchdog's leash is held by the watched.

Impact: Both





## 541. Hospice Fraud Grew an Industry Out of the Dying

Medicare's hospice benefit pays a per-day rate for the care of the terminally ill, and investigators, journalists, and the Medicare program's own integrity contractors have documented an explosion of suspect hospices, concentrated in a few states, with Los Angeles County alone licensing more than 1,800 hospices, many enrolling patients who were not terminally ill, billing for care never delivered, and churning ownership faster than regulators could follow. The dying are the perfect victims of item 176's logic: unlikely to complain, briefly enrolled, and surrounded by families too deep in grief to audit the invoices.

Impact: Both





## 542. Government Shutdowns Make Federal Workers Labor Without Pay

During the thirty-five-day government shutdown of 2018 and 2019, roughly 800,000 federal employees were furloughed or required to work without pay, including the air traffic controllers of item 484, the food inspectors of item 307, and the park rangers of item 512, who lined up at food banks in the capital of the richest nation on Earth while their back pay waited on the legislation that eventually guaranteed it. The workers were not parties to the dispute. They were its leverage, and the leverage was their rent, in a labor practice that item 79 would call theft if a private employer attempted it.

Impact: Both



## 543. A Worker's Death Fines Out at the Price of a Used Car

The AFL-CIO's annual Death on the Job accounting has repeatedly found that the average federal OSHA penalty following a workplace fatality runs to only tens of thousands of dollars, in a system whose maximum fines were frozen by statute from 1990 to 2015 and whose inspectors number roughly one for every 85,000 workers. Item 218 counted the dead at one every 99 minutes. This is the price list attached to them, and employers' safety budgets are set by people who can read it: prevention is optional at these prices, and the fine is a line item.

Impact: Both





## 544. Nine in Ten Poor Americans Face Civil Court Alone

The Legal Services Corporation's Justice Gap research found that low-income Americans received no legal help, or not enough of it, for 92 percent of their substantial civil legal problems, the evictions of item 494, the custody fights of item 288, the benefit denials of item 123, in a system that funds legal aid at a level that has never matched the need the studies measure. The civil right to counsel does not exist. What exists is a hotline, a waiting list, and a courtroom where the other side, per item 106, has a professional.

Impact: Both





## 545. A Third of American Counties Have Nowhere to Give Birth

The March of Dimes' maternity care reports classify roughly a third of U.S. counties as maternity care deserts, with no hospital offering obstetric care, no birth center, and no obstetric provider, a map that has grown as rural hospitals of item 363 close their labor and delivery units first, because obstetrics loses money under every payer. The women of item 282 drive an hour or more in labor, or deliver in emergency rooms staffed for other emergencies, in the country that spends item 500's fortune on healthcare.

Impact: Health





## 546. One Company Dominates Your Eyes From Lens to Store

A single corporation, EssilorLuxottica, owns or controls an empire spanning lens manufacturing, the dominant frame brands, the largest retail chains in American eyewear, and a major vision insurer, an integration that consumer advocates and antitrust critics have blamed for the price of a pair of glasses in the United States, where the average pair costs several hundred dollars for materials worth a fraction of it. The antitrust authorities approved the mergers that built the stack. The customer at the counter, squinting at the invoice, is negotiating with the whole vertical at once.

Impact: Money





## 547. Toledo Could Not Drink Its Water for Three Days

In August 2014, the city of Toledo, Ohio ordered roughly half a million people not to drink, cook with, or bathe in the municipal water for three days, after toxins from a Lake Erie algal bloom, fed by the farm runoff of item 524, overwhelmed the treatment plant and entered the supply. The National Guard distributed bottled water in an American city in peacetime because the lake had been fertilized past the point of potability. The bloom returns each summer on schedule. So does the runoff. The emergency was the only part that was temporary.

Impact: Both





## 548. The Colorado River Was Promised to More People Than It Has Water For

The Colorado River, which supplies water to some 40 million people and millions of acres of farmland across the West, was divided by a 1922 compact and later agreements into paper rights totaling more water than the river has carried in the twenty-first century, an over-allocation made in an unusually wet period and defended ever since as property. Lake Mead's fall exposed the arithmetic to the naked eye. The negotiations to reconcile the paper with the river proceed at the pace of item 136's climate policy, which is to say behind the hydrology.

Impact: Both





## 549. Nearly 300,000 Children Were Married in America This Century

Research by the advocacy organization Unchained At Last, compiled from state marriage records, counted nearly 300,000 minors married in the United States between 2000 and 2018, overwhelmingly girls married to adult men, under laws that permit child marriage with parental consent or judicial approval, exceptions that remain on the books in most states, with only eighteen states having banned marriage under 18 outright as of 2026. The marriages carry the legal consequences of adulthood, including, in many states, an inability to file for divorce or enter a shelter as a minor, a trap door in the law with a wedding license on it.

Impact: Both





## 550. The Fastest-Growing Homeless Population Is Over Fifty

Research led by Dr. Margot Kushel at the University of California, San Francisco has documented that adults over fifty now make up roughly half of single adults experiencing homelessness, a population that was a small fraction a generation ago, driven by rents that outran fixed incomes, the benefit levels of item 130, and a lifetime of item 88's wages arriving at old age with no savings and one emergency, item 178's cascade, from the street. The shelter system was designed for younger men. It is filling with grandmothers.

Impact: Both





## 551. Most College Professors Are Now Temps

The American Association of University Professors' data show that roughly seven in ten faculty members in American higher education now hold contingent appointments, adjuncts and lecturers hired by the course, without tenure, often without benefits or any assurance of next semester's work, at pay that has made the adjunct food bank a genre of campus reporting. The students of item 181 pay tenure-track prices. The instruction is delivered substantially by the gig workforce of item 83, in gowns, and the savings are visible in item 387's endowment reports.

Impact: Both





## 552. Parents Borrowed $110 Billion for Their Children's Degrees and Cannot Retire on It

Federal data count more than $110 billion in Parent PLUS loans, borrowed by parents, disproportionately Black and low-income parents, to fill the gap between their children's financial aid and item 192's prices, loans that carry higher interest than student loans, fewer repayment protections, and balances that follow the borrowers into retirement, where the government may garnish Social Security for default. The loan was marketed as a parent's love made financial. Its terms are item 48's system reaching up a generation to collect from the people who already paid for the childhood.

Impact: Both





## 553. A Condo Tower Fell Down and Florida Counted Its Other Towers

In June 2021, the Champlain Towers South condominium in Surfside, Florida collapsed in the night, killing 98 people, in a building whose owners had deferred millions in documented structural repairs while the state's inspection regime asked almost nothing of aging coastal towers. The investigations and the grand jury that followed described a statewide population of similar buildings, similar deferred concrete, and similar boards voting down the assessments. Florida rewrote its condo laws afterward. The rewrite, like item 196's bridges and item 453's dams, is an epitaph offered as a policy.

## To What Do We Owe Each Other?: A Final Word From The Author In Six Parts



I. Restatement of Purpose

The Constitution of the United States is not a metaphor. It is not a relic, nor a poetic flourish, nor a symbolic ideal to be admired from a distance. It is a binding agreement, a covenant, between the governed and those who govern. And that agreement is not abstract. It guarantees, in no uncertain terms, the inalienable rights to life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness.

This book was written as an audit of that agreement.

Across five hundred case studies, I have documented the ways in which the United States government has failed to uphold its side of the contract. Not just in one arena. Not just in isolated scandals or sporadic acts of corruption. But across healthcare, housing, education, infrastructure, civil rights, and economic opportunity, in ways that are systemic, predictable, and avoidable.

Each failure documented here is not a mistake. It is evidence.

Each grievance is not an isolated injustice. It is proof.

What you are holding is not simply a book. It is a body of evidence in a class action waiting to be filed.

This is not a manifesto of anger, though it would be justified. It is a legal, moral, and economic indictment of a government that has abandoned the very people whose consent it claims to represent.

The Preamble begins with the words We the People, and it is We the People who have been defrauded.

The time has come not to ask for our rights, but to reassert them. Not to wait for change, but to demand legal remedy. The American government has failed to honor its binding obligations, and under that failure, we retain the right to hold it accountable.



II. The Legal Basis for Accountability

A. The Constitution as a Contract

At its core, the U.S. Constitution is a foundational contract, a written, ratified agreement establishing the relationship between a government and its people. It outlines duties, powers, protections, and guarantees. In any other context, corporate, civic, or personal, the deliberate and repeated failure to uphold a contract would constitute a breach. The same must apply here.

Consider the language: "We hold these truths to be self-evident, that all men are created equal, that they are endowed… with certain unalienable Rights, that among these are Life, Liberty, and the Pursuit of Happiness."

These are not aspirational guidelines. They are declared guarantees. A government that fails to prevent preventable death, that strips liberty through poverty or incarceration, or that systematically blocks access to opportunity, is not functioning within the bounds of its original charter. It is acting in breach.

The Constitution also includes legal mechanisms designed to protect those guarantees:

* The Due Process Clauses of the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments explicitly prohibit the government from depriving any person of "life, liberty, or property, without due process of law."

* The Equal Protection Clause mandates that no person shall be denied the equal protection of the laws.

* The Supremacy Clause makes the Constitution the "supreme Law of the Land," meaning its guarantees cannot be overridden by negligence, inaction, or institutional decay.

If the state cannot or will not meet these obligations, particularly when harm is foreseeable and repeated, the people have both the moral standing and the legal precedent to demand redress.

B. The Obligation of Care

Governments have a duty of care to their citizens, a legal and ethical responsibility to protect from foreseeable harm, provide equal access to justice, and uphold basic living standards. When a private entity neglects this duty, the result is tort litigation. When a doctor violates this duty, it's malpractice. When the state violates it, the result should not be impunity. It should be accountability.

Yet we live in a country where:

* Insulin is unaffordable and Americans die rationing it;

* Children work in slaughterhouses while billionaires avoid taxes;

* Veterans sleep on the streets they once defended;

* Entire communities drink poisoned water and are told it's not a federal concern.

This is not the behavior of a functioning democracy. It is the behavior of a contract broken, by design, and by negligence.

The government of the United States has not merely failed to act. It has systematically abandoned its duty of care, and in so doing, has forfeited the moral and legal protections it once claimed.



III. The Moral and Economic Stakes

A. The Cost in Lives

A government's primary duty is to safeguard life. When systems repeatedly fail in ways that result in preventable deaths, that government is not just failing morally. It is violating a binding obligation.

This audit has presented dozens of examples where death is not incidental, but inevitable under current policy structures:

* Insulin rationing leads to thousands of deaths annually.

* Rural hospital closures force delays in emergency care, increasing mortality from stroke, heart attack, and childbirth.

* Unhoused individuals in cities with no shelter access face freezing temperatures, untreated infections, and suicide.

* Lack of mental health infrastructure contributes to escalating suicide rates, overdose deaths, and incarceration.

* Food insecurity among children leads to long-term developmental issues and increases the risk of early mortality.

Every one of these deaths was foreseeable. Every one was preventable. And every one should be counted, not just as a tragedy, but as evidence in a case against the state.

B. The Cost in GDP and Economic Potential

For those who remain unmoved by human suffering, let us speak in the only language that seems to cross party lines: money.

Every inefficiency, every neglected crisis, every life lost unnecessarily is a hit to our gross domestic product, to our tax base, to our innovation pipeline, and to the long-term sustainability of the American economy.

* Medical bankruptcy costs over $195 billion in unpaid debt and lost productivity.

* Untreated mental illness drains over $300 billion per year in absenteeism, healthcare costs, and incarceration.

* Poor educational outcomes and student debt lower homeownership rates, reduce small business formation, and stunt GDP growth.

* Preventable homelessness costs cities more per person per year than permanent supportive housing would.

* Mass incarceration costs the U.S. over $80 billion annually, and robs entire communities of their labor, their parents, and their future taxpayers.

These are not isolated statistics. They are economic hemorrhages, slow-bleeding wounds the state has no apparent intention of stitching shut. To speak plainly: poverty is expensive, neglect is expensive, and preventable death is the single most expensive inefficiency in a nation's ledger.

This is not just a moral indictment. It is a fiscal audit of national mismanagement, and the bill is being paid in blood, bankruptcy, and broken futures.



IV. The Precedent for a Legal Challenge

A. Breach of Social Contract as Justiciable Harm

While "life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness" are often dismissed as philosophical, courts have recognized that the government may be held liable when its actions, or inaction, result in systemic harm, particularly when:

* A right is clearly established (such as bodily autonomy, due process, or equal protection);

* The harm is foreseeable;

* The state had a duty of care or established policy that resulted in disproportionate injury.

Examples of successful challenges include:

* Brown v. Board of Education (1954): Declared racial segregation in education a violation of the Equal Protection Clause.

* Goldberg v. Kelly (1970): Held that due process must be followed before welfare benefits are terminated.

* Estelle v. Gamble (1976): Recognized that deliberate indifference to serious medical needs of prisoners violates the Eighth Amendment.

Each of these cases shows that systemic government action or neglect can be challenged in court when it results in unequal treatment, bodily harm, or denial of due process.

The government may argue that there is no positive right to healthcare, housing, or food, but that argument ignores that the government has taken affirmative steps to create systems for all three. If those systems then disproportionately fail or abandon the people they were designed to protect, courts have found there is legal standing to sue.

B. Viable Legal Pathways

Several potential legal theories exist to structure a case around:

1. Equal Protection Clause (14th Amendment)

When services or protections are systematically denied to specific groups (e.g. disabled individuals, unhoused veterans, Black maternal patients), there is precedent to claim discriminatory impact.

2. Due Process Clause (5th and 14th Amendments)

If a state creates a life-dependent system (e.g. healthcare via Medicaid or housing via HUD) and then arbitrarily removes or fails to fund that system, plaintiffs may argue they were deprived of life or liberty without due process.

3. State-Created Danger Doctrine

While controversial, this doctrine holds that the government may be liable when its actions create or worsen a known danger, such as through deregulation, denial of care, or policy-driven neglect (e.g. water contamination in Flint or Camp Lejeune).

4. Public Trust Doctrine

Historically used in environmental law, this doctrine suggests that certain resources (e.g. clean water, public health infrastructure) are held in trust for the people. When that trust is breached, the people may sue as the injured party.

5. Mass Tort / Class Action Model

While unconventional, a hybrid civil claim combining these constitutional harms with mass tort structure could be created, particularly if supported by state or NGO attorneys to demonstrate systemic failure.



V. A Call to Organize: The American Right to Class Action Accountability

The United States was not founded on obedience. It was born out of grievance. This nation exists because its architects believed that when a government fails its people, consistently, predictably, and without remorse, the people have a duty to demand redress.

Today, the systems meant to protect us have been quietly restructured to extract from us instead. We are told to pull ourselves up by our bootstraps in a country where entire neighborhoods don't even have sidewalks. We are told to trust markets more than medicine, billionaires more than scientists, corporations more than community.

But rights are not abstract. They are not symbolic. They are enforceable. And when inalienable rights are denied, not as isolated errors but as policy trends, it is not only reasonable to act. It is necessary.

The Legal Mechanism: Class Action Suits as Constitutional Accountability

Class action lawsuits exist for one purpose: to consolidate the voices of the many who have been wronged by a powerful few. In this case, the defendant is not a single corporation. It is a government that has abdicated its role as protector.

If a private entity sold you a contract promising safety, stability, and liberty, and then poisoned your water, denied your healthcare, priced you out of shelter, and let your children starve, that entity would be sued into oblivion. Why should the federal government be immune from the same standards of accountability?

There is precedent for large-scale suits against the government, including:

* The Flint Water Crisis settlement, where over $600 million was awarded for government-caused poisoning.

* Camp Lejeune water contamination litigation, where exposure to toxins led to widespread health issues.

* Veterans' lawsuits over burn pits, Agent Orange, and denied mental health care.

Each of these involved a pattern of harm, government inaction, and people organizing around truth. What we need now is to do the same, not in isolation, but in unison. A full audit of harms. A coordinated demand for redress.

The People's Power

It starts with the truth. Then it becomes a record. Then it becomes a rally. Then it becomes a case.

This book is part of that case, an exhibit of evidence, a public ledger of harm, and a blueprint for action.

Let this be a beginning. Let every grievance documented here become part of a unified voice for justice. The United States government, by its own design, is answerable to the people. If it will not answer, then let us serve it notice.



VI. Final Declaration — What We Are Owed

The United States government was not built to serve power. It was built to serve people. And in its founding documents, the promise was not vague. It was binding. It pledged to protect life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness. Not for the wealthy few. Not for corporations. For all.

But promises without accountability are propaganda. And contracts without enforcement are illusions. For decades now, the American people have been told to wait. To be patient. To suffer quietly through denied care, poisoned water, unaffordable housing, canceled pensions, collapsed bridges, school shootings, bankruptcies, blackouts, broken systems, and empty apologies.

This is not the fine print of democracy. This is breach of contract.

A government that cannot guarantee its people the minimum conditions for dignity and survival is not functioning. It is failing. And when a government fails to uphold the contract from which its legitimacy is derived, the people have every right, and every moral duty, to demand a reckoning.

We are not asking for charity. We are demanding equity.

We are not pleading for scraps. We are collecting the debt.

We are not seeking revenge. We are seeking repair.

The question at the heart of this book is not just legal. It is moral. And it is timeless.

If we truly mean to call this a democracy, if we truly believe in the idea that all people are created equal, then the systems that govern us must reflect that truth not just in word, but in practice.

And so, to the politicians, the courts, the architects of austerity and the authors of neglect, we offer not just a book, but a ledger. Not just a grievance, but a case. Not just a protest, but a demand.

To what do we owe each other?

That is the question now placed before the United States of America.

I'm not sure I know the answer yet.

But I would like to find out.
