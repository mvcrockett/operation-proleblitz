# Project Nomad: Free and Nearly Free Stays for RVs and Tiny Homes on Wheels

Verified data for the Project Nomad guide on the Operation ProleBlitz website. Researched November 17, 2026. Every stay limit, fee, and prohibition below carries the source it was checked against. Anything that could not be verified from a real source is listed at the end under Could Not Verify, not mixed into the tables.

The honest headline for central Arkansas: the free system is federal land. The Ouachita National Forest and a set of primitive Army Corps of Engineers areas around Lake Ouachita are the verified free stays near Maumelle. In town, at state parks, and at developed campgrounds, expect a nightly fee. The good news is that the Ouachita forest rule is more generous than the national norm, and the cheap paid stops nearby make a full rotation work.

### A category to know about, with no verified RV spot yet

Arkansas Game and Fish Commission wildlife management areas allow primitive camping in designated areas on some WMAs. The rules in the Arkansas code include a 14 consecutive day limit, a 30 day per calendar year cap, a rule that a camp may not sit unoccupied for more than 48 consecutive hours, and a permit or license requirement. A permit change reported in July 2026 points to a 10.50 dollar annual WMA and Lake Conservation Permit for users 16 and older who do not hold a hunting or fishing license. No specific WMA campsite near Maumelle was verified as fitting an RV or tiny home on wheels from an official AGFC page in this research, so no WMA spot is named as a place in this guide. Confirm the current permit name, price, and a specific designated campsite with AGFC before relying on this category. Sources: https://regulations.justia.com/states/arkansas/agency-002/division-01/rule-002-01-22-123/ and https://arkansasoutside.com/agfc-wma-lake-conservation-permit-arkansas/

## Part B: The national rulebook, the part that works anywhere

### US Forest Service dispersed camping
Camping outside a developed campground is allowed in most national forests, free, with no services. The common national limit is 14 days, but each forest sets its own stay limit, return waiting period, and move distance by forest order, and there is no single national move distance. The Ouachita National Forest is a documented exception to the 14 day norm, with a 30 day limit and a 5 road mile move, covered in Part A. Always check the specific forest page and its current orders before a long stay, because fire restrictions and closures change.
Sources: https://www.fs.usda.gov/r05/recreation/dispersed-camping and an example of a forest setting its own tighter rule at https://www.fs.usda.gov/r02/sanjuan/alerts/forest-wide-camping-stay-limits

### Bureau of Land Management dispersed camping
Dispersed camping on BLM land is generally limited to 14 days within any 28 day period. The 28 day clock starts when you first occupy a location, and the 14 days can be reached across separate visits. After the 14th day you must move outside a 25 mile radius of the previous location until the 29th day. Exact wording varies by state and field office.
Sources: https://www.blm.gov/programs/recreation/camping and https://www.blm.gov/programs/recreation/recreation-activities/california

### Army Corps of Engineers parks
Camping at one or more campsites at any one Corps water project is limited to 14 days in a 30 day period without written permission from the District Commander. Moving to a different campground on the same lake does not reset the count, because the limit runs by project, not by campground. Most developed Corps campgrounds charge a nightly fee and book through Recreation.gov. Some Corps lakes also keep primitive camping areas with no charge at all, and Lake Ouachita in Arkansas is a verified example, covered in Part A.
Sources: https://www.swf-wc.usace.army.mil/bardwell/Recreation/Camping/ and https://www.recreation.gov/camping/campgrounds/233098?tab=info

### Bureau of Reclamation lands
The federal regulation at 43 CFR 423.33 caps camping at 14 days in any 30 consecutive days at a single Reclamation facility or in a single location on Reclamation land, and after 14 days you must move at least 10 miles. Some Reclamation campgrounds are low cost rather than free.
Sources: https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/43/423.33 and https://www.usbr.gov/pn/snakeriver/landuse/blackcanyon/montour.html

### TVA lands
TVA runs six dam reservoir campgrounds through a concessionaire. They are paid campgrounds, open March 15 to November 15, with stays limited to 21 days in high season, May 1 to September 30, and 30 days in the off season, October 1 to April 30. TVA also allows camping on some undeveloped public land, but no free stay limit for that use was verified in this research, so this guide does not promise one.
Source: https://www.tva.com/Environment/Recreation/Camping

### State highway rest areas, and the Arkansas rule
National pattern: there is no national rest area rule. Each state sets its own. Rest areas exist for rest, not camping. Sleeping inside your vehicle is treated as rest in many states, while awnings, chairs, grills, tents, and other camping setups are treated as camping and are prohibited in most places. Posted signs at the individual rest area govern.
Arkansas, stated plainly: no official ARDOT page was found that clearly allows or clearly prohibits overnight vehicle sleep statewide, and Arkansas publishes no statewide overnight time limit or camping rule for its rest areas. Some Arkansas rest areas and welcome centers have RV dump stations. Because the state publishes no clear rule, do not build a rotation that depends on an Arkansas rest area. Treat one as an emergency rest stop where you sleep inside the rig with nothing set up outside, read the posted signs, and move on.
Sources: https://ardot.gov/traveler-info/ and https://camperfaqs.com/can-you-legally-sleep-overnight-at-a-highway-rest-area and https://roadtrippers.com/magazine/overnight-rv-parking-at-rest-stops/

### Walmart overnight parking
Walmart's own answer: the company permits RV parking on store lots as it is able. Permission is given by the individual store manager, based on parking space availability and local laws, and shoppers are asked to contact management at each store before parking. Walmart offers no electrical service or RV hookups. A local ordinance banning overnight parking overrides a manager's yes. This is one night courtesy parking, not camping, and nothing goes outside the rig.
Source: https://corporate.walmart.com/askwalmart

### Cracker Barrel overnight parking
Many Cracker Barrel locations allow overnight RV parking and many have designated RV and bus spaces, but it is not a chain wide guarantee. Company customer service, as quoted in reputable RV coverage, says some locations allow it and others do not, and the decision sits with the individual restaurant manager. Call the specific store ahead or check the lot signs. One night, no hookups, no camping setup. Note on sourcing: no official Cracker Barrel policy page publishing an overnight rule was found, so this rule rests on reputable coverage quoting the company directly.
Sources: https://www.rvtravel.com/cracker-barrel-banning-overnight-camping/ and https://roadtrippers.com/magazine/overnight-rv-parking-at-cracker-barrel/

### Harvest Hosts and Boondockers Welcome
These are paid annual memberships, not free public land. Harvest Hosts Classic is 99 dollars per year. Harvest Hosts plus Boondockers Welcome is 169 dollars per year. All Access is 179 dollars per year. A standalone Boondockers Welcome membership is 79 dollars per year, and Boondockers Welcome is now part of the Harvest Hosts family.
How the stays work: the standard Harvest Hosts stay is one night. Some hosts allow up to four extra nights, often for a fee the host sets. Rigs must be self contained, with an interior toilet and a cooking setup. Hookups are host by host and usually paid. The etiquette is part of the deal: support a business host with a purchase of about 30 dollars or more for the night. Boondockers Welcome hosts are private property owners who allow stays at no charge beyond gratitude, for one or more nights, and hookups there are paid if you use them.
Sources: https://www.harvesthosts.com/blog/what-is-harvest-hosts-a-complete-guide-to-how-it-works and https://www.harvesthosts.com/blog/boondockers-welcome-is-joining-the-harvest-hosts-family

### Long Term Visitor Areas, the snowbird exception
BLM Long Term Visitor Areas in the Southwest desert, including La Posa near Quartzsite, Arizona, are fee based, not free. They exist so winter visitors can legally stay for months. The long term permit costs 180 dollars and covers continuous use of designated LTVAs from September 15 to April 15, a full 7 months. The short visit permit costs 40 dollars for any 14 consecutive day period in that season and can be renewed. Permits work at all BLM LTVAs. Off season, April 16 to September 14, separate daily and annual fees apply and the max stay drops back to 14 days in a 28 day period.
Source: https://www.blm.gov/visit/la-posa-long-term-visitor-area

### Free municipal and county parks
The pattern is real and verified nationally: some small towns, especially in the Midwest and Plains, offer free RV sites in city or county parks, sometimes with electric and water hookups. Common patterns include the first two nights free with a charge after that and a 10 day total limit, free stays capped at 3 days, free stays up to 14 nights with donations accepted, and a first night free with paid nights after. Sites are small, first come first served, and water can be seasonal, so confirm with the town before counting on one. No Arkansas town example was verified from an official city page in this research, so this guide names none. The category is listed here so readers in other states know to look, and so Arkansas readers know to ask their town hall rather than assume.
Source for the pattern: https://www.travelswithted.com/free-rv-parking-with-hookups/

## Part C: A worked rotation in central Arkansas

Here is one honest way to string the verified pieces together for a tiny home on wheels based near Maumelle. Distances are short, which matters, because every move burns fuel and resets nothing for free.

**Days 1 to 30, Ouachita National Forest dispersed camping.** Pick a legal dispersed spot on the Perryville or Jessieville side of the forest, inside the 100 feet from water rule and on an established pull off your rig can actually reach. Cost: 0 dollars. You are fully self contained. No water, no dump, no hookups. The forest clock: out by day 30, move at least 5 road miles, and that exact campsite is closed to you for the rest of the calendar year. Total forest cap is 90 days per year, so this leg can repeat in new spots but cannot run forever.

**Reset stop, 1 to 2 nights.** Swing through a paid anchor to dump tanks, fill fresh water, shower, and do laundry in town. Cheap verified options: South Fourche at 5 dollars a night with no services, Charlton at 15 dollars a night with water and a bathhouse, or a Class A Corps campground on Lake Ouachita at 14 to 30 dollars with a dump station. A state park dump station costs 6 dollars per use for non registered campers if you only need the dump and water, per the published fee schedule.

**Days 33 to 46, Lake Ouachita Corps primitive areas.** Move to one of the no charge primitive areas on the north end of Lake Ouachita, such as Buckville or Irons Fork. Cost: 0 dollars. The Corps clock is separate from the forest clock: 14 days in any 30 at the Lake Ouachita project. Still no water and no dump at the primitive areas themselves, which is why the reset stop comes first.

**Transit nights, as needed.** A Harvest Hosts night at 0 dollars a night after the annual membership, plus about 30 dollars of purchases at the host business by etiquette, or a Walmart or Cracker Barrel night with the manager's permission, covers the gaps when a move runs long. These are single nights, not stays.

**Then back to the forest, a new spot at least 5 road miles from the first.** The Ouachita clock allows it. The Lake Ouachita Corps clock needs its own 30 day window to reset, so alternate the two systems instead of leaning on one.

### The honest limits
- **No hookups is the default.** Every free leg above means carrying your own water, power, and waste capacity. Solar, batteries, and tank size decide how long a free stay actually lasts, and for most rigs that is days, not the full legal limit.
- **Dumps and water cost money and trips.** Budget a paid anchor night or a dump fee roughly every 1 to 2 weeks. Verified anchors: 6 dollar state park dump fee for non campers, 5 dollar South Fourche nights, 14 to 30 dollar Corps Class A nights with dump stations.
- **Moving is not free.** Each reposition burns fuel and a day. As a labeled example, not a sourced price: a 60 mile move in a rig that gets 10 miles per gallon at 3 dollars per gallon costs about 18 dollars in fuel. Short hops inside one forest are the cheapest moves there are.
- **Winter has a cheap legal answer, and it is not in Arkansas.** If a winter away is acceptable, the BLM Long Term Visitor Area permit is 180 dollars for September 15 to April 15 near Quartzsite, Arizona. That is the lowest legal cost per month of standing still anywhere in this guide.
- **Parking, camping, and residing are three different legal things.** Parking is a vehicle standing still, and it is governed by whoever owns the lot plus local ordinances. Camping is occupying a place to live outdoors for a stretch, and it is what stay limits, forest orders, and no camping rules regulate. Residing is using a place as your home address and dwelling, and no system in this guide allows it. Stay limits exist precisely to keep camping from becoming residing. A tiny home on wheels is treated as an RV under most of these rules, not as a house, which is why this rotation works at all, and also why it can never become a permanent address. This paragraph is general information, not legal advice.

## Part D: Could not verify, excluded on purpose

- **Free municipal or county RV parks in Little Rock, Conway, Morrilton, Dardanelle, or Hot Springs.** No official city or county page checked allows free overnight camping in a general city park. Conway's Cadron Settlement Park, for example, is a day use historic park with no camping stated. Burns Park in North Little Rock has a designated RV park and campground, and camping is not allowed elsewhere in the park, but a city fees document lists that RV park as closed after the March 31, 2023 tornado, and its current status and rates were not verified. Do not count on it without calling the city.
- **Wards Crossing Dispersed, Nimrod Lake area.** A free listing exists on The Dyrt and describes a large site on Corps land fitting small RVs, with a warning that the south approach crosses a bridge with a 3 ton weight limit. No official Corps page confirmed it, so it is excluded.
- **Camp Robinson dispersed site near Lake Conway and Mayflower.** A free listing exists on The Dyrt saying RVs up to 25 feet. AGFC code covers Camp Robinson Special Use Area camping permits in general, but no official page confirmed this specific spot as free or RV suitable, so it is excluded.
- **Hickory Nut Mountain campground, Ouachita National Forest.** It appears in the Forest Service 2025 fee schedule in a no charge entry with no water or electric, but no standalone Forest Service site page was opened in this research to confirm current status or rig fit, so it is not listed as a verified place.
- **Bear Creek Trail and Horse Camp, Ouachita National Forest.** The fee schedule lists it as no charge with no water or electric. It is a horse camp, and fit for a tiny home on wheels that is not part of a horse trailer setup was not verified.
- **Jack Creek Recreation Area, Ouachita National Forest.** The fee schedule lists its campground as no charge, but the Forest Service camping page lists Jack Creek as closed. Excluded until the Forest Service lists it open again.
- **Lake Dardanelle Corps parks, such as Shoal Bay, Piney Bay, and Spadra.** These are developed Corps campgrounds near Dardanelle, Russellville, and Clarksville. Older Corps material states a 14 day stay, but current per site prices and any free primitive section were not verified from a current official page in this research. Treat them as fee based.
- **Arkansas state park stay limits.** The camping fees above come from a published Arkansas fee schedule, but a stay limit was not verified on an official Arkansas State Parks page in this research. The guide does not state one as fact.
- **An official Arkansas rest area rule.** As covered in Part B, ARDOT publishes no clear statewide overnight rule. Any source claiming a definite Arkansas rest area time limit or a definite ban should be treated with suspicion.
- **An official Cracker Barrel overnight policy page.** None was found. The manager discretion rule in Part B rests on reputable coverage quoting company customer service, and it is labeled that way there.
- **Free dispersed camping in the Ozark St. Francis National Forest.** The forest north of the Arkansas River has dispersed camping under the same Arkansas forest guidance quoted for the Ouachita, but no specific site was verified from an official page in this research, so no Ozark St. Francis spot is named as a place.
